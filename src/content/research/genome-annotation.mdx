An assembly gives a genome’s sequence; an annotation says what that sequence encodes. Assemblies now arrive far faster than curated annotations such as [NCBI RefSeq (O’Leary et al., 2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkv1189) can follow, and the newest are complete or haplotype-resolved. Lift-over closes part of the gap by carrying a trusted annotation from a reference onto a new assembly.

The difficulty is that a transferred gene can land in the right place and still be wrong: a shifted splice site, a frameshift, a premature stop, a missing copy. I work on lift-over that checks what it transfers against the protein it is supposed to encode.

## Questions I’m pursuing

**How should DNA and protein evidence be combined?** [Liftoff (Shumate and Salzberg, 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa1016) maps whole gene models by DNA alignment and keeps their structure, but it degrades as sequences diverge. Protein aligners such as [miniprot (Li, 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btad014) reach farther yet emit coding exons only, with no UTRs or noncoding genes. The two fail in different places, which is what makes them worth combining.

**What counts as a correct gene model?** Mapping to the expected locus is not enough. I judge a transferred model by the protein it encodes, the integrity of its reading frame, and whether the copies present in the target genome are found.

**Does it hold at genome scale?** A method is useful only if it finishes whole genomes, including very large and repetitive genes, and produces valid, reproducible output. De novo pipelines such as [BRAKER3 (Gabriel et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.278090.123) serve genomes without a close reference; lift-over serves the many that have one.

## What I have built

### LiftOn

**LiftOn** ([Chao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.279620.124)) runs Liftoff and miniprot, pairs their models locus by locus, and aligns both translations to the reference protein. A protein-maximization chaining algorithm splits the two models where they cover the same span of the reference protein and keeps, for each segment, the version with higher identity. An open-reading-frame search repairs remaining frameshifts and premature stops, and a copy search adds gene copies that one-to-one transfer misses; noncoding features stay on the DNA path. Lifting RefSeq from GRCh38 to T2T-CHM13, LiftOn mapped 99.6% of genes, improved 866 protein-coding transcripts over Liftoff, and found extra copies of 86 genes at 320 new loci. The largest gains came in the more divergent pairs: from mouse to rat, it improved 15,420 transcripts over Liftoff.

### Annotating complete human genomes

Complete genomes test lift-over hardest. Annotating Han1 ([Chao et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/g3journal/jkac321)) was one of the threads that led me to LiftOn, and for CHESS 3 ([Varabyou et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-023-03088-4)) I lifted the catalog from GRCh38 to T2T-CHM13. For the complete diploid HG002 benchmark ([Hansen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.06.016)), my contribution was an extra-copy search: aligning MANE proteins to each haplotype with miniprot and filtering them with LiftOn’s copy logic recovered 51 gene copies (30 maternal, 21 paternal) that DNA-based lift-over had missed.

## Future directions

As complete genomes and pangenomes multiply, the interesting questions compare individuals: which genes one person carries and another lacks, and which copies differ. Lift-over will produce most of those annotations, so it has to make its errors visible. I want transfer methods that keep working as evolutionary distance grows, record the evidence behind each change, and let a reader tell real gene-content variation from an artifact of the transfer.
