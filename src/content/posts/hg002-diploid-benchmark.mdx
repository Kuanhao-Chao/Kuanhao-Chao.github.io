You have two genomes, not one. One came from your mother, one from your father, and at almost every position they say something slightly different. Human genetics has known this since before we could sequence anything, and yet nearly every tool we use flattens the two back into one: we map reads to a single reference, call the places where they disagree with it, and hand the result to a clinician as a list of variants.

That works until it doesn't. Reads from a duplicated or structurally polymorphic region often cannot be placed on the reference at all, so those regions produce no calls — not "no variants," but no information. And the benchmarks we use to grade variant callers were built with the same read-mapping methods, so they are blind in exactly the same places. A caller that fails inside a segmental duplication and a benchmark that cannot see inside a segmental duplication will agree with each other perfectly.

**The complete diploid HG002 benchmark** ([Hansen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.06.016)) is the way out of that circle. It is the Telomere-to-Telomere Consortium's Q100 project — 65 authors — and it delivers both haplotypes of a single well-studied person, finished, validated, and annotated. Once you can reconstruct someone's two chromosome sets end to end, you no longer need a reference to describe them against. I was one of the authors, working on the gene annotation, and this post covers the paper before going into detail on that.

## Why a genome benchmark and not another variant list

The Genome in a Bottle consortium made HG002 the most carefully characterized sample in human genomics, and its variant benchmarks ([Zook et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-019-0074-6)) have been the yardstick for a decade. But those benchmarks come with a confidence mask, and the mask is the point. GIAB v4.2.1 excluded both sex chromosomes outright and about 12% of the autosomes. Inside the mask you can measure a caller precisely; outside it you cannot measure at all, and "outside" is disproportionately the medically interesting, structurally complex part of the genome.

T2T-HG002 removes the mask. It is free of detectable errors across 99.4% of the complete diploid genome, and relative to the earlier benchmark it adds 701.4 Mb of autosomal sequence plus both sex chromosomes at 216.8 Mb — 15.3% of the genome that no previous benchmark assessed.

**The conceptual shift matters as much as the coverage.** Variant benchmarking scores you at sites; genome benchmarking scores you at bases. The paper makes the consequence concrete: on one published HG002 assembly, comparing against the complete genome found 66,544 errors, of which 50,843 could be lifted to GRCh38 at all and 30,505 fell inside the small-variant benchmark's high-confidence regions. More than half of the real errors sat where the older approach could not look.

## How the genome was finished

HG002 is a good choice partly for reasons that have nothing to do with sequencing. The cell line is available from Coriell as GM24385, NIST distributes it as Reference Material 8391, the donor consented through the Personal Genome Project to fully open data sharing, and both parents were sequenced too.

The assembly draws on about 170× PacBio HiFi coverage and 209× Oxford Nanopore ultra-long reads, with Illumina reads from both parents for trio phasing and Strand-seq and Hi-C for scaffolding the ribosomal DNA arrays. The assembler was [Verkko (Rautiainen et al., 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-023-01662-6), which builds a graph from HiFi and ONT reads together and resolves the two haplotypes with parental k-mers. Finishing it was a public, iterative process rather than a single run: v0.7 appeared in November 2022, v1.1 in July 2024, and in between, curation was crowdsourced through an open issue tracker, with proposed corrections validated by sampling before being applied.

**The remaining uncertainty is concentrated, not diffuse.** [Merqury (Rhie et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-020-02134-9) quality rose from Q63.1 to Q68.9, roughly one error per 8 million bases. What is left is 3,172 low-confidence regions covering 38.8 Mb, and 86.3% of that is ribosomal DNA — the tandem arrays on the acrocentric chromosomes, where hundreds of near-identical 45 kb units make any reconstruction hard to prove. The project's name, Q100, is an aspiration the authors are candid about not having reached; validating a genome to fewer than one error per 10 Gb is harder than achieving it.

## Annotating both haplotypes

A finished sequence is not yet a usable reference. Somebody has to say where the genes are — and for a diploid genome, has to say it twice.

That sounds like doing the same job over, but it is the opposite. The places where the two haplotypes agree are the easy, uninteresting 99%. The value of a diploid annotation lives in the fraction where they differ — where one parent contributed a gene the other deleted, or three copies where the other has one — and those differences sit overwhelmingly in segmental duplications and tandem arrays, the hardest regions to annotate at all.

### The pipeline

The reference is the T2T-CHM13v2.0 annotation ([Nurk et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abj6987)), itself derived from [RefSeq (O'Leary et al., 2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkv1189). The obstacle is that a complete genome actually contains the repeats a draft leaves as gaps: the ribosomal DNA and immunoglobulin arrays are present as long stretches of near-identical sequence, and an alignment-based transfer will happily scatter spurious copies across them.

The shape of the fix is one I had arrived at before. Building [Han1](/posts/han1/) ([Chao et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/g3journal/jkac321)) I hit the same wall, and the answer was a two-pass lift: mask the rDNA arrays, lift the backbone of the annotation onto the masked assembly, then lift the rDNA units separately and merge. The HG002 annotation is built on that structure.

**Hyun Joo Ji led the HG002 annotation**, and she extended that structure considerably. She masked the V(D)J segments as well as the rDNA — TRA, TRB, TRG, IGL, IGH and IGK — then ran [Liftoff (Shumate and Salzberg, 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa1016) on the masked haplotype with copy search enabled at 95% identity. A second pass carried the rDNA repeats back as whole units; merged, the two gave 59,525 genes on the maternal haplotype and 57,882 on the paternal. She then added two stages that had no precedent in Han1: a coding-sequence repair pass that trimmed 586 maternal and 534 paternal CDS features back to proper codon structure and extended 299 and 311 more to their next in-frame stop codon, and an identifier scheme that gives every gene and transcript a name unique to this assembly.

### The copies a DNA lift misses

**DNA-based lift-over undercounts gene copies, and a complete diploid genome is exactly where the missing ones are.** Liftoff does search for extra copies — that is what `-copies -sc 0.95` asks for — but a copy that has diverged, or that sits inside a segmental duplication where the alignment is ambiguous, can still be dropped. The genes this hits hardest are the ones that vary in copy number between people, which is the same property that made them hard in the first place.

Protein alignment fails differently: an orthologous protein stays recognizable across far more divergence than the DNA encoding it, because synonymous substitutions leave the amino acids alone. That is the premise behind [LiftOn](/posts/lifton/) ([Chao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.279620.124)), and for HG002 I used the part of it aimed squarely at this problem: its extra-copy search submodule.

**The search is deliberately blunt; the filter is where the work is.** The protein set was MANE v1.4 ([Morales et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04558-8)) — one representative transcript per protein-coding gene, agreed jointly by NCBI and EMBL-EBI. Those 19,354 proteins were aligned to the whole diploid assembly in a single [miniprot (Li, 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btad014) run, producing 41,386 alignments from 19,351 of them. Almost all of those land on genes we already have; the question is which land somewhere new. An alignment was discarded if it overlapped 10% or more of an existing gene, or if it spanned more than two adjacent gene loci — the first test finds unannotated space, the second throws out read-through alignments that smear across a gene family instead of marking one member of it.

Surviving hits still had to become usable annotation. miniprot emits coding features only, so each one was rebuilt into a full gene–transcript–exon hierarchy. Where the hit was an additional copy of a gene Liftoff had already placed, it kept that gene's identifier and was marked with an `extra_copy_number` attribute; otherwise it received a new one.

**That procedure added 30 genes to the maternal haplotype and 21 to the paternal**, and they are good models rather than marginal alignments: all but a single 48 bp fragment exceeded 92% identity to their MANE reference protein, the median was above 99%, and 18 matched exactly. They are still identifiable in the released annotation by their `miniprot` source column, and they carry a protein-coding biotype there. Hayden wrote a module into her pipeline specifically to ingest them, which re-checks each one's reading frame independently, renumbers it into the coordinate-sorted identifier scheme, and flags it as lacking UTRs.

They also show up in the paper's headline count, which is the cleanest way I know to check that a contribution actually landed. Table 1 reports 19,117 protein-coding genes on chromosomes 1–22 of the maternal haplotype. Counting the released annotation, Liftoff alone accounts for 19,088 of them; the remaining 29 are the autosomal copies from this pass.

**The list of genes is what convinced me the filter was doing real biology.** *CFHR3* and *CFHR1*, *GSTT1*, the trypsinogens *PRSS1* and *PRSS2*, mucins, *KIR2DL5A*, *LILRA3*, *PRAME* and a handful of olfactory receptors on the maternal side; *GSTM1*, *DMBT1*, *UGT2B28*, *SULT1A1* on the paternal. That is not a random sample of the genome — it is close to a list of the canonical human copy-number-variable loci. Several are ones the paper goes on to discuss: HG002 carries *GSTT1* only on the maternal haplotype and *GSTM1* only on the paternal, and the paternal haplotype carries the common *CFHR1*/*CFHR3* co-deletion associated with protection against age-related macular degeneration. Those copy-number calls were made downstream on the merged annotation, so they are not an independent confirmation — they are the reason the copies were worth recovering.

## What the annotation shows

**Both haplotypes carry a nearly complete gene complement, and the interesting numbers are the differences.** The paper counts 19,117 protein-coding genes on chromosomes 1–22 of the maternal haplotype and 19,084 on the paternal, plus 838 on ChrX and 105 on ChrY: 39,144 across the diploid genome. Counting everything, it reports 59,380 genes on the maternal haplotype and 57,728 on the paternal — figures that set aside the 175 ribosomal RNA genes annotated on each — and 60,074 distinct genes across the two, 2,755 more than T2T-CHM13 carries, because two haplotypes contain things one cannot.

Only 39 distinct genes present in CHM13 are absent from both HG002 haplotypes, and just three are protein-coding: *HLA-DRB5*, *CT45A8* and *OPN1MW*, all known to vary in copy number. Fourteen autosomal genes appear only on the maternal haplotype and twelve only on the paternal — numbers that survived a deliberate filter, since aligning each candidate against the opposite haplotype's proteins removed many apparent singletons that were really paralogs wearing a different name. The clearest picture in the paper is an insertion in the maternal Chr16 pericentromere carrying a *DUSP22* copy that exists on neither the paternal haplotype nor GRCh38.

**Where the annotation breaks, it breaks in duplications.** Calling a gene broken if its mapped MANE transcript has an invalid start or stop codon, a premature in-frame stop, or a protein less than 80% identical to the reference, 129 are broken on the maternal haplotype and 121 on the paternal. Those 250 are strongly enriched in segmental duplications: 28% intersect one, against 8% of the genes that map cleanly. Some are annotation artifacts and some are real, and separating the two is a problem you can only pose once you have the complete sequence.

## What the benchmark says about our methods

Having the answer lets you grade the methods. Across five HG002 assemblies spanning 2020 to 2024, substitution error rates fell by more than two orders of magnitude and indel rates by roughly ten-fold, with long homopolymers the stubborn remainder; the most recent covers 99.94% of the genome at QV 51. Merqury, scored against the benchmark, turns out to overstate HiFi-only assemblies by 7.9 to 11.7 QV, because the k-mer database used to grade them is itself missing the regions only ONT recovers.

**And assembling beats calling.** Reconstructing HG002 by applying variant calls back onto GRCh38 recovers 93.8–92.9% of the real genome at QV 37–40, and onto CHM13 97.8–95.7% at QV 35–39. Assembling it de novo covers 99.94% at QV 51 — about one error per 100 kb, an order of magnitude better, over more of the genome.

## The bigger picture

Annotation is where a benchmark stops being a sequence and becomes a person. Six billion validated bases is a remarkable technical object, but the reason anyone cares is that it can be read as a statement about someone's biology, and that reading requires knowing which stretches are genes and which copy each parent contributed. A diploid annotation keeps that distinction; a haploid one averages it away, and the averaging falls hardest on exactly the copy-number-variable loci that reference-based pipelines already handle worst.

The limits are worth stating alongside the achievement, and the paper states them: HG002 is a cultured cell line, so some somatic variation is expected; the rDNA arrays remain most of what is still uncertain; and a region no technology reads well is a region you cannot easily prove you got right. One complete diploid genome is also still one person.

What the project changes is the target. For twenty years the goal of human resequencing has been a good list of differences from a reference; this says the goal should be the genome itself, and that we are close enough to reach it routinely. That is the thread running through [Han1](/posts/han1/) and [LiftOn](/posts/lifton/) and into this — carrying a trusted gene map onto a more complete genome, and keeping the differences instead of smoothing them out. The copies that exist on only one haplotype are not noise in the annotation. They are the point of it.
