## The question this lesson answers

A grant is funded, a cohort is available, and someone asks how many samples to collect.

Everything downstream in this track — genotyping, imputation, quality control, the scan
itself — is a matter of executing well. This lesson is about the decisions taken before any
of it, because they set a ceiling that no amount of later care raises. A study that
ascertained the wrong cases, or bought controls it did not need, cannot be repaired by better
software.

The case for doing association studies at all was made on exactly these grounds
<Citation id="risch1996future" />: linkage analysis in families has excellent resolution for
a rare variant of large effect and almost none for a common variant of small effect, which is
what complex traits are mostly made of. Association in unrelated people inverts that trade.
Two decades of results <Citation id="visscher2017gwas" /> confirmed the premise and also its
price — the effects are small, so the samples must be enormous.

<Callout variant="intuition">

The single most useful fact about a case-control study is that its power does not run on the
number of people in it. It runs on a **harmonic mean** of the two arms, which is dominated by
the smaller one. Doubling a group that is already the larger of the two moves almost nothing;
adding to the smaller group moves everything. Most of what follows is that sentence with
numbers attached.

</Callout>

## The decisions you cannot undo

**Case definition** fixes what the study is measuring. A broad definition recruits faster and
dilutes the signal; a narrow one is slower and sharper. There is no analysis that recovers a
distinction the phenotype never recorded.

**Ascertainment** fixes the relationship between the study and the population. A study that
enrols cases at fifty times their population rate is not a random sample, and any variance
explained it reports is on the study's own scale, not a scale anyone else can use. The
conversion is mechanical <Citation id="lee2011estimating" /> but it needs the population
prevalence, which is a fact about the world rather than about the data.

**The case-to-control ratio** fixes the study's effective size, which is the subject of the
worked example below.

**Which covariates get measured** fixes what can be adjusted for. Age, sex, batch, and
recruitment site are cheap at collection and unrecoverable later. Ancestry is the exception:
it can be estimated from the genotypes themselves <Citation id="price2006eigenstrat" />, which
is the subject of [Population Structure](/deep_dives/gwas-population-structure/), in Module 2.

## Where the next ten thousand samples go

<WorkedExample
  title="A study with 10,000 cases and 40,000 controls, and a budget for 10,000 more people"
  given={[
    { label: 'Cases', value: '10,000' },
    { label: 'Controls', value: '40,000' },
    { label: 'Population prevalence', value: 'K = 0.01' },
    { label: 'Budget', value: '10,000 more samples, of either kind' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — what the study has now.** Fifty thousand people, but the power calculation does not
see fifty thousand:

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{1/N_{\mathrm{cases}} + 1/N_{\mathrm{controls}}}
= \frac{4}{1/10{,}000 + 1/40{,}000} = 32{,}000 .
$$

Note what a balanced study of the same size would have been worth: 25,000 and 25,000 give
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 50{,}000$, the headcount exactly. The 4:1 split costs 18,000 effective
samples, and no analysis recovers them.

**Step 2 — the ceiling on controls.** Hold the cases fixed and let the controls run away:

$$
\lim_{N_{\mathrm{controls}} \to \infty} N_{\mathrm{eff}}
= 4 N_{\mathrm{cases}} = 40{,}000 .
$$

Every control ever collected, for free, forever, is worth 40,000 effective samples in total.
At 4:1 the study already holds $32{,}000/40{,}000 = 80\%$ of that. This is the fact that
decides the budget.

**Step 3 — the two options, priced.**

$$
\text{+10,000 controls:} \quad N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{1/10{,}000 + 1/50{,}000}
= 33{,}333.33
$$

$$
\text{+10,000 cases:} \quad N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{1/20{,}000 + 1/40{,}000}
= 53{,}333.33
$$

The gains are 1,333.33 and 21,333.33 effective samples. **The same ten thousand people are
worth sixteen times more as cases than as controls** — exactly sixteen, not approximately.
Controls climb toward a ceiling; cases raise it.

**Step 4 — what that buys in detection.** Power for a marginal test is set by $q^2$, the
variance one variant explains, and the sample size needed for 80% power at
$5 \times 10^{-8}$ is $N \geq 39.60/q^2$ — a result derived in
[Association Testing & Linear Mixed Models](/deep_dives/statgen-association-linear-mixed-models/)
and not repeated here. Inverted, it says what a given study can see:

$$
q^2 \geq \frac{39.60}{N_{\mathrm{eff}}} .
$$

At 32,000 effective samples the study can detect $q^2 = 1.2375 \times 10^{-3}$; spending on
cases takes it to $7.4252 \times 10^{-4}$. At an effect-allele frequency of 0.30, and using
$q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$, those are per-allele effects of $\beta = 0.054282$ and
$\beta = 0.042046$. Spending on controls instead reaches only $0.053185$ — a 2% improvement
against 23%.

**Step 5 — put it on a scale someone else can use.** The $q^2$ above is on the study's own
0/1 scale, where the case fraction is $P = 0.2$ rather than the population's $K = 0.01$. The
liability-scale conversion <Citation id="lee2011estimating" /> gives

$$
q^2_{\text{liability}} = 1.0672 \times 10^{-3}
$$

for the current study, and $4.6103 \times 10^{-4}$ for the case-heavy one at $P = 1/3$.
Report the observed-scale number and no one can compare it with anything, including your own
next study at a different ratio.

</WorkedExample>

## What no sample size fixes

Three limits are structural, and a reader who expects a GWAS to overcome them will misread
every result it produces.

**Variants the array cannot see.** A genotyping array types a fixed set of common sites and
imputes the rest from a reference panel. Variants too rare to be on the panel are invisible
regardless of N, which is why rare-variant work uses sequencing and aggregates across a gene
rather than testing sites one at a time — the subject of
[Rare Variant Association](/deep_dives/statgen-rare-variant-association/). Structural
variants, repeat expansions and anything in a region the array does not tile are simply
absent from the results.

**The gap between a signal and a gene.** An association marks a haplotype, not a base. The
lead variant is usually not causal, most signals are non-coding, and the nearest gene is
frequently the wrong one. Resolving that is a separate discipline, taken up in
[Fine-mapping in Practice](/deep_dives/gwas-fine-mapping-practice/), in Module 3.

**The causal direction.** An association between a variant and a trait is not an association
between an exposure and an outcome. Genotype is randomised at conception, which is a genuine
advantage, but converting it into a causal claim requires an instrumental-variable argument
with assumptions of its own.

There is also a limit that is not a defect but is routinely read as one. As samples grow,
the number of associated loci grows with them, and the effects get smaller rather than
converging on a short list <Citation id="yang2010common" />. That was first read as
"missing heritability" <Citation id="manolio2009" /> and is now better understood as the
architecture itself: for a typical complex trait, a very large fraction of the genome
contributes something <Citation id="boyle2017omnigenic" />. A GWAS that returns 500 loci
explaining a few percent each has not failed. It has measured a polygenic trait correctly.

<Callout variant="key" title="Before the first sample is collected">

Write down the population prevalence, the case definition, the target effect size, and the
case-to-control ratio you can actually achieve. Those four numbers determine what the study
can detect, and three of them cannot be changed later. If the arithmetic says the design
cannot reach the effects you care about, that is worth knowing before the genotyping, not
after. The <Citation id="sollis2023gwascatalog" /> record of what has already been found at
what sample size is the cheapest available calibration.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A very unbalanced study" difficulty="warm-up">

A study has 2,000 cases and 100,000 controls.

(a) Compute $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$.

(b) What fraction of the control ceiling has it reached?

(c) The consortium offers either 100,000 more controls or 500 more cases. Which is worth
more?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4/(1/2{,}000 + 1/100{,}000) = 4/(5.1 \times 10^{-4}) = 7{,}843.14$.

**(b)** The ceiling is $4 \times 2{,}000 = 8{,}000$, so the study is at
$7{,}843.14/8{,}000 = 98.04\%$ of it. With 102,000 people collected, 156.86 effective samples
remain available from controls — in total, forever.

**(c)** 500 more cases. Doubling the controls to 200,000 gives
$4/(1/2{,}000 + 1/200{,}000) = 7{,}920.79$, a gain of 77.65. Adding 500 cases gives
$4/(1/2{,}500 + 1/100{,}000) = 9{,}756.10$, a gain of 1,912.96 — **24.6 times more**, from
one two-hundredth as many people. A study this unbalanced should stop collecting controls
entirely.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Design to a target" difficulty="core">

You want to detect a variant with $q^2 = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ at 80% power and
$5 \times 10^{-8}$.

(a) What $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ do you need?

(b) If you can recruit at most 15,000 cases, how many controls does that require — and is it
achievable?

(c) What is the smallest number of cases for which the target is reachable at all, however
many controls you collect?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 39.60/q^2$. The constant is $39.600989$ before rounding, so
$39.600989/(5 \times 10^{-4}) = 79{,}201.98$ and the study needs 79,202. (Dividing the
rounded $39.60$ gives 79,200; at this precision it makes no practical difference, but the
two numbers are not the same and only one of them is the answer.)

**(b)** Solve $4/(1/15{,}000 + 1/x) = 79{,}202$ for $x$. The control ceiling at 15,000 cases
is 60,000 — *below* the 79,202 required. **No number of controls achieves it.** The equation
returns a negative $x$, which is the algebra reporting the same thing.

**(c)** The target is reachable only if $4N_{\mathrm{cases}} > 79{,}202$, so
$N_{\mathrm{cases}} > 19{,}800.49$: at least 19,801 cases, and that only with infinitely many
controls. A practical 4:1 design needs $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.2 N_{\mathrm{cases}} \geq 79{,}202$,
so 24,751 cases and 99,004 controls.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Two studies that disagree" difficulty="stretch">

Study A reports that a variant explains 0.12% of the variance in its data; it has 5,000 cases
and 5,000 controls. Study B reports 0.04% for the same variant; it has 5,000 cases and 45,000
controls. The disease has a population prevalence of 2%.

(a) Convert both to the liability scale.

(b) Do the two studies agree?

(c) What would you need from the authors to be sure?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Study A has $P = 0.5$; study B has $P = 0.1$. With $K = 0.02$, the Lee conversion
gives $7.8657 \times 10^{-4}$ for A and $7.2831 \times 10^{-4}$ for B.

**(b)** **Yes — they agree to within 8%.** On the observed scale B looked like a *third* of A,
a difference that would read as a failed replication. Almost all of that gap was the
ascertainment: A is balanced at $P = 0.5$ while B is at $P = 0.1$, and the observed-scale
variance explained depends on the case fraction. Removing it leaves a discrepancy of
$7.8657/7.2831 = 1.08$, which is well inside what two studies of this size would differ by
from sampling alone.

This is the whole reason the conversion exists, and it is worth noticing which direction it
went: the raw numbers made two agreeing studies look like a contradiction, not the reverse.

**(c)** The population prevalence each study assumed — the conversion is only as good as $K$,
and a study that used 2% where the truth is 5% will land somewhere else entirely. After that,
the effect-allele frequency and the ancestry of each cohort, since a variant can tag a
different causal site in a population with different LD. But on the evidence here the studies
do not need reconciling; the observed-scale reporting did.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Power runs on $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, not headcount**, and $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is a harmonic
  mean dominated by the smaller arm. A 4:1 study of 50,000 people is worth 32,000; balanced,
  it would be worth all 50,000.
- **Controls saturate at $4N_{\mathrm{cases}}$.** A 4:1 design already holds 80% of
  everything controls can contribute, so past that point the next sample should be a case.
  In the worked example the same 10,000 people were worth exactly sixteen times more as
  cases.
- **An effective sample size is a detection limit**: $q^2 \geq 39.60/N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, which
  converts directly into the smallest per-allele effect the study can find.
- **An ascertained study's variance explained is on its own scale** until it is put on the
  liability scale, which needs the population prevalence — a fact about the world, not about
  the data.
- **Some limits are structural.** Rare and structural variants, the distance from a signal to
  a gene, and the direction of causation are not sample-size problems, and a larger study
  does not approach them.
