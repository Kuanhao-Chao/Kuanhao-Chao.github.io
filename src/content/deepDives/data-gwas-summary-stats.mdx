## The question this page answers

Almost nothing in modern genetics runs on individual-level genotypes. Meta-analysis,
fine-mapping, colocalisation, polygenic scores and Mendelian randomisation all consume
*summary statistics*: one row per variant, carrying an effect size, a standard error and the
allele the effect refers to.

That last field is where the trouble lives.

<Callout variant="intuition">
An effect size is meaningless without knowing which allele it belongs to. "This variant
raises LDL by 0.04" is only a statement once you know whether the raising allele is the A or
the G.

Combining two studies therefore means aligning them: agreeing on a reference genome, agreeing
which allele is the effect allele, and flipping the sign wherever they disagree. Almost all
of that is mechanical.

The exception is variants whose two alleles are complements of each other — A/T and C/G. For
those, reading the alleles cannot tell you which strand a study used, because the reverse
complement of an A/T variant is a T/A variant, which is the same pair written backwards. Get
it wrong and you do not get an error; you get a sign flip, on a file that looks perfectly
normal.
</Callout>

## The repositories, and what each is for

**<Dataset id="gwas-catalog" />** is two things that get conflated
<Citation id="sollis2023gwascatalog" />. The curated table of published associations is what
most people mean by "the Catalog", and it holds top hits with their reported effects. The
deposition service holds *full* summary statistics, and that is what any reanalysis needs —
a fine-mapping or a colocalisation cannot run on lead SNPs. Harmonised files are provided on
a common build and strand, which removes most of the work this page is about.

**<Dataset id="finngen" />** is a bottlenecked founder population
<Citation id="kurki2023finngen" />, so variants too rare to test elsewhere reach testable
frequency there. Its phenotypes come from national health registers rather than research
diagnoses, which is a genuine difference in what an endpoint means rather than a caveat about
quality.

**<Dataset id="opentargets-l2g" />** runs harmonisation, fine-mapping
<Citation id="wu2026finemapping" /> and colocalisation as a pipeline
<Citation id="mountjoy2021l2g" />, which is most of what a careful reanalysis would do and
frequently does worse.

<Callout variant="key" title="What a file has to carry to be reusable">
A summary-statistic file is reusable only if it states, unambiguously:

- the **genome build**, and ideally the strand convention;
- **both alleles**, with which one is the effect allele marked;
- the **effect allele frequency**, without which palindromic variants cannot be aligned;
- $\beta$ and its **standard error**, not only a p-value — a p-value has no sign and cannot be
  meta-analysed by inverse variance;
- the **sample size**, per variant where it varies, since imputation quality and missingness
  make it vary.

Files missing the effect allele frequency are the common case and the expensive one: every
A/T and C/G variant in them is unusable in any analysis that combines studies.
</Callout>

## What one misaligned variant costs

<WorkedExample
  title="A palindromic SNP flipped between two studies"
  given={[
    { label: 'Study 1', value: 'A/T variant, effect allele A, EAF 0.18, β = +0.043, SE 0.008' },
    { label: 'Study 2', value: 'same variant, effect allele A, EAF 0.82, β = −0.041, SE 0.009' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — notice the frequencies.** Both studies name the effect allele "A", and their
frequencies are $0.18$ and $0.82$, which sum to one. For a palindromic variant that is the
signature of opposite strands: study 2's "A" is the reverse complement of study 1's "A",
which is study 1's T.

**Step 2 — meta-analyse without noticing.** Taking the files at face value and combining by
inverse variance:

$$
\hat\beta = 0.005924, \qquad \text{SE} = 0.005979, \qquad z = 0.9908, \qquad p = 0.3218
$$

The two effects nearly cancel. The result is null and looks like an honest null.

**Step 3 — align first, then meta-analyse.** Flipping study 2 to study 1's strand turns its
$\beta$ from $-0.041$ to $+0.041$:

$$
\hat\beta = 0.042117, \qquad \text{SE} = 0.005979, \qquad z = 7.0439, \qquad p = 1.882\times10^{-12}
$$

**Step 4 — compare.** The same two studies, the same numbers, and a difference between
$p = 0.32$ and $p = 1.9\times10^{-12}$ — the difference between "no association" and a
genome-wide significant one, decided entirely by which strand a file assumed.

**Step 5 — note what saved it.** The allele frequencies. Had both been near $0.5$ — say $0.48$
and $0.52$ — the ambiguity would be unresolvable from the file alone, which is why careful
pipelines *drop* palindromic variants with intermediate frequency rather than guess.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="λ_GC above 1 is not evidence of confounding">
Genomic control compares the median test statistic to what a null distribution would give
<Citation id="devlin1999gc" />:

$$
\lambda_{GC} = \frac{\text{median}(\chi^2)}{0.454936\ldots}
$$

with the denominator the exact median of $\chi^2_1$. It was introduced when inflation meant
population stratification, and dividing every statistic by $\lambda_{GC}$ was a reasonable
correction.

For a polygenic trait it is not. If thousands of variants carry small true effects, the median
statistic *should* exceed the null — the inflation is signal. A large modern GWAS routinely
reports $\lambda_{GC}$ well above 1 with no confounding at all, and correcting by it discards
real association <Citation id="oconnor2021effects" />. Separating the two needs LD score
regression, whose intercept isolates the confounding part while the slope absorbs polygenicity.
</Callout>

## Why published effect sizes are too large

A variant enters the literature by clearing a threshold. That selection is not neutral: among
variants whose true effect sits near the threshold, the ones that get discovered are
disproportionately those whose noise happened to point the right way.

<WorkedExample
  title="The winner's curse at the discovery threshold"
  given={[
    { label: 'Threshold', value: 'p < 5×10⁻⁸, i.e. |z| > 5.4513' },
    { label: 'Question', value: 'given discovery, how inflated is the published z?' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — a variant barely above the threshold.** For a true effect of $z = 5.45$, the
expected published estimate conditional on being discovered is

$$
\mathbb{E}[z_{\text{obs}} \mid \text{discovered}] = 6.2487
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{inflation } 1.1466\times
$$

Nearly 15% too large, on average, before anyone has made a mistake.

**Step 2 — a moderately strong variant.** At a true $z = 6.50$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[z_{\text{obs}}] = 6.7699 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1.0415\times
$$

**Step 3 — a strong one.** At a true $z = 8.00$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[z_{\text{obs}}] = 8.0156 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1.0019\times
$$

Essentially nothing: a variant this strong would have been found whatever the noise did, so
conditioning on discovery selects almost nothing.

**Step 4 — read the pattern.** The bias is **worst exactly at the threshold** and disappears
for effects comfortably above it. Since most discoveries in a well-powered GWAS sit close to
the threshold — that is where the mass of the effect-size distribution is — the typical
published effect is inflated, and the typical replication estimate is smaller
<Citation id="palmer2017winners" />.

**Step 5 — what follows.** A polygenic score trained on discovery effect sizes inherits the
inflation, which is one reason its out-of-sample $R^2$ underperforms the value predicted from
the discovery statistics. Shrinkage methods correct for this implicitly; taking published
betas at face value does not.

</WorkedExample>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Which variants can be aligned" difficulty="warm-up">

Which of these can be aligned between two studies using the alleles alone, and which need the
effect allele frequency? (a) A/G, (b) C/G, (c) A/T, (d) T/C.

<div slot="solution">

**(a) A/G and (d) T/C align from the alleles alone.** Their reverse complements are T/C and
A/G respectively — a *different* pair of letters — so seeing which pair a study reported
determines the strand.

**(b) C/G and (c) A/T cannot.** Each is its own reverse complement, so both strands produce
the same pair and the file cannot say which was meant. These are the palindromic, or
strand-ambiguous, variants and they need the effect allele frequency to resolve.

Roughly a sixth of common SNPs are palindromic, so this is not a rare corner: any pipeline
that ignores it mis-signs a substantial fraction of the genome, and the mis-signed variants
are scattered rather than clustered, which makes the damage hard to spot.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="When frequency cannot save you either" difficulty="core">

Two studies report the same A/T variant with effect allele A at frequencies 0.48 and 0.52.
Can the strand be determined, and what should be done?

<div slot="solution">

**No.** The frequencies are consistent with the same strand — where 0.48 and 0.52 are ordinary
sampling variation around a common frequency — and equally consistent with opposite strands,
where 0.52 is the complement of 0.48. Nothing in the file distinguishes them.

The correct action is to **drop the variant**, not to guess. The alternatives are both worse: a
coin-flip alignment is wrong half the time, and defaulting to "same strand" is wrong whenever
the studies used different conventions, which is exactly the situation that makes the check
necessary.

Standard pipelines therefore exclude palindromic variants with an effect allele frequency
inside a window around 0.5, commonly 0.4 to 0.6. The cost is a small, *unbiased* loss of
variants. The cost of guessing is a small, *sign-flipped* set of variants, which is far more
damaging: an unbiased loss weakens a meta-analysis, whereas sign flips actively cancel real
signal, as the worked example showed.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Reading a replication that came back smaller" difficulty="stretch">

A discovery study reports a variant at $z = 6.2$. Replication in an independent cohort of the
same size returns $z = 5.4$. Is this a failure to replicate?

<div slot="solution">

**No — it is what the winner's curse predicts.**

The discovery estimate was selected for being large. Inverting the relation above, a published
$z$ of about 6.2 is what a true $z$ of roughly 5.45 produces on average once conditioned on
discovery, because that true effect is inflated by a factor of 1.1466. The replication is
unconditioned — it did not have to clear anything to be reported — so it estimates the true
effect without that bias, and 5.4 is almost exactly what the true value should be.

Two things follow:

- **Replication should be judged against the de-biased estimate**, not the published one.
  Expecting the original figure is expecting the bias to recur, which it will not in an
  unselected sample.
- **A replication returning the *same* effect size is mildly surprising**, and at a threshold-
  adjacent discovery it is weak evidence that something correlated is going on — shared
  samples, shared population structure, or a shared covariate — rather than reassurance.

The general habit: at a discovery near the threshold, treat the effect size as an upper bound
and the direction as the finding.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Summary statistics are the substrate of everything downstream**, and their errors are
  silent: a wrong effect allele produces a valid file, not an error.
- **A reusable file states build, both alleles, which is the effect allele, the effect allele
  frequency, β with its standard error, and per-variant sample size.** A p-value alone has no
  sign and cannot be meta-analysed.
- **Palindromic A/T and C/G variants are strand-ambiguous.** Frequency resolves them unless it
  is near 0.5, in which case the right move is to drop them — an unbiased loss beats a
  sign-flipped inclusion.
- **One misaligned variant cost the whole result.** The same two studies gave
  $p = 1.882\times10^{-12}$ aligned and $p = 0.3218$ flipped.
- **λ_GC above 1 is not evidence of confounding** for a polygenic trait; the inflation is
  largely signal, and correcting by it discards real association.
- **Discovery inflates effect sizes, worst at the threshold.** A true $z$ of 5.45 publishes at
  1.1466× on average, 6.50 at 1.0415× and 8.00 at 1.0019× — so a replication that comes back
  smaller is usually the unbiased estimate rather than a failure.
