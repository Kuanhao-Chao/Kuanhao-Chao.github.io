## The question this lesson answers

Three of the things a GWAS does after the scan — clumping the results, estimating
heritability from them, fine-mapping a locus — take an LD reference panel as an argument.
Which one, and does it matter?

It matters more than almost any other file path in the pipeline, and the reason is a single
property of $r^2$ that is easy to state and easy to forget.

<Callout variant="intuition">

**$r^2$ is not a property of two variants.** It is a property of two variants *in a
population*. The same pair of positions, at the same allele frequencies, can be almost
perfectly correlated in one group of people and nearly independent in another, because
correlation is about which combinations actually occur on the chromosomes those people
carry — and that is a matter of their shared history, not of the variants.

So an LD reference panel is not a lookup table for a fact about the genome. It is a claim
about which population your samples resemble.

</Callout>

## The same pair in two populations

<WorkedExample
  title="Identical allele frequencies, an 8.16-fold difference in what the study needs"
  given={[
    { label: 'Population A haplotypes', value: 'AB 0.45, Ab 0.05, aB 0.05, ab 0.45' },
    { label: 'Population B haplotypes', value: 'AB 0.32, Ab 0.18, aB 0.18, ab 0.32' },
    { label: 'Causal variance explained', value: 'q² = 1.0 × 10⁻³' },
    { label: 'Threshold', value: '80% power at 5 × 10⁻⁸' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the allele frequencies are the same.** In both populations,

$$
p_A = p_{AB} + p_{Ab} = 0.50, \qquad p_B = p_{AB} + p_{aB} = 0.50 .
$$

Nothing that a frequency-based filter, an imputation quality metric or a minor-allele
threshold can see differs between them.

**Step 2 — the haplotypes are not.** Disequilibrium is the excess of a haplotype over what
independence would give <Citation id="hill1968" />:

$$
D = p_{AB} - p_A p_B .
$$

Population A gives $D = 0.45 - 0.25 = 0.2000$; population B gives
$D = 0.32 - 0.25 = 0.0700$.

**Step 3 — normalise twice, two different ways.** $D'$ divides by the largest $D$ the allele
frequencies permit, which here is $0.25$:

$$
D'_A = 0.8000, \qquad D'_B = 0.2800 .
$$

$r^2$ divides by the product of all four allele frequencies:

$$
r^2 = \frac{D^2}{p_A p_a p_B p_b}, \qquad r^2_A = 0.640000, \qquad r^2_B = 0.078400 .
$$

Because both loci sit at exactly $0.50$ here, the two normalisations coincide:
$r^2 = D'^2$, which is why $0.8000^2 = 0.640000$ and $0.2800^2 = 0.078400$. That identity
holds only at equal frequencies, and is a useful check when reading a figure like this one.

**Step 4 — which one does tagging depend on?** $r^2$, and only $r^2$. A causal variant
explaining $q^2$ of the trait shows up at a tag as

$$
q^2_{\text{tag}} = r^2 \, q^2_{\text{causal}} ,
$$

so with $q^2_{\text{causal}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ the tag carries $6.4 \times 10^{-4}$ in
population A and $7.84 \times 10^{-5}$ in population B.

**Step 5 — in effective samples.** Using $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 39.60/q^2$ from
[Study Design](/deep_dives/gwas-study-design/), the study needs

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff},A} = 61{,}877 \qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{\mathrm{eff},B} = 505{,}115 .
$$

These are effective samples, the unit the rest of the track counts in — at a 4:1 case-control
ratio they are 96,683 and 789,242 people.

The ratio is $8.1633$, exactly the ratio of the two $r^2$ values, because the sample size is
inversely proportional to the variance the tag carries. Choosing the wrong reference panel
does not make a study slightly less efficient. It can make it eight times too small.

</WorkedExample>

## Pruning and clumping are different jobs

These two are constantly confused, partly because the same tool performs both
<Citation id="purcell2007plink" />, and choosing the wrong one silently changes the answer.

**Pruning** walks the genome and removes variants correlated above a threshold with a
retained neighbour. It knows nothing about the phenotype. What survives is a set that is
roughly independent and *unbiased with respect to signal* — which is exactly what an ancestry
principal-components calculation and a genetic relationship matrix need, and why the previous
module insisted on pruning before computing either.

**Clumping** keeps the most significant variant in each LD neighbourhood and discards the
rest. It is phenotype-aware by construction. What survives is a set of independent *signals*,
which is what a polygenic score wants — one weight per locus rather than fifty correlated
copies of the same one.

Using them the wrong way round produces two distinct failures. Clumping before a PC
calculation enriches the variant set for associated regions, so the components partly
describe the phenotype rather than the ancestry. Pruning before a score keeps an arbitrary
member of each block rather than the best one, discarding signal for no reason.

## Where a mismatched panel does its damage

**Clumping** decides which variants are "the same signal" using the panel's $r^2$. With a
panel from the wrong population, a genuinely independent second signal in the locus gets
absorbed into the first, or one signal fragments into several — and both errors propagate
into whatever consumes the clumped list.

**LD score regression** regresses $\chi^2$ on how much of the genome each variant tags
<Citation id="bulik2015ldsc" />, and those LD scores come entirely from the panel. Because
the slope is $Nh^2/M$, a systematically wrong LD score gives a systematically wrong
heritability, with no diagnostic in the output to reveal it. This is why LDSC's documentation
is emphatic about ancestry matching.

The intercept is not the safe harbour it is often taken for, either. It survives a *purely
multiplicative* discrepancy — scale every LD score by 1.2 and the slope moves while the
intercept stays exactly where it was — but a real panel mismatch is not a clean rescaling.
It errs variant by variant, which is textbook errors-in-variables: the slope attenuates and
the part of the signal it loses reappears in the intercept. Fitting the same clean statistics
against LD scores carrying variant-level noise moves an intercept of 1.02 to 1.70, which a
reader would diagnose as heavy confounding in a scan that has none. So a clean intercept is
not evidence the LD scores are right, and an inflated one is not proof of confounding.

**Fine-mapping** is the most sensitive of the three, because it is trying to distinguish
variants that are nearly indistinguishable. It is the subject of the next lesson, which uses
in-sample LD rather than a panel for precisely this reason.

<Callout variant="warning" title="There is no universal panel">

Reference panels <Citation id="hapmap2005" /><Citation id="tgp2015" /> are collections of
particular people, and haplotype structure varies continuously rather than falling into
labelled boxes <Citation id="gabriel2002" /><Citation id="pritchard2001" />. A cohort that is
admixed, or that comes from a population under-represented in every available panel, has no
correct choice — only a least-wrong one, and an obligation to say which was used.

This is a concrete part of why results transfer poorly across populations
<Citation id="martin2019" />: not only are the effect sizes estimated in one group, the LD
structure used to interpret them is too. Both have to travel for the result to.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Two loci at unequal frequencies" difficulty="warm-up">

Haplotype frequencies are $p_{AB} = 0.30$, $p_{Ab} = 0.10$, $p_{aB} = 0.10$, $p_{ab} = 0.50$.

(a) Compute $p_A$, $p_B$, $D$ and $r^2$.

(b) Does $r^2 = D'^2$ here?

(c) A causal variant explains $q^2 = 8 \times 10^{-4}$. What does a study see at this tag?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $p_A = 0.40$, $p_B = 0.40$, $D = 0.30 - 0.16 = 0.1400$, and
$r^2 = 0.14^2/(0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.6) = 0.0196/0.0576 = 0.340278$.

**(b)** No. $D_{\max} = \min(p_A p_b,\, p_a p_B) = \min(0.24, 0.24) = 0.24$, so
$D' = 0.583333$ and $D'^2 = 0.340278$ — which does equal $r^2$ here, because the two loci
still share the *same* allele frequency as each other (both 0.40), even though neither is
0.50. The identity needs the two loci to share a frequency *and* $D$ to be positive: with $D > 0$
and $p_A = p_B = p$, $D_{\max} = p(1-p) = \sqrt{p_A p_a p_B p_b}$ and the two normalisations
coincide. With $D < 0$ the maximum becomes $\min(p_A p_B,\, p_a p_b)$, which equals
$p(1-p)$ only at $p = 0.5$ — so at $p_A = p_B = 0.40$ and $D = -0.10$ the identity fails
outright, giving $D'^2 = 0.390625$ against $r^2 = 0.173611$. The worked example sits at
$p_A = p_B = 0.5$, which satisfies it for either sign.

**(c)** $q^2_{\text{tag}} = 0.340278 \times 8 \times 10^{-4} = 2.72222 \times 10^{-4}$, so
the study needs $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 39.600989/(2.72222 \times 10^{-4}) = 145{,}474$.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Which tool for which job?" difficulty="core">

For each task, say whether to prune or to clump, and what goes wrong with the other choice.

(a) Computing ancestry principal components.

(b) Selecting variants for a polygenic score.

(c) Building a genetic relationship matrix for a mixed model.

(d) Counting how many independent signals a locus contains.

<div slot="solution">

**(a) Prune.** Clumping selects for association with the phenotype, so the leading components
would partly describe the trait rather than ancestry — and adjusting a scan for components
built from its own signal removes the signal.

**(b) Clump.** Pruning keeps an arbitrary variant per block; clumping keeps the one carrying
the evidence, which is the one whose weight you want.

**(c) Prune.** Same reason as (a): a relationship matrix should describe how related people
are, not how similar their phenotypes are.

**(d) Clump**, but carefully — and this is the case where neither tool is really right.
Clumping counts signals by an $r^2$ rule, which cannot distinguish two causal variants in
modest LD from one causal variant tagged twice. That question needs the fine-mapping of the
next lesson, and treating a clump count as a signal count is a common way to over-report
independent associations.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A heritability that will not replicate" difficulty="stretch">

A group estimates $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$ by LD score regression on a South Asian cohort, using
LD scores computed from a European panel because that is what the software ships with. They
report $h^2 = 0.31$ with a clean intercept of 1.01.

(a) Is the intercept reassuring?

(b) In which direction is the heritability likely wrong, and why can you not simply correct
it?

(c) What should they do?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** No, and not for the reason usually given. The intercept is insulated from a purely
multiplicative error in the LD scores — rescale them all and it does not move — so a clean
intercept is consistent with a badly scaled panel, and looks like a passing diagnostic while
$h^2$ is wrong. But a mismatched panel also errs *variant by variant*, and that pushes the
intercept **up**: the same clean statistics fitted against noisy LD scores gave 1.70 rather
than 1.02 in the worked figures above. So an intercept of 1.01 here says the panel's error is
close to multiplicative, not that the panel is right — and had it come back at 1.4 the team
would have been equally wrong to read it as confounding.

**(b)** The slope is $Nh^2/M$, so $h^2$ is misestimated by whatever factor the LD scores are
wrong by, and the direction depends on whether European LD scores are systematically higher
or lower than South Asian ones for the variants analysed. It cannot be corrected by a scalar
because the error is not a constant factor: it varies variant by variant with how the two
populations' haplotype structures differ, which is the same fact the worked example
demonstrated at a single pair.

**(c)** Compute LD scores from a panel matched to the cohort — a South Asian subset of a
large reference resource <Citation id="tgp2015" />, or better, in-sample from the cohort's own
genotypes if it is large enough. Then report which panel was used, because a reader cannot
otherwise tell this analysis from a correct one: the published numbers look identical.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **$r^2$ is a property of a pair *in a population*.** Two variants at identical allele
  frequencies gave $r^2 = 0.640000$ in one population and $0.078400$ in another, and an
  LD panel is a claim about which population your samples resemble.
- **Tagging depends on $r^2$, not $D'$.** The variance an association carries is
  $q^2_{\text{tag}} = r^2 q^2_{\text{causal}}$, so effective sample size scales as
  $1/r^2$ — 61,877 against 505,115 for the same causal variant.
- **The two normalisations coincide when the loci share an allele frequency**, where
  $r^2 = D'^2$. That is a useful arithmetic check and not a general fact.
- **Pruning is signal-blind, clumping is signal-aware.** Prune for principal components and
  relationship matrices; clump for scores. Swapping them puts phenotype into your ancestry
  axes or throws away the best variant in every block.
- **A mismatched panel corrupts the LDSC slope while leaving the intercept clean**, which is
  the most misleading diagnostic combination there is — the heritability is wrong and nothing
  in the output says so.
