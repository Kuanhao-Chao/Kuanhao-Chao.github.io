## The mental model: a learner, not just a model

A trained network is one output of a learning procedure applied to one sample. Generalization
depends on the population that sample represents, what functions the model and optimizer can
select, and how closely evaluation reproduces deployment.

<Callout variant="intuition">
Training loss asks, “Did this solution fit the examples it saw?” Generalization asks, “What
will the **whole procedure**—sampling, preprocessing, optimization, model selection and
thresholding—do on a new unit from the population we care about?” A test set estimates that
only when it is untouched and population-matched.
</Callout>

## Risk and the classical picture

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-risk">

For loss $\ell$, population risk is
$R(f)=\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\ell(f(X),Y)]$. Because $P$ is unknown, training minimizes
$\hat R_n(f)=n^{-1}\sum_i\ell(f(x_i),y_i)$. Their difference is a generalization gap, but
the quantity we ultimately care about is deployment risk—not a small gap by itself. A model
with both risks high underfits.

Empirical risk minimization is meaningful only after defining the sample unit, loss,
hypothesis class, and target population. Validation used repeatedly for choices becomes
part of training; the final test must evaluate the entire selected procedure. Under shift,
even a small IID test gap can say little about deployment.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-bias-variance">

For $Y=f(X)+\varepsilon$, with $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$ and variance $\sigma^2$,
average over possible training sets $D$:

$$
\mathbb E_{D,Y}[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2]
=\sigma^2+
(\mathbb E_D[\hat f_D(x)]-f(x))^2+
\operatorname{Var}_D(\hat f_D(x)).
$$

Noise is irreducible for predicting $Y$ from $X$; squared bias measures systematic error;
variance measures sensitivity to the sample. It is an exact squared-loss identity at a
fixed $x$, not a universal decomposition for every metric. More capacity often lowers bias
and raises variance, but regularization and optimizer bias can make an overparameterized
model interpolate while remaining stable.

</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="A little shrinkage lowers expected error"
  given={[
    { label: 'Observations', value: 'Yᵢ = μ + εᵢ, μ = 3, Var(εᵢ) = 4' },
    { label: 'Sample size', value: 'n = 4' },
    { label: 'Estimator', value: 'μ̂ = 0.9Ȳ' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>

The estimator's bias is $(0.9-1)3=-0.3$, so squared bias is $0.09$. Its variance is
$0.9^2(4/4)=0.81$. Predicting a new observation adds irreducible noise 4:

$$
\mathbb E[(Y_{new}-\hat\mu)^2]=4+0.09+0.81=4.90.
$$

The unshrunk sample mean has zero bias and variance $4/4=1$, for total error $5.00$.
Shrinkage deliberately buys $0.09$ bias to remove $0.19$ variance. This is not a universal
win: if $\mu$ were far from the shrinkage target zero, squared bias could dominate.

</WorkedExample>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-overfitting">

Underfitting typically shows high training and validation loss: the representation,
optimization, or signal is inadequate. Overfitting shows low training loss but a persistent
validation gap. Plot both against training set size and training steps, then inspect slices
and label quality.

Responses differ. For underfitting: verify optimization, improve features, relax
regularization, or increase capacity. For overfitting: obtain independent data, reduce
effective capacity, augment validly, regularize, or stop earlier. A validation gap can also
be a distribution mismatch; and uniformly poor validation with excellent train scores can
be leakage or duplicate removal exposing the real task.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Capacity and learnability

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-capacity">

Capacity describes how many distinct input-output patterns a hypothesis class and training
procedure can realize. Parameter count can correlate with capacity but misses weight
sharing, norm constraints, margins, architecture, and optimizer selection. A convolutional
network can have fewer parameters yet express a strong translation-aware family; a large
network constrained near initialization may have low effective complexity.

Useful measures depend on the question: VC dimension for binary shattering, Rademacher
complexity for data-dependent fit to random signs, norm or margin bounds for networks, and
algorithmic stability for sensitivity to one sample. Capacity is not inherently bad: enough
is required to express the signal. It becomes risky relative to data, noise, and inductive
bias.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-vc">

A set of $m$ points is shattered if the class can realize all $2^m$ binary labelings. VC
dimension is the largest $m$ for which some set can be shattered. A threshold on the real
line has VC dimension one; intervals have two. Linear separators in general position in
$\mathbb R^d$ have VC dimension $d+1$.

Finite VC dimension enables distribution-free uniform convergence: empirical errors of all
hypotheses approach population errors together as sample size grows
<Citation id="vapnik1971uniform" />. The framework explains capacity control but worst-case
bounds are often loose for deep networks and ignore data geometry and optimizer bias. Use it
as a guarantee language, not a precise sample-size calculator.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-pac">

In the realizable PAC setup, an algorithm is probably approximately correct if, for any
distribution, it returns with probability at least $1-\delta$ a hypothesis with error at
most $\epsilon$, using resources polynomial in $1/\epsilon$, $1/\delta$, and problem size
<Citation id="valiant1984learnable" />. Agnostic PAC compares against the best hypothesis in
the class when labels need not be perfectly realizable.

“Probably” refers to randomness of the sample; “approximately” to allowed error. A PAC
claim must name hypothesis class, data assumptions, loss, computational requirement, and
confidence. It does not say a particular finite training run meets a practical reliability
target under distribution shift.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-generalization-bound">

A representative bound has

$$
R(f)\le\hat R_n(f)+O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{C+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)
$$

simultaneously for a class, where $C$ is a complexity term. It explains why more data and a
smaller effective class shrink uncertainty. Some bounds are uniform over all hypotheses;
others use margins, norms, stability, compression, or posterior-dependent complexity.

Check whether the chosen $f$ was covered, the observations satisfy independence or mixing
conditions, loss is bounded or controlled, and deployment matches the bound's distribution.
A vacuous bound above the loss maximum remains mathematically valid but operationally
uninformative. Bounds support reasoning about dependencies; validation estimates actual
performance.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-no-free-lunch">

No-free-lunch results average performance over all target functions or problems and show
that gains on some must be offset elsewhere <Citation id="wolpert1996nfl" />. The practical
message is that learning requires inductive bias: smoothness, sparsity, locality,
compositionality, invariance, or another assumption matching the domain.

It does **not** mean all algorithms perform equally on images, language, or a defined
business distribution. Those domains are far from uniform over arbitrary labelings.
Compare models using domain knowledge and deployment-matched evidence; state what structural
bias makes a chosen model plausible.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Regularization and overparameterization

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-regularization">

Explicit regularization solves $\min_f\hat R(f)+\lambda\Omega(f)$. L2 discourages large
Euclidean weights and stabilizes correlated linear problems; L1 promotes zeros; group and
structured penalties encode other preferences. In Bayesian terms, they can correspond to
negative log priors under particular parameterizations.

Tune $\lambda$ inside validation. Scaling features changes what equal weight penalties mean.
In neural networks, weight decay and adding an L2 term are not identical for every optimizer,
especially adaptive ones. Regularization trades training fit for a preferred solution; it
cannot fix leakage, wrong labels, or a deployment shift.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-implicit">

Implicit regularization is a solution preference induced by the learning process without an
explicit penalty. Gradient descent on underdetermined linear regression from zero selects a
minimum-L2-norm interpolant. Architecture, initialization, early stopping, stochastic noise,
normalization, and augmentation can similarly privilege some fitted functions.

The distinction matters because two procedures with identical zero training error can
generalize differently. It also means parameter count alone cannot predict behavior. Treat
implicit-bias claims as algorithm- and regime-specific: changing optimizer, learning rate,
batch size, or parameterization may change the selected solution.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-double-descent">

Classical intuition predicts a U-shaped test-error curve as capacity rises. Double descent
adds a peak near the interpolation threshold, followed by decreasing error in a highly
overparameterized regime <Citation id="belkin2019double" />. In linear models, sufficiently
benign data geometry can let the minimum-norm interpolator fit noise without paying large
population error <Citation id="bartlett2020benign" />.

This does not prove “bigger is always better.” The curve depends on data, noise, optimizer,
regularization, and compute; the interpolation peak can be dangerous. It reconciles why deep
nets can fit random labels <Citation id="zhang2017rethinking" /> yet sometimes generalize:
ability to memorize and the solution actually selected are separate facts.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Shift, shortcuts, and evidence

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-shift">

Write $p(x,y)=p(y\mid x)p(x)=p(x\mid y)p(y)$. Covariate shift changes $p(x)$ while
$p(y\mid x)$ stays fixed; importance weighting can correct it if density ratios are
estimable and supports overlap <Citation id="sugiyama2007covariate" />. Label shift changes
$p(y)$ while $p(x\mid y)$ stays fixed. Concept shift changes $p(y\mid x)$ and usually
requires new labels, features, or model adaptation.

Real systems can mix all three. Diagnose feature distributions, prevalence, conditional
performance, calibration, and temporal slices. Unlabeled drift detection cannot establish
concept shift. Reweighting fails when deployment has regions absent from training.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-spurious">

A shortcut is predictive in training but unstable or semantically wrong for deployment: a
hospital token predicting disease, background predicting animal class, or future workflow
code revealing an outcome. ERM rewards it because loss does not distinguish mechanism from
correlation. Average IID accuracy can hide failure on rare groups.

Use environment and subgroup splits, counterfactual perturbations, feature ablations,
error inspection, and external validation. Invariant-learning methods attempt to find
predictors stable across environments <Citation id="arjovsky2019irm" />, but guarantees need
strong environment and causal assumptions. The safest answer combines data provenance,
stress tests, and monitoring rather than naming one robust objective.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-theory-evaluation">

Convincing evidence starts with a target population and untouched evaluation units. Report a
strong simple baseline, confidence across resamples and seeds, subgroup and temporal
performance, calibration, leakage/duplicate audits, ablations, and robustness to plausible
perturbations. Use external, prospective, or online evaluation where consequences justify
it.

Match split unit to deployment: users, patients, devices, sites, or time—not arbitrary rows.
Record every model-selection choice; validation is no longer independent after enough
adaptive tuning. Discuss failure costs and reject options. One leaderboard number is a
comparison on one sample, not evidence of universal generalization.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="Read the learning curves" difficulty="warm-up">

Training and validation losses are both high and nearly equal. Would adding regularization
be your first move? What would you check?

<div slot="solution">

No: the pattern suggests underfitting rather than a variance gap, so stronger regularization
may worsen both. First verify optimization and label/feature correctness. Then try a more
expressive representation, longer or better optimization, or weaker regularization. Check
slices because averaging can hide one easy majority group and one failing minority group.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Bound the generalization gap" difficulty="core">

A simplified bound is $\sqrt{(C+\ln(1/\delta))/n}$. With $C=20$, $\delta=0.05$, and
$n=10{,}000$, compute the term.

<div slot="solution">

$\ln(20)\approx2.996$, so the numerator is $22.996$. Divide by 10,000 and take the square
root: $\sqrt{0.0022996}=0.04795$, about **4.8 percentage points**. This is a bound term under
its assumptions, not an expected observed gap; constants and the definition of $C$ matter.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Name the shift" difficulty="stretch">

A fraud model moves to a country with a higher fraud prevalence. Conditional transaction
patterns $p(x\mid y)$ within fraud and legitimate classes are believed unchanged. What shift
is assumed, and what would you verify before correction?

<div slot="solution">

This is label shift: $p(y)$ changes while $p(x\mid y)$ is invariant. Estimate the new class
prior from unlabeled predictions only if the classifier/confusion model is identifiable and
stable. Verify class-conditional feature and error distributions on labeled samples, support
overlap, calibration after prior adjustment, and whether concept or covariate shift also
occurred.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Expected risk concerns deployment; empirical risk is the observable training surrogate.
- Bias–variance is an exact squared-loss decomposition under explicit averaging assumptions.
- Capacity measures a function class or procedure, not just a raw parameter count.
- VC/PAC and bounds give assumption-dependent guarantees; they need not predict a neural
  network's exact test error.
- Explicit and implicit regularization select among fitted solutions; double descent shows
  interpolation need not imply poor generalization.
- Generalization claims require population-matched, leakage-resistant, sliced and uncertain
  evaluation—especially under shift and shortcuts.
