## The question this page answers

Most trait-associated variants are non-coding, and the previous layer's QTLs only reach the
ones that change a measurable molecular phenotype in a tissue somebody assayed. For the rest,
the question is whether the variant sits somewhere the genome is doing regulatory work at all.

Regulatory maps answer that — with a caveat that is easy to state and constantly dropped.

<Callout variant="intuition">
These assays measure **correlates of activity**, not activity. Open chromatin means a
nuclease could cut there. A ChIP-seq peak means a protein was crosslinked nearby. A histone
mark means a particular modification was present.

Each is genuinely informative and none of them demonstrates that the sequence *does*
anything. The genome contains far more places that look regulatory than places whose
disruption changes a gene — which is why the field's own vocabulary hedges: ENCODE publishes
**candidate** cis-regulatory elements, and the word is doing real work.

Turning a candidate into a finding needs perturbation: change the sequence and see whether
expression moves. That is what the CRISPR screens and IGVF exist to do, and it is why a
variant landing in a cCRE is a hypothesis rather than a mechanism.
</Callout>

## What the assays measure

**DNase-seq and ATAC-seq** find accessible chromatin — regions a nuclease or a transposase can
reach because nucleosomes are not packed over them <Citation id="buenrostro2013atac" />. ATAC
needs far fewer cells, which is why single-cell chromatin profiling is an ATAC method.

**ChIP-seq** finds where a particular protein was crosslinked to DNA: a transcription factor,
or a histone carrying a specific modification. H3K4me1 and H3K27ac together are the usual
enhancer signature, H3K4me3 marks promoters, H3K27me3 marks repression.

**Chromatin states** are not measurements at all. A segmentation such as Roadmap's assigns
each region a label — enhancer, promoter, heterochromatin — by fitting a hidden Markov model
across several marks <Citation id="roadmap2015" />. The label is model output, and two
segmentations of the same data can disagree.

**<Dataset id="encode" />** aggregates all of it and publishes the cCRE registry
<Citation id="encode2012" />
<Citation id="encode2020ccre" />. **<Dataset id="roadmap" />**
complements it with primary tissues rather than cell lines.

**<Dataset id="igvf" />** and **<Dataset id="crispr-screens" />** are the step past
correlation <Citation id="igvf2024" />. CRISPR interference tiled across a locus perturbs
elements directly and measures which genes respond <Citation id="gasperini2019crispr" />,
and the activity-by-contact model turns accessibility and 3D contact into a prediction of
which element regulates which gene <Citation id="fulco2019abc" />. The recurring result is
that the regulated gene is frequently not the nearest one.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The registry is a union, and a union answers a weaker question">
The cCRE registry is built across hundreds of biosamples, so an element is in it if it looked
regulatory *anywhere*. Intersecting a variant list with the whole registry therefore asks
"does this sit somewhere that is regulatory in some cell type", which a large fraction of the
genome satisfies.

The question worth asking is almost always narrower — is it regulatory *in the tissue this
trait acts through* — and answering it means intersecting with one biosample's peaks, not the
union. The two give very different enrichments, and papers that report the union figure while
discussing a specific tissue are comparing against the wrong denominator.
</Callout>

## Enrichment, and the two questions it hides

<WorkedExample
  title="Fine-mapped variants in candidate regulatory elements"
  given={[
    { label: 'Fine-mapped variants', value: '500, of which 120 fall in a cCRE' },
    { label: 'Background', value: '10,000 matched control variants, 800 in a cCRE' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the two rates.**

$$
\frac{120}{500} = 0.2400, \qquad f_{\text{bg}} = \frac{800}{10{,}000} = 0.0800
$$

**Step 2 — what the background predicts.** If the fine-mapped variants were an ordinary sample
of the genome, the expected overlap would be

$$
500 \times 0.0800 = 40.0 \text{ variants}
$$

against 120 observed.

**Step 3 — fold enrichment.**

$$
\text{fold} = \frac{0.2400}{0.0800} = 3.0000
$$

**Step 4 — significance.** The one-sided Fisher exact test on the 2×2 table asks how often
chance alone would produce 120 or more:

$$
p = 6.473\times10^{-26}
$$

**Step 5 — read them as two separate claims.** The fold change says the effect is
*large* — three times background. The p-value says it is *not chance*. Neither implies the
other, and the next exercises separate them: the same threefold enrichment is unremarkable
with twelve variants, and a 1.4-fold enrichment is comfortably significant with five hundred.

</WorkedExample>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="The same enrichment, a different verdict" difficulty="warm-up">

A smaller study fine-maps only 12 variants and finds 3 of them in cCREs, against the same 8%
background. What is the fold enrichment, and is it significant?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{fold} = \frac{3/12}{0.08} = \frac{0.25}{0.08} = 3.1250
\qquad
p = 6.549\times10^{-2}
$$

Slightly *more* enriched than the worked example, and **not significant** at 0.05.

Nothing about the biology differs. Three variants out of twelve is compatible with chance
because chance produces about one, and getting three instead is not surprising. Five hundred
variants producing 120 where 40 were expected is a different matter entirely.

The lesson is that a fold enrichment is an effect size and needs its sample size quoted beside
it, exactly as an odds ratio does. A paper reporting "3.1-fold enrichment in regulatory
elements" without the counts has reported half a result.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Significant and uninteresting" difficulty="core">

A study of 500 fine-mapped variants finds 56 in cCREs. Compute the enrichment and the
p-value, and say whether the result supports a regulatory mechanism.

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{fold} = \frac{56/500}{0.08} = \frac{0.1120}{0.08} = 1.4000
\qquad
p = 8.750\times10^{-3}
$$

**Significant, and weak.** It is comfortably below 0.05 — and it is more significant than
the threefold result on twelve variants from Exercise 1, which is worth sitting with.

What does a 1.4-fold enrichment support? That *some* of these variants act through regulatory
elements, which was never in doubt. It does not support the claim the reader usually takes
from such a sentence, that the trait acts predominantly through regulation: 88% of the
variants are still outside cCREs, and the excess amounts to about sixteen variants.

$$
56 - 500 \times 0.08 = 16 \text{ variants above expectation}
$$

Significance answers "is this more than chance". Fold enrichment answers "by enough to matter".
Reporting only the first is how a marginal effect acquires the language of a mechanism.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The background you did not choose carefully" difficulty="stretch">

The worked example compared against controls that are 8% cCRE. Fine-mapped variants are not
ordinary variants — they are common, near genes, and in well-covered regions. Suppose properly
matched controls turn out to be 12% cCRE, or 18%. What happens?

<div slot="solution">

The observed rate is fixed at $120/500 = 0.2400$; only the comparison moves.

| background $f_{\text{bg}}$ | fold | $p$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.08 | 3.0000 | $6.473\times10^{-26}$ |
| 0.12 | 2.0000 | $4.427\times10^{-13}$ |
| 0.18 | 1.3333 | $6.224\times10^{-4}$ |

The enrichment falls from threefold to a third, and the result goes from overwhelming to
merely significant — without a single variant changing.

**Why the background is not a detail.** Fine-mapped variants are selected for being common
enough to be well powered, for being in regions with dense genotyping, and often for being
near genes. Every one of those properties raises the chance of landing in a cCRE for reasons
having nothing to do with the trait. A background of "all variants in dbSNP" therefore builds
in an enrichment before the biology is consulted.

The remedy is matched controls — sampled to have the same allele-frequency spectrum, the same
distance-to-gene distribution and the same LD-block structure — which is what turns the number
into a statement about the trait rather than about the ascertainment. When a paper's enrichment
looks implausibly large, the background is the first thing to check.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **These assays measure correlates of activity.** Accessibility means a nuclease could cut
  there; a ChIP peak means a protein was crosslinked nearby; a chromatin state is a hidden
  Markov model's label, not a measurement.
- **A candidate cis-regulatory element is a candidate.** The genome has far more
  regulatory-looking sequence than sequence whose disruption moves a gene, which is why
  perturbation — CRISPR screens, IGVF — is a different kind of evidence, and why the regulated
  gene is frequently not the nearest one.
- **The cCRE registry is a union across biosamples.** Intersecting with all of it asks whether
  a variant is regulatory *somewhere*; the useful question is almost always about one tissue.
- **Fold enrichment and significance are separate claims.** 120 of 500 variants against an 8%
  background is 3.0000-fold at $p = 6.473\times10^{-26}$; the same threefold enrichment on
  twelve variants is $p = 6.549\times10^{-2}$, and a 1.4-fold enrichment on five hundred is
  $p = 8.750\times10^{-3}$ — more significant than the stronger effect.
- **The background decides the answer.** Holding the observations fixed, moving the control
  rate from 8% to 18% takes the enrichment from 3.0 to 1.33. Fine-mapped variants are selected
  in ways that raise cCRE overlap on their own, so matched controls are what make the number a
  statement about the trait.
