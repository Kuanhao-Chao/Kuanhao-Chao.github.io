## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson arrived at $5\times10^{-8}$ by demanding that the probability of **even
one** false positive across the whole scan stay below 0.05. That demand has a name — the
family-wise error rate — and it is a strong one. It is the right demand when a single false
claim is expensive: a variant reported as disease-causing, a locus that will absorb a decade
of follow-up.

It is the wrong demand almost everywhere else in molecular genetics. An eQTL scan testing
twenty thousand genes expects thousands of real signals, and a procedure built to avoid one
error will discard most of them. What is wanted there is not "no mistakes" but "a known and
tolerable proportion of mistakes" — a different quantity, controlled by a different procedure,
at a different price.

This lesson derives that quantity and that price. It owns the theory only: the genome-wide
threshold and its $M_{\mathrm{eff}}$ belong to lesson 1, and which to reach for on a given
dataset belongs to the GWAS track's lesson on reading a scan's output.

## Two error rates, and which is larger

Write $V$ for the number of false rejections and $R$ for the total. The two rates are

$$
\mathrm{FWER} = \Pr(V \ge 1),
\qquad
\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{V}{R}\,\middle|\,R>0\right]\Pr(R>0)
$$

The conditioning is a convention that sets the ratio to zero when nothing is rejected, which
is the only sensible reading of "what fraction of my discoveries are wrong" when there are no
discoveries.

**FWER is never smaller than FDR.** If $V \ge 1$ then $V/R \le 1$, so
$\mathbb{E}[V/R] \le \Pr(V \ge 1)$. Controlling the family-wise rate therefore controls the
false discovery rate automatically — the converse fails, and that gap is exactly the power
that FDR control buys.

The two coincide when every null is true. Then any rejection is false, $V = R$, and $V/R = 1$
whenever $R > 0$. So a procedure that controls FDR at $q$ under the global null also controls
FWER at $q$ — which is worth knowing, because it means FDR control is not a weaker guarantee
in the case where nothing is there to find. It is weaker only when something is.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The two questions a reviewer might be asking">

"Is any of this real?" is a family-wise question. One false positive ruins the answer, so the
procedure has to make one false positive unlikely, and the cost of that is a threshold that
scales with how many tests you ran.

"How much of this list can I trust?" is a false-discovery question. The answer "about 95% of
it" is useful even when the list is long, and crucially the threshold does **not** have to
scale with $m$ in the same punishing way — it adapts to how many signals are actually there.

A genome-wide scan looking for the handful of loci that will be followed up is asking the
first question. A differential-expression analysis producing a gene list for enrichment
testing is asking the second. Neither procedure is more correct; they answer different
questions.

</Callout>

## The step-up procedure

<Citation id="benjamini1995fdr" /> sort the p-values and find

$$
k = \max\left\{ i : p_{(i)} \le \frac{i}{m}\,q \right\}
$$

then reject the $k$ smallest. The comparison line rises with rank, which is the whole idea: a
p-value does not have to clear the strictest possible bar, only the bar appropriate to how
many other tests are also small.

<Callout variant="key" title="Why it is a step-up, and what that changes">

The rule takes the **largest** $i$ that passes, not every $i$ that passes. A p-value sitting
above its own line is still rejected if some larger rank clears its own — because the
evidence that many tests are small is itself evidence that this one is not a fluke.

That is not a technicality; it is where the extra power comes from. Comparing each $p_{(i)}$
against $iq/m$ independently and rejecting only those that pass would be a different, weaker
procedure.

</Callout>

**Benjamini–Hochberg.** The line at rank $i$ is $i \times 0.05/20 = 0.0025i$. Rank 1 passes
(0.0001 against 0.0025), rank 2 passes, rank 3 passes, **rank 4 fails** (0.0115 against
0.0100), rank 5 passes (0.0120 against 0.0125), rank 6 passes, rank 7 passes (0.0140 against
0.0175), rank 8 fails and nothing beyond it passes. The largest passing rank is 7, so
**7 rejections** at an effective threshold of 0.0140 — including the rank-4 test that failed
its own comparison.

**Bonferroni.** Every test against $0.05/20 = 0.0025$: three p-values clear it, so
**3 rejections**.

**Benjamini–Yekutieli.** The same staircase divided by $H_{20} = 3.5977$, so the line at rank
$i$ is $0.000695i$. Rank 1 passes, rank 2 passes, rank 3 fails (0.0021 against 0.002085), and
nothing later recovers it: **2 rejections**, fewer than Bonferroni.

**Uncorrected.** Ten p-values fall below 0.05, so the naive count is **10** — of which, if the
nulls were all true, one would be expected by chance at this many tests.

</WorkedExample>

## What BH actually controls

Here is the property that matters, and that is most often misquoted. Run at nominal $q$, the
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate not at $q$ but at

$$
\mathrm{FDR}_{\mathrm{BH}} = \pi_0\, q
$$

where $\pi_0 = m_0/m$ is the proportion of hypotheses for which the null is true. The
procedure spends only the null share of the budget it was handed.

Simulation with 2,000 independent tests and 3,000 replicates reproduces it: at $\pi_0 = 0.95$
the realised rate is 0.0474 against a predicted 0.0475; at 0.90, 0.0449 against 0.0450; at
0.75, 0.0376 against 0.0375; at 0.50, 0.0251 against 0.0250; at 0.25, 0.0125 against 0.0125.
Every one within a standard error.

<Callout variant="assumption" title="When the conservatism matters, and when it does not">

In a genome-wide scan almost everything is null — $\pi_0$ is 0.999 or better — so $\pi_0 q$
and $q$ are the same number and nothing is lost.

In a *cis*-eQTL scan a large fraction of genes have a real signal, and $\pi_0$ near 0.5 means
BH is controlling at half the rate asked for. That is where recovering the difference is worth
something, and it is why q-value machinery is standard in molecular-QTL work and essentially
absent from GWAS.

</Callout>

## Recovering the unspent budget, and what it is worth

If $\pi_0$ were known, running BH at $q/\pi_0$ would control at exactly $q$.
<Citation id="storey2003qvalue" /> estimates it from the shape of the p-value histogram: real
effects pile up near zero, so above some $\lambda$ the histogram is essentially all null and
flat. Count what lands there and scale by the width:

$$
\hat{\pi}_0 = \frac{\#\{p_i > \lambda\}}{m(1-\lambda)}
$$

On the twenty p-values above, four exceed 0.5, giving $\hat{\pi}_0 = 4/10 = 0.40$. That
estimate rests on four observations and should not be trusted — Storey's method is a large-$m$
method, and at $m = 20$ the histogram has no shape to read.

Because $\pi_0 = 0.5$, the corrected procedure runs BH at $q/\hat{\pi}_0 = 0.10$ — exactly
twice as permissive, at every rank, in every row below. The discovery counts, averaged over
200 replicates:

| Alternative mean *z* | Per-test power | BH finds | Storey finds | Gain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2.0 | 0.222 | 2,281 | 3,444 | 1.510 |
| 2.5 | 0.507 | 5,197 | 6,673 | 1.284 |
| 3.0 | 0.744 | 7,635 | 8,792 | 1.152 |
| 3.5 | 0.890 | 9,125 | 9,865 | 1.081 |
| 4.0 | 0.960 | 9,847 | 10,314 | 1.048 |

**Doubling the threshold does not double the discoveries anywhere.** The gain runs from 51%
down to 5%, and the reason is geometric: the procedure stops where the sorted p-value curve
crosses the staircase, and moving the staircase up buys many extra rejections only if the
p-value curve is shallow there. Strong effects make that curve steep — most of the real
p-values are far below the crossing and already rejected — so raising the line catches almost
nothing new.

The practical reading is the opposite of the usual intuition about refinements. **Storey's
correction pays most exactly where power is worst**, which is where a study can least afford
to leave discoveries on the table.

</WorkedExample>

## The price of dependence

BH's guarantee as proved assumes independent tests. Real ones are not: variants in LD produce
correlated statistics, and so do genes in a co-expression module.

<Citation id="benjamini2001dependency" /> settled this in two parts. Under **positive
regression dependence** — which is what LD produces, since correlated tests are pushed in the
same direction — plain BH still controls the FDR at $\pi_0 q$, unmodified. Under *arbitrary*
dependence it does not, and the fix is to run at $q/H_m$ with $H_m = \sum_{i=1}^{m} 1/i$.

$H_m$ grows as $\ln m + \gamma$, so the price is logarithmic rather than linear: 3.5977 at
twenty tests, 10.4807 at twenty thousand, **14.3927 at a million**. Against Bonferroni's
factor of $m$, a factor of 14 is cheap.

<Callout variant="warning" title="BY is not uniformly weaker than Bonferroni">

The BY line at rank $i$ is $iq/(H_m m)$ and Bonferroni's is $\alpha/m$. Setting them equal
gives $i = H_m$ exactly. So for the $H_m$ smallest p-values BY is **stricter** than
Bonferroni, and only above that rank does it become more permissive.

At $m = 20$ that crossing is at rank 3.5977, which is why the worked example above has BY
rejecting two where Bonferroni rejects three: only three p-values were small enough to matter
and all of them sat below the crossing. BY wins when there are many signals, and loses when
there are few — which is the same trade FDR control always makes, sharpened by the harmonic
penalty.

</Callout>

For completeness, the family-wise side has an exact counterpart to Bonferroni's approximation.
<Citation id="sidak1967" /> gives $1-(1-\alpha)^{1/m}$, which is what
$\Pr(\text{no false positive}) = (1-\alpha_{\text{per test}})^m$ inverts to under
independence. It is uniformly less strict than $\alpha/m$, by a factor that converges to
$-\ln(1-\alpha)/\alpha = 1.025866$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ and is essentially independent of $m$:
at a million tests Šidák gives $5.129\times10^{-8}$ against Bonferroni's $5\times10^{-8}$.
Two and a half percent on a threshold changes nothing anyone will notice — the conservatism
people actually feel in Bonferroni comes from treating correlated tests as independent, which
is the $M_{\mathrm{eff}}$ question of lesson 1

## What a false discovery rate does not tell you

A false discovery rate is a property of a **set**. It says that among everything you rejected,
such-and-such a fraction is expected to be wrong. It says almost nothing about any particular
member of that set, and the difference is large.

Take 20,000 tests with $\pi_0 = 0.5$ and alternatives at $z \sim N(3,1)$, run BH at
$q = 0.05$. Over 300 replicates the realised FDR is 0.0251, as $\pi_0 q$ predicts. But among
the discoveries in the **last tenth before the cut** — the ones that only just made it —
**10.62% are false**, 4.22 times the set-level rate.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The misreading this produces">

"This gene passed at q &lt; 0.05, so there is a 5% chance it is a false positive" does not
follow, and is wrong in the direction that matters: for a marginal discovery the chance is
several times higher. The quantity that *does* answer the per-test question is the local false
discovery rate <Citation id="efron2004largescale" />, the posterior probability of the null
given that test's own statistic — a different object, computed from the mixture density rather
than from a tail area.

The practical consequence is that a ranked list should be read as a ranked list. The top of a
q &lt; 0.05 list is far more trustworthy than the bottom of it, and the single summary number
attached to the whole list conceals that entirely.

</Callout>

<Exercise n={1} title="Walking the staircase" difficulty="warm-up">

Ten tests give p-values 0.0004, 0.0090, 0.0180, 0.0220, 0.0300, 0.0400, 0.0600, 0.1200,
0.4000, 0.7000. At $q = 0.05$: (a) how many does BH reject? (b) how many does Bonferroni?
(c) which p-values fail their own line but are rejected anyway?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The line at rank $i$ is $0.005i$. Rank 1: 0.0004 ≤ 0.005 ✓. Rank 2: 0.0090 ≤ 0.010 ✓.
Rank 3: 0.0180 > 0.015 ✗. Rank 4: 0.0220 > 0.020 ✗. Rank 5: 0.0300 > 0.025 ✗. Rank 6:
0.0400 > 0.030 ✗. Rank 7: 0.0600 > 0.035 ✗, and the rest are worse. The largest passing rank
is 2, so **2 rejections**.

**(b)** $0.05/10 = 0.005$; only 0.0004 clears it, so **1 rejection**.

**(c)** None — here the largest passing rank is 2 and both of the rejected p-values pass their
own lines. The step-up only matters when a later rank rescues an earlier one, which needs a
run of small p-values rather than one very small one. This dataset has a single strong signal
and a long tail, which is the shape where BH gains least over Bonferroni.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Which rate for which study" difficulty="core">

For each, say which error rate is appropriate and why: (a) a GWAS reporting loci that will
each receive a functional follow-up study; (b) a differential-expression analysis whose output
is a gene list fed to pathway enrichment; (c) a screen returning a single candidate drug
target for a five-year programme.

<div slot="solution">

**(a) Family-wise.** Each reported locus commissions expensive work, so the cost of one false
positive is measured in years. It is also the case that $\pi_0$ is very close to 1 here, so
FDR control at $q$ and at $\pi_0 q$ are the same thing and the choice costs nothing in
conservatism terms — the real difference is that FWER at 0.05 across a million tests is a far
stricter bar than FDR at 0.05.

**(b) False discovery.** The list is an input to an aggregate procedure that is itself robust
to a minority of wrong entries; enrichment testing over a list that is 95% correct gives
essentially the right answer. Insisting on FWER here would shrink the list to a handful of
genes and destroy the enrichment analysis's power. This is also the setting where $\pi_0$ is
far from 1, so Storey's correction is worth applying.

**(c) Family-wise, and arguably stricter.** With one candidate going forward, the false
discovery rate of the *set* is irrelevant — what matters is the probability that this
particular candidate is wrong, which is the local false discovery rate, not the tail one. A
q-value of 0.05 on a list whose marginal members are 10% false is not the reassurance it looks
like.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The crossing" difficulty="core">

At $m = 1{,}000{,}000$ and $q = 0.05$: (a) what is the Benjamini–Yekutieli level $q/H_m$?
(b) at what rank does the BY line cross Bonferroni's? (c) a scan yields 400 p-values below
$10^{-7}$. Which procedure rejects more, and why?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $H_{10^6} = 14.3927$, so $q/H_m = 0.05/14.3927 = 3.474\times10^{-3}$.

**(b)** At rank $i = H_m = 14.3927$ — call it the fifteenth smallest p-value. Below that rank
BY is stricter than Bonferroni; above it, more permissive.

**(c)** BY, by a wide margin. Bonferroni's cutoff is $5\times10^{-8}$, so of the 400 p-values
below $10^{-7}$ only those also below $5\times10^{-8}$ are rejected. BY's line at rank 400 is
$400 \times 3.474\times10^{-3}/10^6 = 1.39\times10^{-6}$, comfortably above $10^{-7}$, so all
400 clear it provided the staircase is satisfied at that rank. The crossing at rank 14 is far
behind; with 400 signals present, the procedure that scales its line with the number of
signals wins easily.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="Reading a q-value correctly" difficulty="stretch">

A collaborator reports 1,000 genes at $q < 0.05$ and says "so about 50 of these are false
positives, and any one of them has a 5% chance of being wrong." (a) Which half of that is
right? (b) Given that in a comparable simulation the last tenth of discoveries ran 4.22 times
the set-level false rate, roughly how many of the bottom 100 genes would you expect to be
false? (c) What would you ask for instead?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The first half is right and the second is wrong. The expected number of false
discoveries in the set really is about $0.05 \times 1000 = 50$ — that is what controlling the
rate at 0.05 means. The per-gene statement does not follow, because the 50 are not spread
evenly through the list.

**(b)** If the bottom tenth runs at 4.22 times the set rate, that is roughly
$0.05 \times 4.22 = 0.211$, so about 21 of the bottom 100 genes — nearly half the total false
discoveries concentrated in a tenth of the list. The top of the list is correspondingly
cleaner than 5%.

**(c)** A local false discovery rate per gene, or at minimum the ranked list with its
p-values, so that the reader can see where in the list a gene sits. "Made the cut" is not a
statement about a gene; where it sits relative to the cut is.

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Two rates, and FWER ≥ FDR always**, with equality under the global null. Controlling the
  family-wise rate controls the false discovery rate automatically; the converse fails, and
  that gap is the power FDR control buys.
- **BH is a step-up procedure**: take the largest rank whose p-value clears $iq/m$ and reject
  everything below it. A p-value above its own line is still rejected — that is where the
  power comes from, not a technicality.
- **BH controls at $\pi_0 q$, not $q$.** Simulation reproduces it at five null fractions to
  within a standard error. When almost everything is null the distinction vanishes; at
  $\pi_0 = 0.5$ the procedure is controlling at half the rate asked for.
- **Storey's correction recovers the remainder, and pays most where power is worst** — at
  $\pi_0 = 0.5$ the threshold doubles everywhere, but the discovery count rises 51% at a
  per-test power of 0.222 and 5% at 0.960.
- **Arbitrary dependence costs a factor of $H_m$** — 14.3927 at a million tests — and
  Benjamini–Yekutieli crosses Bonferroni at exactly rank $H_m$, so it is stricter below that
  rank. Under the positive dependence that LD produces, plain BH needs no correction at all.
- **A false discovery rate describes a set.** At a set-level 2.51%, the discoveries nearest
  the cut ran 10.62% false — 4.22 times higher. The per-test question is answered by the local
  false discovery rate, which is a different object.
