## The question this lesson answers

The command is one line. What is it doing ten million times, and which parts of it were
decisions?

The regression itself is standard and the derivations belong to
[Mathematical Foundations](/deep_dives/statgen-mathematical-foundations/), which builds the
score, Wald and likelihood-ratio tests from one likelihood. This lesson is about the three
choices wrapped around that regression which change what comes out: how genotypes are coded,
which covariates go in, and what happens to a binary trait when the arms are badly
unbalanced.

<Callout variant="intuition">

Every genotype encoding is the **same test with different scores**. Give the three genotypes
numbers, correlate those numbers with the phenotype, and the trend statistic is $N$ times the
squared correlation. Additive scores them 0, 1, 2; dominant scores 0, 1, 1; recessive scores
0, 0, 1. Nothing else changes — same table, same machinery, same one degree of freedom, same
threshold. The choice is purely a claim about which arrangement of the three genotypes the
biology follows.

</Callout>

## One locus, three encodings

<WorkedExample
  title="A recessive-truth locus, tested three ways"
  given={[
    { label: 'Cases', value: '1,000 / 1,000 / 900 by genotype' },
    { label: 'Controls', value: '1,200 / 1,200 / 400 by genotype' },
    { label: 'Threshold', value: 'χ² ≥ 29.7168 at 5 × 10⁻⁸, 1 df' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — one machine, three sets of scores.** The trend statistic on a 2 × 3 table has an
exact closed form: it is $N$ times the squared correlation between the genotype score and
case status,

$$
\chi^2_{\mathrm{CA}} = N\,r^2_{x,y} .
$$

Feeding the three encodings through it:

| encoding | scores | χ² | reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| additive | 0, 1, 2 | 152.6291 | strongly significant |
| dominant | 0, 1, 1 | 42.1547 | significant |
| recessive | 0, 0, 1 | 226.9868 | strongly significant, and the largest |

**Step 2 — the recessive test wins, as it should.** These counts were built so that risk
sits only in the double-carrier genotype, and the encoding that matches recovers the most
signal. That is the case *for* trying several encodings.

**Step 3 — and the case against.** The additive test returns 152.6291, which is

$$
\frac{152.6291}{226.9868} = 67.2\%
$$

of the best available statistic — and still enormously significant, at more than five times
the threshold. The additive encoding did not match the biology and lost a third of the
statistic; it did not lose the locus.

**Step 4 — what running all three would cost.** Three tests per variant means a Bonferroni
correction of $5 \times 10^{-8}/3 = 1.6667 \times 10^{-8}$, which moves the threshold from

$$
\chi^2 \geq 29.7168 \qquad\text{to}\qquad \chi^2 \geq 31.8486 .
$$

That penalty applies at **every variant in the genome**, including the overwhelming majority
where the truth is close to additive and the extra encodings find nothing. The unconstrained
2-df genotypic test is worse still, needing $\chi^2 \geq 33.6225$.

So the scan runs additive, and the loci where that choice cost something are found again in
follow-up, at one locus rather than ten million. This is not a claim that biology is
additive. It is a claim that across the monotone models — where risk rises or falls with
allele count — the additive test's worst case is acceptable, which is a different and more
defensible statement.

It is worth knowing where that guarantee stops. Under a **heterozygote-effect** model, where
both homozygotes carry the same risk and only the heterozygote differs, the 0/1/2 score is
uncorrelated with the pattern and the additive test collapses completely: cases of
1,000 / 1,400 / 1,000 against controls of 1,200 / 1,000 / 1,200 give an additive $\chi^2$ of
exactly $0$ while dominant and recessive both return $26.8775$. Overdominance is rare enough
in complex traits that the default survives, but "never the worst" is not true of it.

</WorkedExample>

## Covariates, and the ones that hurt

Age, sex, genotyping batch and the ancestry components from the previous lesson are the
standard set, and including them is uncontroversial. Two other kinds are not.

**Do not adjust for a heritable mediator.** If the variant affects the trait *through* some
intermediate — body mass index in a study of type 2 diabetes, say — then adjusting for that
intermediate removes the part of the effect that runs through it, and the remaining estimate
answers a question nobody asked. Worse, if the mediator is itself heritable and shares causes
with the outcome, conditioning on it opens a path between them and can create association
where none exists. This bias does not shrink with sample size; it is estimated more precisely.

**Be careful adjusting for anything measured after the phenotype.** The general rule is that
a covariate is safe if it could not have been affected by the genotype. Age and sex qualify.
Medication use, disease duration and biomarker levels do not.

**Adjust for what generated the data.** Genotyping batch, plate, and recruitment centre are
the covariates most likely to matter and least likely to be recorded properly. If cases and
controls were assayed separately, no covariate rescues the study — that is a design failure,
and the differential-missingness test in
[Quality Control](/deep_dives/gwas-quality-control/) is where it shows up.

## Binary traits, and where the standard model breaks

For a quantitative trait, linear regression on dosage is the whole story. For a binary trait
there is a subtlety worth knowing before it produces a false positive.

The ordinary logistic score test is calibrated when the expected cell counts are reasonable.
It stops being calibrated when they are not — and that happens exactly where a modern biobank
is most interesting: a rare variant, in a study with 1,000 cases and 400,000 controls. The
test becomes anti-conservative, and the resulting p-values are too small in a way that no
genomic-control-style correction fixes, because the miscalibration depends on the variant's
frequency.

Two remedies are standard. **Firth's penalised likelihood** shrinks the estimate and restores
calibration at the cost of some power, and is what REGENIE applies to variants that trip its
threshold <Citation id="mbatchou2021regenie" />. **Saddlepoint approximation**, as
implemented in SAIGE <Citation id="zhou2018saige" />, approximates the score statistic's
actual distribution rather than assuming it is normal, which is both accurate and cheap
enough for whole-biobank use.

<Callout variant="key" title="Choosing the software">

The three mainstream choices differ in what they solve, not in the regression they run.
<Citation id="purcell2007plink" /> and its successor <Citation id="chang2015plink2" /> are
fastest for unrelated samples with covariate adjustment.
<Citation id="loh2015bolt" /> and <Citation id="kang2010emmax" /> fit the mixed model that
absorbs relatedness and structure together; <Citation id="jiang2019fastgwa" /> makes that
affordable at biobank scale. <Citation id="mbatchou2021regenie" /> and
<Citation id="zhou2018saige" /> add the binary-trait machinery above.

Whichever you use, check whether it leaves the tested variant's chromosome out of the
relationship matrix <Citation id="yang2014mlm" />. A pipeline that does not is quietly
fitting the signal away, and the symptom is a scan that looks clean and finds less than it
should.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="The additive test on an additive locus" difficulty="warm-up">

Cases are 1,200 / 1,600 / 700 by genotype; controls are 1,500 / 1,550 / 450.

(a) Which of the three encodings gives the largest statistic?

(b) The additive test gives 86.2613. How does that compare with the others?

(c) What does that tell you about running additive only?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The additive test, at 86.2613. Dominant gives 54.2636 and recessive 65.0316.

**(b)** It is the largest of the three, by a comfortable margin — these counts were built
with an additive truth, and the matching encoding wins.

**(c)** That this locus costs nothing. Combined with the worked example, the pattern is the
one that justifies the convention: when the truth is additive the additive test is best, and
when it is not, the additive test is still close enough to find the locus. A scan that runs
additive only is trading a bounded loss at a few loci against a threshold penalty at all of
them.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="What does a fourth encoding cost?" difficulty="core">

A team proposes testing additive, dominant, recessive and the 2-df genotypic model at every
variant.

(a) What threshold does a Bonferroni correction over four tests imply, on the 1-df scale?

(b) The 2-df test has its own threshold. What is it at $5 \times 10^{-8}$, and what at
$5 \times 10^{-8}/4$?

(c) A locus has additive $\chi^2 = 30.5$. What happens to it under this scheme?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\alpha = 5 \times 10^{-8}/4 = 1.25 \times 10^{-8}$, which on 1 df is
$\chi^2 \geq 32.4075$.

**(b)** The 2-df test needs $\chi^2 \geq 33.6225$ at $5 \times 10^{-8}$ and
$\chi^2 \geq 36.3951$ at $1.25 \times 10^{-8}$. Note that these are not comparable with the
1-df numbers — a 2-df statistic of 36 is not "larger" evidence than a 1-df statistic of 33.
Comparing them directly is a common and serious error.

**(c)** It is lost. At 30.5 it clears the single-test threshold of 29.7168 and fails the
four-test threshold of 32.4075. The team would have to find it at one of the other three
encodings by a wide enough margin to pay for all four — and if the truth is additive, they
will not.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A covariate that should not be there" difficulty="stretch">

A study of type 2 diabetes adjusts for body mass index, reasoning that BMI is a confounder.
The reported effect of a known adiposity variant drops to near zero, and a variant with no
prior connection to either trait becomes significant.

(a) Is BMI a confounder here?

(b) Explain both observations.

(c) What should the study report instead?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** No. A confounder causes both exposure and outcome. Genotype is fixed at conception,
so nothing measured in adulthood can cause it — BMI here is a **mediator**, on the path from
genotype to disease.

**(b)** The first observation is mediation: adjusting for BMI removes the part of the
variant's effect that runs *through* BMI, and for an adiposity variant that is most of it.
The estimate that survives answers "what is this variant's effect on diabetes at fixed BMI?",
which is a coherent question and not the one the study asked.

The second is **collider bias**. Conditioning on BMI — a common effect of many causes — opens
a non-causal path between anything else that influences BMI and anything else that influences
diabetes. A variant affecting BMI through a route unrelated to diabetes can acquire an
apparent association with diabetes purely from the adjustment. It will replicate in any other
study that adjusts the same way, which is what makes it dangerous.

**(c)** The unadjusted association as the primary result, since that is the effect of the
variant on the disease. A BMI-adjusted analysis can be reported alongside it, labelled as
what it is — an estimate of the effect not mediated by BMI — with the collider risk stated.
Where the mediation itself is the question, a design built for it is required, not a
covariate.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Every encoding is the same test with different scores**, and the trend statistic is
  exactly $N r^2$ between the score and case status. Additive, dominant and recessive are all
  1-df and directly comparable.
- **The additive encoding is the default because its worst case is acceptable**, not because
  biology is additive. On a recessive-truth locus it still returns 67.2% of the best
  statistic and finds the locus comfortably.
- **Running more encodings costs the whole genome.** Three tests move the threshold from
  $\chi^2 \geq 29.7168$ to $31.8486$, and four move it to $32.4075$, at every variant —
  including the vast majority where the extra encodings find nothing.
- **Do not adjust for a heritable mediator.** It removes the effect you are measuring, and
  conditioning on a collider can manufacture associations that replicate in every study that
  makes the same mistake.
- **The ordinary logistic test loses calibration exactly where biobanks are interesting** —
  rare variants in badly unbalanced cohorts. Firth's penalisation or a saddlepoint
  approximation is the fix, and both are standard in the software that expects to meet the
  problem.
