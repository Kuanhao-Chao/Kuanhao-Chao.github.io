## The question behind every metric

Suppose two fraud models have these summaries:

- model A has AUROC $0.96$;
- model B has precision $0.72$ at 80% recall;
- the operations team can investigate 400 alerts per day;
- a missed fraud costs about 100 times a manual review.

Which model is better? There is deliberately not enough information. AUROC averages over
thresholds the team will never use. Precision depends on the deployment prevalence. Neither
number says whether 400 alerts reach the high-cost cases or whether the labels arrive soon
enough to measure them. A strong interview answer notices the missing decision before doing
arithmetic.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="A metric is a compressed decision model">
Every metric chooses—often silently—what counts as an error, how errors are weighted, which
examples are averaged together, and whether probabilities, rankings, or decisions are being
judged. Start an evaluation answer with four nouns: **decision, cost, population, unit**.

Only then name a metric. This habit prevents the most common interview failure: reciting a
catalogue of formulas while leaving the actual product question unanswered.
</Callout>

## Choosing and interpreting metrics

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-objective">

Work backward from the deployment decision:

1. **Action:** What happens after a score—auto-approve, rank, route to a human, or estimate a
   quantity?
2. **Errors and costs:** Is a missed cancer case worse than a biopsy, or is one irrelevant
   recommendation nearly free? Include latency and capacity, not only label error.
3. **Population and unit:** Is the estimand per impression, user, patient, query, or month?
   Correlated rows do not become independent users.
4. **Output contract:** Does the consumer need a ranking, a calibrated probability, a point
   estimate, or a hard decision?

Then select a primary metric and guardrails. A ranking stage with a fixed screen might use
recall@100; a triage queue with limited reviewers might use precision at a fixed daily volume;
a pricing decision needs calibration or expected cost; a forecasting model might use MAE plus
tail-error slices. State the threshold and aggregation explicitly.

A good answer also separates the **training loss**, the **offline selection metric**, and the
**online outcome**. They can differ because a differentiable surrogate such as cross-entropy is
convenient to optimize, while the product may care about recall under a latency constraint.
Validate that improvements in the surrogate predict the downstream outcome rather than assuming
they do.

**Interview trap:** “Use F1 for imbalance” is not a decision analysis. Ask whether precision and
recall are actually equally valuable and why true negatives are excluded.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-confusion">

At one threshold, binary predictions produce four counts. The denominators tell the story:

$$
\mathrm{recall}=\mathrm{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{specificity}=\mathrm{TNR}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}
$$

$$
\mathrm{FPR}=1-\mathrm{specificity}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},\qquad
\mathrm{precision}=\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.
$$

Recall and FPR condition on the **true class**, so a model with fixed class-conditional score
distributions can retain the same ROC curve when prevalence changes. Precision conditions on a
**positive prediction**, so Bayes' rule makes prevalence explicit:

$$
P(y=1\mid \hat y=1)=
\frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi)}.
$$

For example, with TPR $=0.9$, FPR $=0.01$, and prevalence $\pi=0.001$, precision is only
$0.0009/(0.0009+0.00999)=8.26\%$. “One percent false-positive rate” sounds excellent until it
is multiplied by a huge negative population.

In code, protect undefined denominators and report support:

```python
precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else float("nan")
recall = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else float("nan")
```

Silently replacing an undefined precision with zero mixes “the model made no positive calls”
with “every positive call was wrong.”

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-imbalance">

If 0.1% of transactions are fraudulent, predicting “not fraud” for everything obtains 99.9%
accuracy. It has zero detection value. Accuracy is not mathematically broken; its implicit cost
is one unit per misclassified row, so the large negative class dominates.

Use a metric that exposes the intended failure: per-class recall and specificity, precision at
the review capacity, PR curves for rare-positive retrieval, calibrated expected cost, and slices
by amount or fraud type. Always include the majority and simple-rule baselines.

Reweighting the loss or resampling training data does not automatically repair evaluation.
Evaluate on a sample whose prevalence represents deployment, or explicitly reweight to it. If a
case-control test set was balanced artificially, raw precision from that set is not deployment
precision.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-roc-pr">

An ROC curve sweeps thresholds and plots TPR against FPR. A PR curve plots precision against
recall. Davis and Goadrich showed the mathematical relationship between the two spaces and why
interpolation behaves differently <Citation id="davis2006prroc" />.

Prefer ROC when both class-conditional error rates are meaningful, when prevalence may differ
but conditional score distributions are comparable, or when selecting among operating points
across a broad cost range. Prefer PR when the positive class is rare and the consumer directly
cares how many retrieved items are relevant: precision turns the negative-class burden into a
visible quantity.

Two cautions matter in interviews:

- AUROC is the probability that a randomly drawn positive outranks a randomly drawn negative;
  it is not accuracy and does not choose a threshold.
- AUPRC depends on prevalence—the no-skill baseline is $\pi$—so AUPRC values from populations
  with different prevalence are not directly comparable without reweighting or qualification.

Partial AUC or precision/recall at the actual operating constraint is often more honest than a
full-curve area.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-f1">

The harmonic mean

$$
F_1=\frac{2\,\mathrm{precision}\,\mathrm{recall}}
{\mathrm{precision}+\mathrm{recall}}=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}
$$

is low if either precision or recall is low. $F_\beta$ weights recall by $\beta^2$, but even
then the weights are not a calibrated statement of real cost. F1 discards true negatives,
probability quality, ranking beyond one threshold, and absolute scale. It is also
prevalence-dependent through precision.

Use F1 when positive retrieval is the right abstraction, true negatives genuinely carry little
value, and a single operating point is needed. Otherwise show the frontier or minimize expected
cost. A classic trap is comparing F1 after tuning the threshold on the test set; threshold
selection is model selection and belongs on validation data.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-threshold">

Assume a calibrated probability $p=P(y=1\mid x)$, zero cost for correct decisions, false-positive
cost $C_{FP}$, and false-negative cost $C_{FN}$. Predicting positive has expected cost
$C_{FP}(1-p)$; predicting negative costs $C_{FN}p$. Choose positive when

$$
C_{FP}(1-p) < C_{FN}p
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}.
$$

This is why 0.5 is special only when costs are equal and probabilities are calibrated. With
$C_{FN}=9C_{FP}$, the threshold is $0.1$. If reviewers can accept only $k$ cases, the constraint
may instead choose the top $k$ scores, after which precision@k and captured value are natural.

Estimate the threshold on a held-out validation distribution, then lock it before the test.
Stress-test prevalence, delayed labels, cost ranges, and capacity. If probabilities are not
calibrated, directly optimize empirical cost at a threshold but avoid pretending the score is a
probability.

</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="Choose a threshold from expected cost"
  given={[
    { label: '1,000 validation cases', value: '100 positive, 900 negative' },
    { label: 'Threshold A', value: 'TP=90, FN=10, FP=180, TN=720' },
    { label: 'Threshold B', value: 'TP=75, FN=25, FP=45, TN=855' },
    { label: 'Costs', value: 'False negative = 500; false positive = 20' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"

At A, recall is $90/100=0.90$ and precision is $90/270=0.333$. At B, recall is $0.75$ and
precision is $75/120=0.625$. Neither pair alone chooses the threshold.

Apply the stated costs:

$$
\mathrm{cost}_A=10(500)+180(20)=\$8{,}600,
$$

$$
\mathrm{cost}_B=25(500)+45(20)=\$13{,}400.
$$

Despite worse precision, A wins under these costs because 15 additional misses cost far more
than 135 additional reviews. The conclusion changes if capacity forbids 270 alerts or if the
false-positive cost includes customer harm. Report the assumptions with the answer.

</WorkedExample>

## Probabilities, regression, and ranking

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-calibration">

Calibration asks whether probabilities mean what they say:

$$
P(Y=1\mid \hat p=p)=p.
$$

Discrimination asks whether positive examples receive higher scores. Any strictly increasing
transformation preserves ranks and AUROC but changes probabilities. Squaring every predicted
probability, for example, preserves ordering while usually destroying calibration; whether the
result is over- or underconfident depends on the original probabilities and event frequencies.

Reliability diagrams bin predictions and compare mean confidence with empirical frequency, but
the conclusion depends on binning and sample size. Pair them with a proper scoring rule and
uncertainty. Expected calibration error is a useful summary, not a proper scoring rule and not
proof of calibration.

Fit Platt scaling, isotonic regression, or temperature scaling on data not used to train the
base model, and evaluate again on a third split or by cross-fitting. Temperature scaling divides
multiclass logits by a learned scalar and was found to be a strong simple baseline for modern
neural networks <Citation id="guo2017calibration" />. Recalibrate after meaningful distribution
shift; a calibrator cannot make unobserved populations safe.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-proper-scoring">

For binary outcomes,

$$
\mathrm{logloss}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{Brier}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i(p_i-y_i)^2.
$$

A scoring rule is **strictly proper** when the expected score is uniquely optimized by reporting
the forecaster's true belief. Brier's quadratic score dates to probabilistic forecast
verification <Citation id="brier1950" />. Log loss is the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood and
penalizes a confident wrong prediction without bound; clip only inside the numerical
implementation, not to hide poor forecasts. Brier is bounded by one in the binary convention
above and is easier to decompose, but less sensitive to catastrophic confidence.

Neither score tells you the operational threshold. Use them to train or compare probabilities,
then apply decision cost. Class-weighted cross-entropy changes the target risk and often destroys
raw probability calibration unless corrected.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-regression">

For residuals $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$:

$$
\mathrm{MAE}=N^{-1}\sum_i|e_i|,\qquad
\mathrm{MSE}=N^{-1}\sum_i e_i^2,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}.
$$

Minimizing expected squared error predicts the conditional mean; minimizing absolute error
predicts a conditional median. MSE emphasizes large errors and is smooth; MAE is robust to
outliers but nonsmooth at zero. RMSE is in target units but ranks models identically to MSE on
the same observations.

$R^2=1-\sum e_i^2/\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$ compares with the sample-mean baseline. It can be negative
out of sample and cannot compare tasks with different outcome variance. MAPE divides by $|y_i|$,
so zeros are undefined and near-zero targets dominate; symmetric MAPE has its own biases.

Name the deployment cost and distribution. For asymmetric costs use quantile loss; for positive
heavy-tailed targets consider log-space errors but explain that exponentiating a conditional log
mean is not generally the original-scale mean.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-ranking">

Ranking metrics encode where relevance matters:

- precision@$k$ is the relevant fraction among the first $k$; recall@$k$ is the recovered
  fraction of all relevant items;
- reciprocal rank is $1/r$ for the first relevant result, and MRR averages it over queries;
- average precision averages precision at each relevant rank, and MAP averages across queries;
- DCG@$k=\sum_{i=1}^k(2^{rel_i}-1)/\log_2(i+1)$ rewards graded relevance near the top; NDCG
  divides by the ideal DCG for that query <Citation id="jarvelin2002ndcg" />.

Compute per query before averaging so queries, not documents, are the unit. Define what happens
when a query has no relevant items. Report latency, coverage, diversity and calibration alongside
relevance when they affect users.

The hard part is labels: clicks are censored by the old ranker, position, snippet, and exposure.
Randomized interleaving, propensity weighting, and online experiments can help; treating unshown
items as irrelevant trains the logging policy back into the metric.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-averaging">

For $C$ classes, compute a one-versus-rest metric $m_c$ per class. **Macro** averaging is
$C^{-1}\sum_c m_c$ and gives every class equal weight. **Weighted macro** uses class supports.
**Micro** first sums $TP_c,FP_c,FN_c$ across classes, then computes the metric, so frequent
decisions dominate.

In single-label multiclass classification, micro precision, recall, F1, and accuracy coincide.
That surprising fact is a useful interview check: every incorrect prediction contributes one FP
for the predicted class and one FN for the true class.

Use macro when minority classes deserve equal attention, micro when every decision has equal
value, and weighted macro when the deployment mixture is itself the weighting policy. Always show
per-class support and critical slices; an average can hide a class with zero recall.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Validation and uncertainty

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-cross-validation">

Random $k$-fold cross-validation assumes observations are exchangeable and the train/test split
mimics deployment. It fails when the same patient, user, molecule, time window, location, or
duplicate can cross folds. Use group folds for shared entities and temporal forward splits when
the future must be predicted from the past. Fit every learned preprocessing step—including
imputation, vocabulary, scaling, feature selection and calibration—inside each training fold.

If cross-validation selects hyperparameters and estimates their performance on the same folds,
the estimate is optimistically selected. Nested CV uses outer folds for assessment and inner
folds for selection. Kohavi's empirical study remains a foundational comparison of resampling
procedures <Citation id="kohavi1995crossvalidation" />.

```python
for outer_train, outer_test in outer_split(groups):
    # The complete pipeline and hyperparameters are chosen only within outer_train.
    fitted = inner_search(pipeline, X[outer_train], y[outer_train])
    outer_scores.append(metric(y[outer_test], fitted.predict(X[outer_test])))
```

Fold-to-fold standard deviation describes sensitivity to those particular partitions; it is not
automatically a confidence interval because training sets overlap.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-uncertainty">

First identify the independent sampling unit. If predictions from two models are evaluated on
the same users, compare them **paired**: resample users, recompute both metrics, and take the
distribution of the difference. Resampling rows would understate uncertainty when each user has
many correlated rows.

The nonparametric bootstrap samples $N$ units with replacement, fits or reevaluates as required,
and uses the empirical distribution of the statistic <Citation id="efron1979bootstrap" />.
Percentile intervals are easy; BCa or problem-specific analytic intervals may improve coverage.
For rare outcomes or small clusters, stratification or cluster bootstrap matters.

Statistical significance is not practical significance. Report the effect, interval, sample
support, and decision threshold. No resampling method repairs selection bias, label leakage, or a
test distribution unrelated to deployment.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-ab-design">

A defensible A/B plan specifies before exposure:

1. hypothesis and one primary outcome;
2. assignment unit and analysis unit;
3. eligibility, allocation, duration, MDE, power, and stopping rule;
4. guardrails such as latency, errors, complaints, or safety;
5. logging and an intention-to-treat analysis.

Randomize at the unit that can interfere. If a recommendation changes a user's later behavior,
randomize users, not impressions; if marketplace participants affect each other, ordinary
individual randomization may violate no-interference assumptions. After launch, check sample
ratio mismatch and invariant pre-treatment features before reading outcomes.

Online controlled experiments are the strongest way to resolve the causal offline–online gap,
but engineering details and organizational assumptions determine whether they are trustworthy
<Citation id="kohavi2009experiments" />. Do not drop assigned users because they failed to use the
feature: that conditions on a post-treatment variable.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-power">

For equal-size arms comparing means with common variance $\sigma^2$, an approximate two-sided
per-arm size is

$$
n\approx \frac{2\sigma^2(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2}{\delta^2}.
$$

$1-\beta$ is power at true effect $\delta$. With $\alpha=0.05$, power $0.80$, standard deviation
$10$, and MDE $2$, $z_{.975}=1.96$ and $z_{.8}=0.84$ give
$n\approx2(100)(2.8)^2/4=392$ per arm. Halving $\delta$ quadruples $n$.

Use baseline data for variance and conversion rates, inflate for clustering and attrition, and
choose an MDE meaningful to the decision—not the effect the current traffic conveniently detects.
For ratio metrics and heavy tails, simulation or delta-method variance may be more reliable than
plugging row-level variance into this formula. The distinction between type-I and type-II error
follows the classical hypothesis-testing framework of Neyman and Pearson
<Citation id="neymanpearson1933" />.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-multiple-testing">

At level $0.05$, twenty independent null tests have probability
$1-0.95^{20}=0.642$ of at least one false rejection. Model variants, metrics, slices, and repeated
looks all create multiplicity.

Predeclare one primary metric and a limited guardrail family. Bonferroni or Holm controls the
probability of any false rejection; Benjamini–Hochberg orders p-values and controls expected false
discovery proportion under its assumptions <Citation id="benjamini1995fdr" />. Exploratory
results can remain exploratory with adjusted q-values and replication.

Fixed-horizon p-values are invalid under arbitrary repeated peeking. Use a planned alpha-spending,
group-sequential, always-valid, or Bayesian decision procedure whose stopping rule matches the
monitoring. “We stopped when it became significant” is both a statistical and interview red flag.

</InterviewQuestion>

## From offline evaluation to a living system

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-offline-online">

Offline evaluation observes labels under an old data-collection and exposure policy. Deployment
changes actions, which changes future data. A recommender trained on clicks may increase click
through by promoting sensational items while lowering satisfaction; a fraud model may deter the
fraud it is later evaluated against.

Walk through failure categories:

- **proxy:** offline loss does not encode long-term value, diversity, safety, or cost;
- **selection:** only items shown by the old policy receive labels;
- **system:** latency, feature freshness, fallbacks, and quantization change predictions;
- **shift:** prevalence, population, and feedback change;
- **measurement:** online logging or attribution differs from the training definition.

Use temporal and policy-aware validation, replay or counterfactual methods where assumptions hold,
shadow and canary deployment, online randomization, and guardrails. State that an A/B test validates
the full policy, not merely the model weights.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-metrics-slices-drift">

An average can be stable while one subgroup collapses. Choose slices from domain mechanisms and
harms—device, geography, acquisition channel, rare class, data quality, uncertainty—not by mining
hundreds of tiny groups for the worst noise fluctuation. Report numerator, denominator and
uncertainty.

Distinguish:

- covariate drift: $P(X)$ changes;
- label or prior drift: $P(Y)$ changes;
- concept drift: $P(Y\mid X)$ changes;
- instrumentation or policy drift: the measurement process changes.

Population stability, two-sample tests, embedding distances, and prediction distributions can
alert before labels arrive, but they do not say performance worsened. Once delayed labels arrive,
monitor the actual outcome and calibration. Build a response policy—investigate logging, rollback,
recalibrate, retrain, or relabel—rather than a dashboard of unactionable alarms.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Prevalence changes precision" difficulty="warm-up">

A classifier has sensitivity $0.90$ and specificity $0.98$. What precision should you expect at
10% prevalence and at 1% prevalence, assuming those class-conditional rates transfer?

<div slot="solution">

The FPR is $0.02$. Apply Bayes' rule:

$$
\mathrm{PPV}_{10\%}=\frac{0.90(0.10)}{0.90(0.10)+0.02(0.90)}
=\frac{0.09}{0.108}=0.8333.
$$

At 1% prevalence,

$$
\mathrm{PPV}_{1\%}=\frac{0.90(0.01)}{0.90(0.01)+0.02(0.99)}
=\frac{0.009}{0.0288}=0.3125.
$$

The model's TPR and FPR stayed fixed while precision fell from 83.3% to 31.25%. This is why a PR
curve or precision measured on a balanced benchmark does not transfer without the target
prevalence.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Find the leakage-resistant split" difficulty="core">

You have five years of hospital encounters. A patient can appear many times, and the production
model predicts a patient's next admission using all history available that day. Design validation.

<div slot="solution">

Use a forward temporal boundary so every evaluation prediction occurs after its training data.
Within a boundary, keep each prediction's features strictly before its index date and fit
preprocessing only on past training data. Decide the deployment estimand: if production serves
returning patients, the same patient may legitimately appear earlier in training and later in
test, but no future encounter or label may leak backward. If the claim is generalization to new
patients, add a patient-grouped holdout.

Use rolling origins to measure stability across seasons, and bootstrap patients—not encounters—for
uncertainty. A random encounter-level split is invalid because near-identical histories and future
information cross folds.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Size an experiment, then challenge the assumptions" difficulty="stretch">

A continuous primary metric has standard deviation $12$. You want a two-sided 5% test, 80% power,
and MDE $3$. Estimate equal per-arm sample size. What changes if every user generates ten strongly
correlated events?

<div slot="solution">

Using $z_{.975}=1.96$ and $z_{.8}=0.84$,

$$
n\approx\frac{2(12^2)(1.96+0.84)^2}{3^2}
=\frac{288(7.84)}{9}=250.88,
$$

so round up to **251 users per arm** before attrition. Ten events per user do not create ten times
the independent information. Randomize and analyze at user level, or use a cluster-robust variance
and inflate by the design effect $1+(m-1)\rho$. The intraclass correlation $\rho$ must be estimated;
without it, “2,510 events per arm” is not a valid power statement.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Start with the decision, error costs, deployment population, and independent unit.
- Distinguish ranking, probability quality, and thresholded decisions; no one metric substitutes
  for all three.
- Treat splitting, preprocessing, threshold choice, and calibration as one leakage-sensitive
  validation pipeline.
- Report effects with uncertainty and practical meaning, not isolated p-values.
- An online experiment measures the deployed policy; offline metrics are evidence, not the final
  causal answer.
- Monitor slices and drift with an action policy, because aggregate stability can conceal the
  failure that matters.
