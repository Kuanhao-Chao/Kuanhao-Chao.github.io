## The question this lesson answers

Your scan is finished and the QQ plot lifts off the diagonal early. Is that structure, or is
it signal?

The honest answer for most modern studies is *both*, and the interesting question is the
proportion. Getting it wrong in one direction leaves a confounded scan; getting it wrong in
the other throws away real loci to fix a problem the study did not have. This lesson is about
telling them apart and about what each mistake costs.

<Callout variant="intuition">

Structure manufactures association through a mechanism with nothing to do with biology. If
two ancestral groups differ in the frequency of a variant, *and* they differ in the rate of
the phenotype, then that variant is associated with the phenotype in the pooled sample. Both
differences can be entirely incidental — the allele frequency from drift, the phenotype rate
from diet, healthcare access or how the cohort was recruited.

The crucial property is that this is **not noise**. A larger sample estimates the spurious
association more precisely, so structure does not average out — it gets more significant.
Every other confounder in this curriculum behaves the same way, which is why none of them is
a sample-size problem.

</Callout>

## The inflation factor cannot answer the question

The traditional diagnostic <Citation id="devlin1999gc" /> compares the middle of the observed
test-statistic distribution with the middle of the null:

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = \frac{\mathrm{median}(\chi^2_j)}{0.4549364231195727} ,
$$

where the denominator is the exact median of a $\chi^2_1$ variate. The original proposal was
to divide every statistic by $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$, which is where the trouble starts.

The problem is that a **truly polygenic trait inflates every statistic on its own**. Under
the decomposition that LD score regression rests on
<Citation id="bulik2015ldsc" />,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + \frac{N h^2}{M}\,\ell_j ,
$$

the third term grows with $N$ and is nothing to do with confounding: it is the study
correctly detecting a polygenic architecture. So $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rises with sample
size under a perfectly clean analysis, and in a biobank-scale study a $\lambda$ of 1.1 or 1.2
is the expected result of doing everything right. The full derivation belongs to
[LDSC & Stratified LDSC](/deep_dives/statgen-ldsc-sldsc/); what matters here is the
consequence.

**The intercept is the part $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ was being asked for.** Confounding shifts
every statistic regardless of how much LD a variant tags, so it lands in the intercept;
polygenicity scales with the LD score, so it lands in the slope. Reading the intercept
instead of $\lambda$ is the whole diagnostic.

## What genomic control costs when it is not needed

<WorkedExample
  title="A study with λ = 1.18 and an intercept of 1.02"
  given={[
    { label: 'Genomic inflation', value: 'λ_GC = 1.18' },
    { label: 'LDSC intercept', value: '1.02' },
    { label: 'Mean χ²', value: '1.35' },
    { label: 'Threshold', value: 'χ² ≥ 29.7168, i.e. 5 × 10⁻⁸' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — how much of the inflation is real confounding?** The excess mean statistic is
$1.35 - 1 = 0.35$, and the part attributable to the intercept is $1.02 - 1 = 0.02$. So

$$
\frac{0.02}{0.35} = 5.71\% ,
$$

and the other 94% is the study detecting polygenicity. A scan in this state does not need
correcting; it needs reporting.

**Step 2 — what genomic control would do anyway.** Dividing by $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$
removes

$$
1 - \frac{1}{1.18} = 15.25\%
$$

of *every* statistic, against the $1 - 1/1.02 = 1.96\%$ that the confounding justifies. It
is not a small over-correction: it is nearly eight times too large.

**Step 3 — in loci.** A locus at $\chi^2 = 33$ has $p = 9.216 \times 10^{-9}$, comfortably
genome-wide significant. After genomic control it is

$$
\frac{33}{1.18} = 27.9661 \qquad (p = 1.235 \times 10^{-7}),
$$

which no longer clears the threshold. Correcting by the intercept instead gives
$33/1.02 = 32.3529$, still significant. The locus is real either way; only one of the two
corrections reports it.

**Step 4 — the whole band.** Every statistic between

$$
29.7168 \times 1.02 = 30.3111 \quad\text{and}\quad 29.7168 \times 1.18 = 35.0658
$$

is in the same position: genuinely significant, kept by the correct correction, destroyed by
genomic control. That is a window 4.7547 wide in $\chi^2$, corresponding to p-values from
$3.680 \times 10^{-8}$ down to $3.187 \times 10^{-9}$ — precisely the range where a
well-powered study finds most of its new loci.

</WorkedExample>

## The two corrections that work

**Principal components** on the genotype matrix <Citation id="price2006eigenstrat" /> give
axes of ancestry variation that can be included as covariates. Three practical points decide
whether they work:

- **Compute them on LD-pruned variants.** Without pruning the leading components describe
  local LD structure rather than ancestry.
- **Exclude the long-range LD regions** — the MHC and the chromosome 8 inversion are the
  usual offenders. A component that turns out to be one locus is not an ancestry axis.
- **Use 10 PCs**, and say so. The number is a convention rather than a result: it is well
  past the point where components stop describing continental-scale ancestry in a
  single-ancestry cohort, and adding more costs a little power for nothing. A cohort spanning
  several ancestries, or one with fine structure within a country, needs more — and needs to
  justify how many by showing what the extra components capture.

**Mixed models** put a genetic relationship matrix into the model as a random effect
<Citation id="kang2010emmax" />, and modern implementations
<Citation id="loh2015bolt" /><Citation id="mbatchou2021regenie" /> make that affordable at
biobank scale. They absorb structure and relatedness in one step, which is why they have
become the default — a mixed model does not need the relatedness pruning of the previous
lesson, and therefore does not pay its cost.

The trade is **proximal contamination**: the relationship matrix includes the variant being
tested, so the model partly fits the signal away, and power drops
<Citation id="yang2014mlm" />. The implementations that solve it leave the tested
variant's chromosome out of the relationship matrix — BOLT-LMM and REGENIE do this, and GCTA
offers it; EMMAX, cited above, fits one genome-wide matrix and does not, while fastGWA
sidesteps the issue with a sparse relatedness matrix rather than by leaving anything out. If a pipeline does not say
whether it does this, that is worth finding out before interpreting a null result.

<Callout variant="warning" title="What neither correction touches">

Both methods correct for **ancestry as measured by genotype**. Neither corrects for a
phenotype whose *definition* varies with ancestry, for differential participation, or for an
environment that tracks ancestry without being caused by it. A structure correction that
returns an intercept of 1.00 is evidence about genotype-phenotype confounding through
ancestry, and about nothing else <Citation id="astle2009structure" />.

The sharpest demonstration of the limit is that within-family analyses — where the
confounding cannot survive, because siblings share ancestry exactly — recover systematically
smaller effects for some traits than population studies do
<Citation id="brumpton2020withinfamily" />. Whatever produced that difference was not removed
by any number of principal components.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading two studies" difficulty="warm-up">

Study A: $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.31$, LDSC intercept 1.29, mean $\chi^2 = 1.33$.
Study B: $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.31$, LDSC intercept 1.01, mean $\chi^2 = 1.60$.

(a) Which is confounded?

(b) What fraction of each study's inflation is confounding?

(c) What should each do next?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Study A. The two have identical $\lambda$ and completely different diagnoses, which
is the point of the exercise — $\lambda$ carries no information about the split.

**(b)** A: $(1.29 - 1)/(1.33 - 1) = 87.88\%$ of the inflation is confounding. B:
$(1.01 - 1)/(1.60 - 1) = 1.67\%$.

**(c)** Study A should not report these results. Nearly all of its inflation is unmodelled
structure, and the fix is a better correction — more principal components, or a mixed model
— not a division. Study B should report as-is: its inflation is the trait being polygenic
and its scan is clean. Applying genomic control to B would remove $1 - 1/1.31 = 23.66\%$ of
every statistic to address 1.67% of the problem.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="How many loci does the correction cost?" difficulty="core">

A study has $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.25$ and an intercept of 1.03, and 40 loci with
$\chi^2$ between 30 and 40.

(a) Which $\chi^2$ values are destroyed by genomic control but kept by the intercept
correction?

(b) If the 40 loci are spread evenly across 30 to 40, how many fall in that band?

(c) One reviewer asks for genomic control to be applied "to be safe". What is the reply?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The band runs from $29.7168 \times 1.03 = 30.6083$ to
$29.7168 \times 1.25 = 37.1460$.

**(b)** The band spans $37.1460 - 30.6083 = 6.5377$ of the 10-wide interval, so
$40 \times 0.65377 = 26.15$ — about **26 of the 40 loci**, nearly two thirds.

**(c)** That applying it is not the safe choice; it is a choice to trade about 26 real loci
against removing $1 - 1/1.25 = 20\%$ of a $\chi^2$ inflation that the intercept says is 3%
confounding. "To be safe" describes the intercept correction, which is both smaller and
targeted at the thing actually estimated to be wrong. If the reviewer's concern is that the
intercept might be underestimated, the answer is a sensitivity analysis at a larger
intercept, not a correction nearly seven times bigger than any estimate supports — $20\%$ against the $2.91\%$ the intercept warrants.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A clean intercept and a wrong answer" difficulty="stretch">

A study of educational attainment reports an LDSC intercept of 1.00, uses 10 PCs, and finds
hundreds of genome-wide significant loci. A within-family analysis of the same trait recovers
effects roughly half the size.

(a) Does the intercept of 1.00 mean the population estimates are unconfounded?

(b) Name two mechanisms that would produce this pattern and survive every correction in this
lesson.

(c) What would you need to distinguish them?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** No. An intercept of 1.00 says the *test statistics* are not inflated by the kind of
stratification that shifts every variant equally. It does not say the effect estimates are
unbiased. Those are different claims, and the first is routinely reported as though it
established the second.

**(b)** First, **indirect genetic effects**: a parent's genotype affects the child's
environment, so the child's genotype is associated with an environment it did not cause. The
association is real, replicates, and is not what "the effect of this variant" is usually
taken to mean. Second, **assortative mating**, which induces correlations between variants at
unlinked loci and inflates estimates of the genetic contribution without any structure a PC
would see.

**(c)** Family data — specifically, comparing within-sibship estimates against population
estimates for the same variants <Citation id="brumpton2020withinfamily" />. Siblings share
ancestry and share parents, so both mechanisms are removed by the design rather than modelled.
The gap between the two estimates *is* the measurement. No amount of unrelated-individual data
substitutes, which is the real limitation of the correction methods above: they can only
remove what they can see in a genotype matrix.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Structure is not noise.** A larger sample estimates a spurious association more
  precisely, so confounding gets more significant rather than averaging out.
- **$\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ cannot separate confounding from polygenicity**, and rises with
  sample size under a clean analysis. A biobank-scale $\lambda$ of 1.1–1.2 is what doing
  everything right looks like.
- **The LDSC intercept is the diagnostic $\lambda$ was being asked for.** Confounding shifts
  every statistic; polygenicity scales with the LD score.
- **Genomic control on a clean scan is expensive.** At $\lambda = 1.18$ against an intercept
  of 1.02 it removes 15.25% of every statistic instead of 1.96%, and destroys every genuinely
  significant locus between $\chi^2 = 30.3111$ and $35.0658$.
- **Principal components and mixed models are the real corrections.** Compute PCs on
  LD-pruned variants outside long-range LD regions; prefer a mixed model at scale, and check
  it leaves the tested chromosome out of the relationship matrix.
- **Neither corrects what it cannot see in a genotype matrix** — indirect genetic effects,
  assortative mating, or a phenotype defined differently across groups. Family designs, not
  more components, are what address those.
