## The question this lesson answers

Samples processed on different days, by different people, with different reagent lots, differ
systematically. Integration methods remove that difference: mutual nearest neighbours match
cells across datasets and subtract the offset between them
<Citation id="haghverdi2018mnn" />, Harmony iterates between clustering and correcting until
batches are mixed within every cluster <Citation id="korsunsky2019harmony" />, anchor-based transfer aligns one dataset onto another
<Citation id="hao2021seurat" />, and dozens more exist with the same objective
<Citation id="luecken2022benchmark" />.

The objective is always some version of *make the batches indistinguishable*. So the question
that decides whether integration helps is not which method to use. It is whether the batches
ought to be distinguishable — and if the treated samples were run on Tuesday and the controls
on Wednesday, they ought to be, by exactly the amount the experiment was designed to measure.

## A composition table is a confounding table

Take two equal-sized batches, and a cell type that is fraction $f_1$ of the first and $f_2$ of
the second. Code batch as an indicator and cell type as an indicator, and their correlation
works out to

$$
r = |f_1 - f_2|
$$

exactly. Nothing else enters — not the number of cells, not the size of the batch effect. So
the degree to which a design confounds batch with biology is readable straight off the
composition table, and it needs no model to compute.

A 50/50 split gives $r = 0$: batch and type are independent, and removing the batch cannot
touch the type. An 80/20 split gives $r = 0.6$. Every treated sample in one batch and every
control in the other gives $r = 1$.

## What centring costs

Now let the two cell types differ by $\delta$ along the same axis the batch effect acts on —
the worst case, and the one integration is designed for. Per-batch centring subtracts each
batch's own mean. But that mean is

$$
\bar{y}_k = \mu_B + f_k\,\delta + b_k,
$$

which contains $f_k \delta$: a piece of the *biological* signal, present because the batch
happens to hold more of one type than the other. Subtracting it removes that piece.

Working the pooled difference through after centring gives an estimate of

$$
\left(1 - r^2\right)\delta,
$$

so the fraction of the real difference that survives is exactly $1 - r^2$. At an 80/20 split
that is 64%; at 90/10, 36%; at 99/1, 3.96%. The loss is silent — the output is a corrected
matrix with no indication that a third of the effect went into the correction.

## The same bill, presented twice

The obvious objection is that centring is crude, and a proper regression — fitting batch and
biology jointly — handles collinearity correctly. It does. It returns an unbiased estimate of
$\delta$, with no attenuation at all.

What it cannot do is invent precision. The variance of that estimate is multiplied by the
variance inflation factor

$$
\mathrm{VIF} = \frac{1}{1 - r^2},
$$

which is 1.56 at an 80/20 composition, 2.78 at 90/10, 5.26 at 95/5 and 25.25 at 99/1. Since
$(1 - r^2)$ and $1/(1 - r^2)$ are reciprocals, both routes leave exactly $1 - r^2$ of the
information about the effect. Centring spends it on the point estimate; regression spends it on
the standard error. Neither can spend less, because $1 - r^2$ is how much the design contains.

At $r = 1$ this is zero and infinite respectively, which is the same statement twice:
**batch and condition are the same column, and no method separates them.**

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Integration is a claim about what the batches share">

The useful way to hold this is that running an integration method asserts something: that the
cell types present in these batches are the same cell types, in the same states, and that the
differences between batches are technical.

When that assertion is true — an atlas assembled from healthy donors, a technical replicate —
integration is doing exactly what it should, and the benchmarks that rank methods are answering
a real question <Citation id="luecken2022benchmark" />.

When it is false, integration is not a preprocessing step but a hypothesis being imposed on the
data, and the more effective the method the more thoroughly the hypothesis wins. A method that
mixes batches perfectly has, in a confounded design, perfectly erased the finding.

The diagnostic is the composition table, and it costs nothing to look at
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

**Step 1 — the confounding.**

$$
r = |0.8 - 0.2| = 0.6.
$$

**Step 2 — what centring leaves.**

$$
1 - r^2 = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64,
$$

so **64%** of the difference survives and 36% is absorbed into the batch correction. A reported
log fold change of 1.0 was really 1.5625 before the pipeline touched it.

**Step 3 — what regression costs instead.**

$$
\mathrm{VIF} = \frac{1}{0.64} = 1.5625,
$$

so the estimate is correct and its variance is 56% larger — equivalent to having run the study
with 64% of the samples.

**Step 4 — the two are one number.** $0.64 \times 1.5625 = 1$ exactly. The design contains 64%
of the information a balanced one would, and every analysis route spends that same budget
somewhere.

**Step 5 — what would have fixed it.** Not a better method. Splitting each sample across both
batches, which sets $f_1 = f_2$ and $r = 0$, at no cost beyond planning. Where samples are
pooled and hashed this is automatic, because every donor appears in every lane
<Citation id="stoeckius2018hashing" />.

</WorkedExample>

## Where this sits relative to the rest of the track

Two connections are worth making explicit.

**It is the same failure as lesson 13's.** There, one population's change contaminated every
other proportion because the total was unknown. Here, the batch mean contaminates the
biological difference because composition is unbalanced. Both are cases of a quantity being
estimated from data that does not identify it, and in both the remedy is a design change rather
than an analysis choice.

**And it compounds with lesson 12's.** Integration is applied per cell, and the corrected values
are then usually tested per cell. A confounded design therefore attenuates the effect *and*
inflates the false-positive rate at the same time, and the two errors point in opposite
directions — the effect gets smaller while the confidence in it gets larger. Aggregating to one
value per sample before testing addresses the second
<Citation id="crowell2020muscat" />
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />; nothing addresses the first except the bench.

## Summary

- Integration methods optimise **batch indistinguishability**, so they remove biological
  differences between batches whenever those exist.
- For two equal batches, the batch–cell-type correlation is exactly $r = |f_1 - f_2|$: a
  **composition table is a confounding table**, computable with no model.
- Per-batch centring retains exactly $1 - r^2$ of a real difference — **64%** at 80/20, 36% at
  90/10, **3.96%** at 99/1 — and the loss is silent.
- A correct regression keeps the estimate and multiplies its variance by
  $1/(1 - r^2)$: 1.5625 at 80/20, **25.25** at 99/1.
- The two are reciprocals, so the surviving information is $1 - r^2$ either way. **At $r = 1$
  it is zero**, and no method can do better because the design contains nothing to recover.
- The fix is at the bench: split samples across batches so $f_1 = f_2$. Hashing gives it for
  free.

A study has two batches. A cell type of interest makes up 70% of batch 1 and 30% of batch 2.

(a) What is the batch–cell-type correlation?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $r = |0.7 - 0.3| = 0.4$.

(b) $1 - r^2 = 1 - 0.16 = 0.84$, so **84%** survives and 16% is absorbed.

(c) $1/0.84 = 1.1905$, so variance rises about 19%. Note $0.84 \times 1.1905 = 1$ — the same
budget, spent differently.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Two designs, same cells" difficulty="core">

A lab has 8 samples, 4 treated and 4 control, and can run 2 batches of 4.

(a) Design A puts all 4 treated in batch 1. Compute $r$ and the surviving information.

(b) Design B puts 2 treated and 2 control in each batch. Compute the same.

(c) The lab argues design A is simpler to execute and that integration will fix it. Respond.

<div slot="solution">

(a) The treated fraction is 1.0 in batch 1 and 0.0 in batch 2, so $r = |1 - 0| = 1$ and the
surviving information is $1 - 1 = 0$. Nothing about the treatment effect is recoverable.

(b) The treated fraction is 0.5 in both, so $r = 0$ and **100%** survives. Integration removes
the batch effect and leaves the treatment effect untouched, which is exactly what it is for.

(c) Integration cannot fix design A, and the stronger the method the worse the outcome. Batch
and treatment are the same column: any procedure that removes one removes the other, and a
method that mixes the batches perfectly has removed the entire treatment effect while reporting
success on its own objective. The cost of design B is a few minutes of planning, and it moves
the study from zero recoverable information to all of it.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Partial confounding, and what to report" difficulty="stretch">

A dataset has an unavoidable 95/5 imbalance for a rare population.

(a) Compute the surviving information and the variance inflation.

(b) The analysis reports a significant difference in this population after integration. How
should the effect size be interpreted?

(c) What should the paper report so a reader can judge it?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $r = 0.9$, so $1 - r^2 = 0.19$ — **19%** survives — and $\mathrm{VIF} = 5.2632$.

(b) With care, and depending on which route was taken. If the pipeline centred or integrated
per batch, the reported effect is attenuated: the true difference is roughly $1/0.19 = 5.26$
times what was reported, so a modest-looking effect may be large. If the batch was regressed
out properly, the effect size is unbiased but its confidence interval should be about
$\sqrt{5.2632} = 2.294$ times wider than a balanced design would give. The two readings differ
by a factor of five, so a paper that does not say which route it took cannot be interpreted at
all.

(c) The composition table, from which $r$ follows immediately; which correction was applied and
at what stage; and the effect size with an interval that accounts for the inflation. None of
these is expensive, and together they let a reader recompute everything in this lesson. A claim
about a rare population in a confounded design is not wrong by default — it is just weaker than
its p-value suggests, by a factor the reader can compute if given four numbers.

</div>

</Exercise>
