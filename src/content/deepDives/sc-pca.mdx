## The question this lesson answers

Every step so far has been about the counts. From here on the analysis is geometry: cells are
points, distances between them mean similarity, and clusters are regions of high density.
Principal component analysis is where the change happens, and it is run for two reasons at
once — to compress two thousand dimensions into a few dozen, and to denoise, on the argument
that the leading components carry the structure and the trailing ones carry noise.

That argument is right in outline. The question it leaves open is where the boundary sits, and
the near-universal answer — look at the scree plot and find the elbow — does not survive
contact with what a scree plot of pure noise looks like.

## What PCA computes, briefly

Scale each gene to unit variance, form the covariance matrix
$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}/(n-1)$, and take its eigenvectors. The eigenvector
with the largest eigenvalue is the direction along which cells vary most; the eigenvalue is how
much variance lies along it. Because the genes were scaled, the total variance is exactly $p$,
so component $k$ explains $\lambda_k / p$ of it. Standard numerical linear algebra
<Citation id="golub2013matrix" />; nothing single-cell about it yet.

What is single-cell about it is the input. Lesson 5 showed that the transform decides how genes
are weighted, and lesson 6 showed that feature selection decides which genes exist at all. PCA
inherits both. Running it on log1p-transformed data weights mid-expression genes most heavily,
because that is where the log transform's variance is largest — which is a defensible choice
only if it was a choice <Citation id="hafemeister2019sctransform" />
<Citation id="lause2021pearson" />.

## A scree plot of pure noise has an elbow

Here is the fact that makes the elbow unreliable. If you take $n$ observations of $p$
*completely independent* features and compute the covariance eigenvalues, they do not come out
equal. They spread — and the spread has a closed form. As $n, p \to \infty$ with
$\gamma = p/n$ fixed, the eigenvalues fill the **Marchenko–Pastur** interval

$$
\lambda_{\pm} = \sigma^2\left(1 \pm \sqrt{\gamma}\right)^2.
$$

For $n = 400$ and $p = 60$, $\gamma = 0.15$ and the interval is $[0.3754,\ 1.9246]$. A
simulation of exactly that shape, with nothing in it but independent standard normals,
produces a largest eigenvalue of **1.9272** — and a scree plot with a visible elbow, a
shoulder and a long tail. Every feature a scree plot is read for is present, and there is no
structure whatsoever to find.

Plant one real structure — two groups of cells differing in fifteen of the sixty features — and
the picture separates cleanly. The leading eigenvalue becomes **22.43**, which is **11.7×** the
edge, and every remaining component sits below it. The signal is not "the components before
the elbow". It is the components that clear the noise floor, and there is exactly one.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The edge is a floor, not a certificate">

Two honest limits, both of which matter in practice.

**The edge is asymptotic, so the top noise eigenvalue overshoots it.** In the simulation above
it lands at 1.9272 against a predicted 1.9246 — 0.13% over. That fluctuation has its own
limiting law <Citation id="johnstone2001largest" />, and the practical consequence is that
"above the edge" is not a test. "Well above the edge" is; a component at 1.05 times the edge is
noise, and one at 11.7 times is not.

**Marchenko–Pastur describes independent features, and genes are not independent.** Genes in a
pathway covary for reasons that have nothing to do with cell type, so the real noise floor for
expression data sits *above* the MP edge, not at it. The edge is therefore a lower bound on
where noise stops — useful for rejecting components, not for accepting them.

The empirical version of the same idea avoids both caveats by construction: permute each gene's
values across cells independently, which destroys between-gene structure while preserving each
gene's own distribution exactly, recompute the eigenvalues, and keep the components that exceed
the largest permuted one. That is parallel analysis <Citation id="buja1992parallel" />, it costs
a handful of extra decompositions, and it is the right answer when the closed form's assumptions
are in doubt.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Where the noise floor sits for a real dataset"
  given={[
    '5,000 cells and 2,000 highly variable genes',
    'Each gene scaled to unit variance',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the aspect ratio.**

$$
\gamma = \frac{p}{n} = \frac{2{,}000}{5{,}000} = 0.4,
\qquad
\sqrt{\gamma} = 0.632456.
$$

**Step 2 — the edges.**

$$
\lambda_{+} = (1 + 0.632456)^2 = 2.664911,
\qquad
\lambda_{-} = (1 - 0.632456)^2 = 0.135089.
$$

So a component whose eigenvalue is below **2.6649** is consistent with carrying nothing.

**Step 3 — in the units people actually report.** Total variance is $p = 2{,}000$, so the edge
corresponds to

$$
\frac{2.664911}{2{,}000} = 0.1332\%
$$

of the variance. A component explaining a tenth of a percent is exactly at the noise floor —
and a scree plot's y-axis, which is usually "percent variance explained", gives no hint of that.

**Step 4 — the same calculation at other cell counts.** With 2,000 genes fixed:

- 500 cells: $\gamma = 4$, $\lambda_{+} = 9.0000$, which is **0.450%** of variance.
- 5,000 cells: $\lambda_{+} = 2.6649$, **0.1332%**.
- 20,000 cells: $\gamma = 0.1$, $\lambda_{+} = 1.7325$, **0.0866%**.

The floor falls by a factor of **5.19** from the smallest dataset to the largest.

**What this rules out.** Any rule of the form "keep components explaining more than *x*% of
variance", transferred between datasets. At 500 cells such a rule set at 1% is conservative; at
20,000 cells the same rule discards everything between 0.0866% and 1%, which is where a real but
modest structure in a large dataset would sit. The rule has to move with $\gamma$, and $\gamma$
is a property of the experiment, not of the biology.

</WorkedExample>

## What to do in practice

Three things, in order of how much they cost.

**Compute the edge.** It is one line, it needs only the matrix shape, and it immediately rules
out the components that cannot be real. Most pipelines default to 50 components; for many
datasets a substantial fraction of those are below the floor.

**Run parallel analysis if the answer matters.** Permuting within genes and re-decomposing gives
an empirical floor that accounts for the between-gene correlation MP ignores
<Citation id="buja1992parallel" />.

**Check that the downstream result survives the choice.** This is the one that actually protects
you. Re-run the clustering at 15, 30 and 50 components; if the populations you intend to report
appear at all three, the choice was not load-bearing. If they appear only at one, that is a
result about the component count <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

It is also worth knowing that the question can be avoided. Model-based dimension reductions fit
a count likelihood directly and select dimensionality by the same criterion they fit with, which
sidesteps both the transform and the scree plot <Citation id="townes2019glmpca" />. The reason
PCA-on-scaled-log-counts remains standard is speed and the ecosystem built on it
<Citation id="stuart2019seurat3" />, not superior statistics.

## Summary

- PCA turns the count matrix into geometry, and inherits every decision from lessons 5 and 6:
  the transform sets the gene weighting, feature selection sets the gene set.
- **A pure-noise matrix has a scree plot with an elbow.** Its eigenvalues fill the
  Marchenko–Pastur interval $\sigma^2(1 \pm \sqrt{\gamma})^2$; at $n = 400$, $p = 60$ the top
  one is **1.9272** against a predicted edge of **1.9246**, carrying 3.22% of the variance.
- One planted structure gives **22.43**, **11.7×** the edge, with nothing else above it. Signal
  is what clears the floor, not what precedes the elbow.
- **The edge is a floor, not a certificate.** It overshoots slightly at finite size, and genes
  are not independent, so the true noise floor is higher. Parallel analysis measures it
  empirically.
- **The floor depends on the matrix shape.** With 2,000 genes it is 0.450% of variance at 500
  cells and 0.0866% at 20,000 — a factor of **5.19** — so fixed percent-of-variance rules do not
  transfer between datasets.
- The reliable check is whether the downstream result survives the choice.

<Exercise n={1} title="Computing a noise floor" difficulty="warm-up">

A dataset has 1,000 cells and 3,000 highly variable genes, each scaled to unit variance.

(a) Compute $\gamma$ and both Marchenko–Pastur edges.

(b) What fraction of the total variance does the upper edge correspond to?

(c) The pipeline kept 50 components. What does the edge tell you about that choice?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\gamma = 3{,}000/1{,}000 = 3$, so $\sqrt{\gamma} = 1.732051$ and

$$
\lambda_{+} = (1 + 1.732051)^2 = 7.464102,
\qquad
\lambda_{-} = (1 - 1.732051)^2 = 0.535898.
$$

(b) Total variance is 3,000, so the edge is $7.464102/3{,}000 = 0.2488\%$.

(c) Nothing directly — the edge constrains eigenvalues, not counts. What it says is that any of
those 50 components whose eigenvalue falls below 7.4641 is indistinguishable from noise, and
with $\gamma = 3$ the noise interval is wide, so a large share of them probably are. The check
is to compute the eigenvalues and count how many clear 7.4641; keeping 50 regardless is the
part that has no justification.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Why the rule cannot be transferred" difficulty="core">

A method paper reports that keeping components explaining more than 0.5% of variance worked
well on their data of 800 cells and 2,000 genes.

(a) Compute their noise floor as a percentage of variance.

(b) You have 30,000 cells and the same 2,000 genes. Compute yours.

(c) What happens if you apply their rule to your data?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\gamma = 2{,}000/800 = 2.5$, $\lambda_{+} = (1 + 1.581139)^2 = 6.662278$, which over a
total variance of 2,000 is **0.3331%**.

(b) $\gamma = 2{,}000/30{,}000 = 0.066667$, $\lambda_{+} = (1 + 0.258199)^2 = 1.583064$, which
is **0.0792%**.

(c) Their 0.5% rule sits just above their own floor of 0.333%, so on their data it is a
reasonable, slightly conservative cut. On yours the floor is 0.0792%, and a 0.5% rule discards
everything between 0.0792% and 0.5% — a range that on 30,000 cells comfortably contains real
populations, because a rare cell type contributes variance in proportion to its abundance. The
rule was never about 0.5%; it was about their $\gamma$, and $\gamma$ changed by a factor of
**37.5**.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="What the edge cannot tell you" difficulty="stretch">

A component has an eigenvalue of 1.03 times the Marchenko–Pastur upper edge.

(a) Is it signal?

(b) A colleague proposes running parallel analysis instead. What would that change, and in
which direction would you expect the threshold to move?

(c) Give a case where a real, important biological structure produces an eigenvalue *below* the
edge, and say what would find it.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Almost certainly not. The edge is the asymptotic limit and the largest noise eigenvalue
fluctuates above it at finite size — 0.13% over in the lesson's simulation, and the overshoot
grows as the matrix gets smaller. A ratio of 1.03 is well inside that fluctuation band, so the
component is consistent with noise. This is why the usable rule is "well above", not "above".

(b) Parallel analysis permutes each gene independently across cells, which destroys correlation
*between* genes while preserving each gene's own marginal distribution exactly. Since real genes
are positively correlated in pathways and that correlation inflates the leading eigenvalues, the
permuted floor should come out **higher** than the MP edge. So the threshold moves up and the
component in (a) is rejected more firmly.

(c) A cell type at 0.5% abundance that differs in a modest number of genes. Its contribution to
total variance scales with the product of its abundance and the squared effect, so a rare
population produces a small eigenvalue however distinct it is — the variance is an average over
all cells, and 99.5% of them are unaffected. Nothing in a global eigenvalue spectrum recovers
it. What finds it is a method that does not average over all cells: a neighbour graph, which
only ever compares a cell to its own locality, and which is the subject of the next lesson.

</div>

</Exercise>
