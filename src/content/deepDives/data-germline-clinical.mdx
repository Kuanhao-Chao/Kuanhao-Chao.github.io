## The question this page answers

Every layer so far produced a measurement. This one produces a **conclusion** — somebody
looked at the evidence and wrote down what they thought.

That makes it the most immediately useful layer and the easiest to misuse, because a
conclusion looks like a fact and is not one. It is a fact about what a submitter concluded,
on the evidence they had, under the framework they applied.

<Callout variant="intuition">
The distinction that matters here is between a *measurement* and an *assertion*.

gnomAD reports what was observed. A multiplexed assay reports what happened in cells.
ClinVar reports what a laboratory decided — and two laboratories looking at the same variant
can decide differently, which is why ClinVar records conflicts rather than resolving them.

So the question to ask of any record in this layer is not "what does it say" but **"who said
it, on what evidence, and did anyone check?"** ClinVar answers that last part with a review
status, and the distribution of review statuses is the single most useful thing to know about
the resource: of 4.3 million classified variants, about 22,000 have been through an expert
panel. That is roughly one in 192.
</Callout>

## Six resources, six different questions

**<Dataset id="clinvar" />** archives submitted assertions <Citation id="landrum2024clinvar" />.
It is deliberately *not* a curator: it accepts conflicting submissions and displays them side
by side, and its review status records how much scrutiny each record received rather than how
correct it is.

**<Dataset id="clingen" />** does the curation ClinVar does not
<Citation id="rehm2015clingen" />. Three outputs matter. **Gene–disease validity** asks whether
a gene causes a disease at all, on a scale from Definitive to Refuted — a question that has to
be settled before any variant in that gene can be called pathogenic *for* it. **Dosage
sensitivity** scores whether losing or gaining a copy causes disease, which is what makes a
CNV interpretable <Citation id="riggs2020dosage" />. And **gene-specific rule specifications**
turn the general ACMG criteria into concrete thresholds for a particular gene.

**<Dataset id="omim" />** is the phenotype catalogue <Citation id="amberger2019omim" />: what
disorders exist, what genes cause them, and the narrative of how that was established. It
answers "what is this disease" rather than "is this variant causal".

**<Dataset id="hgmd" />** curates published disease-associated variants from the literature
<Citation id="stenson2020hgmd" />. Its inclusion criterion is *published association*, not
verified causality, and the professional release sits behind a subscription while the public
one lags by years. Both facts matter more than its reputation suggests.

**<Dataset id="decipher" />** shares consented patient-level data — structural variants and
sequence variants with phenotype attached <Citation id="firth2009decipher" />, which is what
makes it useful for the CNV cases the other resources handle poorly.

**<Dataset id="pharmgkb" />** answers a different question entirely
<Citation id="whirlcarrillo2021pharmgkb" />: not whether a variant causes disease but whether
it changes how a drug should be dosed. Its evidence levels are its own scale and do not
translate into ACMG tiers.

<Callout variant="warning" title="A ClinVar label is not evidence you may add to your own">
The framework has a criterion, PP5, for "reputable source reports pathogenic". Using it means
counting somebody else's conclusion as a piece of your evidence — and their conclusion was
itself built from evidence you are probably also counting.

That is circular, and it inflates. The 2015 guidelines included PP5 and BP6; subsequent work
recommended against using them precisely because the evidence behind a submitted classification
is usually the same evidence the reader has already tallied <Citation id="tavtigian2020" />.
Exercise 3 prices the error.

The right use of a ClinVar record is as a **pointer to evidence**: open the submission, read
what it cites, and count *that* — once.
</Callout>

## Combining evidence into a call

The ACMG/AMP framework <Citation id="richards2015" /> assigns each criterion a strength, and
the Bayesian reading <Citation id="tavtigian2018" /> makes those strengths points on one scale:
supporting 1, moderate 2, strong 4, very strong 8, with benign criteria the same magnitudes
negative. The accumulated points give an odds, and the odds updates a prior:

$$
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{\text{points}/8},
\qquad
\text{posterior} = \frac{\text{OddsPath}\cdot\pi_0}{(\text{OddsPath}-1)\,\pi_0 + 1}
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="A BRCA1 missense variant, criterion by criterion"
  given={[
    { label: 'PS3 — functional assay abnormal', value: 'strong, +4' },
    { label: 'PM1 — critical functional domain', value: 'moderate, +2' },
    { label: 'PM2_Supporting — absent from gnomAD', value: 'supporting, +1' },
    { label: 'PP3 — computational evidence', value: 'supporting, +1' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

The variant is BRCA1 c.181T>G, p.Cys61Gly — the one this track has been following. Cys61
coordinates zinc in the RING domain, saturation genome editing scores it non-functional, and
it is absent from population reference data.

**Step 1 — total the points.**

$$
4 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 8 \text{ points}
$$

**Step 2 — convert to odds.** Eight points is one very strong criterion's worth of evidence,
by construction:

$$
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{8/8} = 350
$$

**Step 3 — update the prior.**

$$
\text{posterior} = \frac{350 \times 0.1}{(350 - 1)\times 0.1 + 1} = \frac{35}{35.9} = 0.9749
$$

**Step 4 — read the tier.** The thresholds are 0.99 for Pathogenic and 0.90 for Likely
pathogenic, which correspond to 10 and 6 points. At 8 points and a posterior of 0.9749 the
call is **Likely pathogenic** — not Pathogenic.

**Step 5 — see what the last two points would take.** Reaching 10 needs another moderate
criterion or two supporting ones: segregation data, a second independent functional assay, or
case–control enrichment. What it does *not* legitimately include is the observation that
somebody else already called it pathogenic.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="key" title="The point thresholds encode a prior of 0.1">
Six points is "likely pathogenic" only because $\pi_0 = 0.1$. The threshold is a posterior of
0.90, and the points needed to reach it depend entirely on where you started.

Hold the evidence at 8 points and vary the prior:

$$
\pi_0 = 0.1 \Rightarrow 0.9749, \qquad
\pi_0 = 0.03 \Rightarrow 0.9154, \qquad
\pi_0 = 0.01 \Rightarrow 0.7795
$$

The same eight points is Likely pathogenic in the first two cases and **Uncertain** in the
third. A prior of 0.1 is reasonable for a variant already filtered to a disease-relevant gene
in an affected patient; it is much too high for a variant pulled at random from a sequencing
run, and gene-specific specifications exist partly to adjust for this.

The lesson is that the point tally is not the answer. It is the answer *given a starting
point*, and the starting point is an assumption you are making whether or not you notice.
</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="One strong criterion is not enough" difficulty="warm-up">

A variant has exactly one strong criterion in its favour and nothing else — 4 points. What is
the posterior, and what is the call?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{4/8} = 18.7083,
\qquad
\text{posterior} = \frac{18.7083 \times 0.1}{17.7083 \times 0.1 + 1} = 0.6752
$$

Four points is below the six needed for Likely pathogenic, so the call is **Uncertain
significance** — a VUS.

This surprises people, and it should not. A single strong criterion moves the odds by a
factor of about nineteen, which takes a one-in-ten prior to roughly two-in-three. Two thirds
is not a basis for clinical action, and the framework is right to say so. Most variants that
feel "probably pathogenic" on one good piece of evidence are exactly here.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Evidence pointing both ways" difficulty="core">

A functional assay says the variant behaves normally — BS3, strong benign, −4 points — but the
computational predictors call it damaging, PP3, +1. What is the call?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{points} = -4 + 1 = -3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{-3/8} = 0.1112
$$

$$
\text{posterior} = \frac{0.1112 \times 0.1}{(0.1112 - 1) \times 0.1 + 1} = 0.0122
$$

**Likely benign.** Benign and pathogenic evidence combine on one scale, so they genuinely
cancel — and here the strong functional result outweighs the supporting computational one by
four points to one.

Two cautions. First, this is only correct if the assay actually interrogates a function
relevant to the disease; the previous layer's warning applies, and a normal result in an assay
that cannot see the relevant mechanism is not BS3 at all. Second, a large positive tally and a
large negative tally that happen to sum near zero should be reported as *conflicting*, not
quietly averaged into a VUS — the arithmetic cannot tell the difference and a curator must.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The cost of counting a conclusion" difficulty="stretch">

A variant has been tallied at 9 points — Likely pathogenic. The curator then notes that a
ClinVar submitter has already classified it pathogenic, and adds PP5 for +1. What changes?

<div slot="solution">

$$
9 \text{ points} \Rightarrow \text{posterior } 0.9878, \qquad
10 \text{ points} \Rightarrow \text{posterior } 0.9941
$$

The threshold for Pathogenic is 10 points, so the variant crosses from **Likely pathogenic to
Pathogenic** on the strength of one supporting criterion.

Why that is not legitimate: the submitter's classification was built from evidence — very
likely the same population data, the same functional assay and the same computational
predictions the curator has already counted. Adding PP5 counts that evidence a second time and
calls the repetition corroboration.

The consequence is not academic. Pathogenic and Likely pathogenic are reported differently and
can be actioned differently, so a tier flip driven by circular evidence changes what happens to
a patient. This is why PP5 and its benign counterpart BP6 were recommended against
<Citation id="tavtigian2020" />, and why the useful move is to open the submission and count
what it cites rather than counting that it exists.

Note the asymmetry with Exercise 2: real conflicting evidence *reduces* certainty, while
circular agreement inflates it. Only one of those failure modes announces itself.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **This layer records conclusions, not measurements.** The question to ask is who concluded
  it, on what evidence, and whether anyone checked.
- **ClinVar archives assertions and displays conflicts** rather than resolving them. Of
  4,302,878 classified variation records, 22,402 have expert-panel review — about one in 192 —
  and 663 have a practice guideline behind them.
- **ClinGen does the curation ClinVar does not**: whether the gene causes the disease at all,
  whether dosage matters, and what the ACMG criteria mean for that specific gene.
- **The other four answer other questions.** OMIM catalogues phenotypes; HGMD curates published
  associations rather than verified causality and is subscription-gated; DECIPHER shares
  patient-level data with phenotype; PharmGKB is about dosing, on its own evidence scale.
- **Points combine into a posterior**, $350^{\text{points}/8}$ applied to a prior. BRCA1
  c.181T>G at 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 8 points gives 0.9749 — Likely pathogenic, and not Pathogenic.
- **The thresholds encode $\pi_0 = 0.1$.** The same 8 points is 0.9154 at a prior of 0.03 and
  0.7795 — Uncertain — at 0.01. The starting point is an assumption whether or not you notice
  it.
- **Do not count someone else's conclusion as your evidence.** Adding PP5 to a 9-point tally
  flips Likely pathogenic to Pathogenic on evidence already counted once.
