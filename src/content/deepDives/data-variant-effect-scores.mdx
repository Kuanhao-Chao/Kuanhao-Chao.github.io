## The question this page answers

The variant is missense, absent from population databases, in a constrained gene. Every
predictor you can query returns a number: CADD 24.7, REVEL 0.71, AlphaMissense "likely
pathogenic".

None of that is yet evidence. Evidence is a quantity — how much these numbers should move
your belief — and getting from a score to that quantity requires two things the score does
not carry: what it was trained to separate, and how often it is wrong at the threshold you
picked.

<Callout variant="intuition">
A predictor gives you a **ranking**. A classification framework needs a **likelihood ratio**.
Those are different objects, and almost every misuse of these scores is a conversion error
between them.

The ranking question is "does this score put pathogenic variants above benign ones?" — and
the area under the ROC curve answers it, across every possible threshold at once. The
evidence question is "at the one threshold I actually used, how much more often does a
positive call happen on a pathogenic variant than on a benign one?" A predictor can be
excellent at the first and nearly useless at the second, because you chose a bad place to
cut.

The worked example below is exactly that case: an AUC of 0.909, and a threshold worth the
weakest evidence tier the framework has.
</Callout>

## What each score was trained to separate

This is the question that decides what a score means, and it is answered differently by
every resource in the table.

**<Dataset id="cadd" version />** was never trained on pathogenic variants at all. It
separates variants that became fixed in the human lineage from simulated ones matched for
mutation rate <Citation id="kircher2014cadd" />. What it learns is **depletion** — which
changes selection has been removing — and deleteriousness is a proxy read off that. Its
PHRED scaling is a *rank*: 20 means "in the top 1% of all possible SNVs", which is a
statement about the genome, not about your variant's probability of causing disease
<Citation id="rentzsch2019cadd" />.

**<Dataset id="revel" />** is a supervised ensemble over thirteen component predictors
<Citation id="ioannidis2016revel" />. It performs well, and it inherits everything its
components were trained on.

**<Dataset id="alphamissense" />** is structure-informed and trained without human clinical
labels <Citation id="cheng2023alphamissense" />, which is what lets it speak about genes no
clinical laboratory has ever submitted a variant for.

**<Dataset id="spliceai" />** answers a different question entirely: not "is this protein
change damaging" but "does this sequence change where the spliceosome cuts"
<Citation id="jaganathan2019spliceai" />. It works on deep intronic variants no protein-level
predictor can see. Its delta scores are precomputed only within a window around the variant;
outside it the model has to be run, and the two are not interchangeable.

**<Dataset id="esm1b-variants" />** scores zero-shot: the log-likelihood ratio a protein
language model assigns to the alternate residue against the reference, with no variant
labels used anywhere in training <Citation id="brandes2023esm1b" />.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Several of these are not independent of ClinVar, or of each other">
Supervised predictors are trained on labelled variants, and the labels overwhelmingly come
from ClinVar and HGMD. Two consequences follow, and both are routinely ignored.

**Evaluating on ClinVar overstates.** A test set drawn from the same resource the training
labels came from measures memorisation as much as generalisation. This is why benchmark
suites go to such lengths over splits, and why the unsupervised scores are worth having
even when they rank slightly worse.

**Stacking them double-counts.** REVEL is an ensemble *of* other predictors; using REVEL and
one of its components as two lines of evidence counts the same signal twice. The framework
<Citation id="richards2015" /> has one computational criterion, PP3, and treats it as one
piece of evidence for exactly this reason. Exercise 3 puts a number on what ignoring that
costs.

The scores genuinely independent of clinical labels — AlphaMissense, ESM-1b, SpliceAI — are
valuable partly *because* of that independence, not only because of their accuracy.
</Callout>

## From a score to an amount of evidence

The ACMG/AMP framework is Bayesian underneath <Citation id="tavtigian2018" />: each criterion
carries an odds of pathogenicity, and the tiers are powers of a single constant. One very
strong criterion is $\text{OddsPath} = 350$, and every other tier is a root of it —
$350^{1/2} = 18.71$ for strong, $350^{1/4} = 4.33$ for moderate, $350^{1/8} = 2.08$ for
supporting.

A predictor supplies that odds directly. At a chosen threshold it has some sensitivity and
some false positive rate, and

$$
\text{OddsPath} \;=\; \text{LR}^{+} \;=\; \frac{\text{TPR}}{\text{FPR}}
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="An impressive AUC worth the weakest tier"
  given={[
    { label: 'Sensitivity at the threshold', value: 'TPR = 0.88' },
    { label: 'False positive rate', value: 'FPR = 0.28' },
    { label: 'Curve through that point', value: 'AUC = 0.909' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the likelihood ratio.**

$$
\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{0.88}{0.28} = 3.1429
$$

**Step 2 — convert to points.** Points are the logarithm of the odds in base $350^{1/8}$:

$$
\text{points} = 8\,\frac{\ln 3.1429}{\ln 350} = 8 \times \frac{1.1451}{5.8579} = 1.5639
$$

**Step 3 — read off the tier.** The tiers sit at 1, 2, 4 and 8 points. At 1.5639 the
threshold has cleared **supporting** and not reached moderate, so it earns *supporting* —
the weakest tier the framework defines.

**Step 4 — put that beside the AUC.** The ROC curve through this operating point has an area
of $0.909$. By the usual reading that is a strong predictor, and it is: it ranks well. But
ranking well across all thresholds and generating evidence at *this* threshold are different
claims, and only the second one is what a classification needs.

**Step 5 — see where the loss is.** Nothing is wrong with the predictor. The threshold is
letting through 28% of benign variants, and a call that fires on more than a quarter of
benign variants cannot be worth much however often it also fires on pathogenic ones.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample
  title="What it would take to reach strong"
  given={[
    { label: 'Target', value: 'strong evidence, OddsPath ≥ 18.708' },
    { label: 'Sensitivity held at', value: 'TPR = 0.88' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — invert the ratio.** Strong requires $\text{TPR}/\text{FPR} \ge 18.708$, so

$$
\text{FPR} \le \frac{0.88}{18.708} = 0.04704
$$

**Step 2 — read it as specificity.**

$$
\text{specificity} \ge 1 - 0.04704 = 0.95296
$$

The threshold must reject better than **95.3%** of benign variants while still catching 88%
of pathogenic ones. That is a far harder requirement than the original threshold met, and it
is why computational evidence at strong is rare.

**Step 3 — notice what sensitivity cannot buy.** Hold the false positive rate at its original
$0.28$ and ask what sensitivity would reach strong:

$$
\text{TPR} \ge 18.708 \times 0.28 = 5.238
$$

which is impossible, since a rate cannot exceed 1. **At a 28% false positive rate, strong
evidence is unreachable at any sensitivity whatsoever.** Evidence strength is bought with
specificity; sensitivity only decides how often you get to use it.

</WorkedExample>

## Calibration

Published default thresholds — CADD above 20, REVEL above 0.5 — were not chosen to deliver a
stated amount of evidence. They were chosen as reasonable operating points, and the amount of
evidence they happen to carry was nobody's design parameter.

Calibration fixes that by working backwards <Citation id="pejaver2022" />: take a set of
variants with known classification, find the score thresholds at which the observed
likelihood ratio equals each tier's required OddsPath, and publish *those* thresholds with
the tier attached. The result is usually sobering — the thresholds that deliver strong
evidence are much more extreme than the defaults, and for several tools no threshold reaches
strong at all.

The practical consequence is that a calibrated tool reports a strength, not a score, and the
strength is what enters the classification.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A sensitive threshold worth nothing" difficulty="warm-up">

A predictor is run at a permissive threshold: it catches 95% of pathogenic variants but also
calls 60% of benign ones. What evidence strength does it supply?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{0.95}{0.60} = 1.5833
$$

$$
\text{points} = 8\,\frac{\ln 1.5833}{\ln 350} = 0.6276
$$

Below one point, so it reaches **no tier at all** — not even supporting. A call that fires on
three benign variants for every five pathogenic ones barely moves the odds, and the framework
correctly assigns it nothing.

The lesson is that sensitivity alone is worthless as evidence. A threshold that flags almost
everything catches almost every pathogenic variant by construction, and tells you nothing when
it does.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="One predictor, two verdicts" difficulty="core">

A predictor has AUC 0.95 — better than the one in the worked example. Its ROC curve has the
form $\text{TPR} = \text{FPR}^{a}$, for which $\text{AUC} = 1/(1+a)$. Compute the evidence
strength at a threshold with FPR 0.01, and at one with FPR 0.50.

<div slot="solution">

From $\text{AUC} = 1/(1+a) = 0.95$ we get $a = 1/0.95 - 1 = 0.052632$.

**At FPR = 0.01:**

$$
\text{TPR} = 0.01^{0.052632} = 0.7848,
\qquad
\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{0.7848}{0.01} = 78.48
$$

which is $5.958$ points — comfortably **strong**.

**At FPR = 0.50:**

$$
\text{TPR} = 0.50^{0.052632} = 0.9642,
\qquad
\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{0.9642}{0.50} = 1.9284
$$

which is $0.897$ points — **no tier at all**.

The same predictor, the same ROC curve, the same AUC of 0.95, and two thresholds that differ
by the entire range the framework can express. This is the concrete form of the claim that
AUC does not determine evidence strength: it is a property of the curve, and evidence is a
property of the point you stand on.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Counting the same evidence twice" difficulty="stretch">

Two predictors each supply supporting evidence for the same variant. Treated as independent,
they combine to 2 points. If they are in fact so correlated that the second adds nothing, only
1 point is real. Starting from a prior of 0.1, what posterior does each give, and how large is
the error?

<div slot="solution">

The posterior from a point tally is $\text{OddsPath} = 350^{\text{points}/8}$ applied to the
prior odds:

**Treated as independent, 2 points:**

$$
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{1/4} = 4.3253
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{posterior} = 0.3246
$$

**Counting once, 1 point:**

$$
\text{OddsPath} = 350^{1/8} = 2.0797
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{posterior} = 0.1877
$$

The double-count nearly doubles the posterior, from 0.1877 to 0.3246 — a $72.9\%$ relative
inflation from a mistake that looks like diligence.

**Why it happens.** REVEL is an ensemble over thirteen predictors; several of those thirteen
are themselves available separately. Running "two tools" can mean running one tool and a
weighted average that contains it. The framework's single PP3 criterion exists precisely to
stop this, and the discipline is to ask not "how many tools agree" but "how many *independent*
lines of evidence are there" — which, for supervised predictors trained on the same labels, is
usually one.

Note the direction: this error always inflates, never deflates, because agreeing predictors
are counted as corroboration when they are actually the same measurement repeated.

## Summary

- **What a score was trained to separate decides what it means.** CADD learns depletion from
  simulated versus fixed variants and reports a genome-wide rank; REVEL is a supervised
  ensemble; AlphaMissense and ESM-1b use no clinical labels; SpliceAI answers a different
  question altogether.
- **Evidence is a likelihood ratio at one threshold**, $\text{LR}^{+} = \text{TPR}/\text{FPR}$,
  and the ACMG tiers are powers of 350: supporting 2.08, moderate 4.33, strong 18.71, very
  strong 350.
- **AUC does not determine evidence strength.** A curve of area 0.909 supplied only
  *supporting* evidence at its threshold, and a curve of area 0.95 supplied *strong* at one
  threshold and *nothing* at another.
- **Specificity buys evidence; sensitivity buys opportunity.** Reaching strong at 88%
  sensitivity needs 95.3% specificity — and at a 28% false positive rate, strong is
  unreachable at any sensitivity at all.
- **Supervised predictors trained on ClinVar are not independent of ClinVar, or of each
  other.** Stacking two correlated tools as two supporting criteria inflated the posterior
  from 0.1877 to 0.3246 in the exercise above — a 72.9% overstatement.
- **Calibration turns a score into a strength** by finding the threshold at which the observed
  likelihood ratio equals a tier's requirement, rather than inheriting a default nobody chose
  for that purpose.
