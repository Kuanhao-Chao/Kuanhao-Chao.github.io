## The question this page answers

A variant is not in gnomAD. Is it rare?

The question sounds like a lookup and is not one. "Not in gnomAD" could mean the variant is
genuinely vanishingly rare, or that the position was poorly covered, or that nobody of the
relevant ancestry was sequenced, or that it was filtered out for a quality reason and is
sitting in the data under a flag you did not read.

This layer is where variant interpretation gets its most powerful single piece of evidence —
and where that evidence is most often over-read.

<Callout variant="intuition">
An allele frequency is a fraction, and the interesting number is underneath the line.

The numerator, AC, is what you saw. The denominator, AN, is how hard you looked. A count of
zero out of five thousand and a count of zero out of two million are the same numerator and
completely different statements — the first excludes almost nothing, the second excludes
almost everything.

So the reflex worth building is: **never read an AF without reading its AN.** A frequency
computed on a small denominator is not really a frequency. It is an upper bound wearing a
point estimate's clothes.
</Callout>

## The resources

**<Dataset id="gnomad" version />** is the reference by default, and the reason is the
denominator: 807,162 individuals is enough AN to make "absent" mean something. The lineage
runs from ExAC's 60,706 exomes <Citation id="lek2016exac" /> through gnomAD v2's 141,456
<Citation id="karczewski2020" /> to here — an order of magnitude roughly every five years,
and each one moved the frequency floor down with it. Two features
of v4 matter in practice.

*Joint call sets.* Exomes and genomes are called separately and reported together, so a
site covered in one and not the other does not silently lose callability — the AN reflects
who was actually genotyped there, not who was in the cohort <Citation id="chen2024constraint" />.

*The non-UK-Biobank subset.* gnomAD v4 incorporates UK Biobank exomes. If you evaluate a
model or a polygenic score on UK Biobank and filter with gnomAD, the same samples are on
both sides. The subset exists to break that, and it is the kind of leakage that produces a
result nobody can reproduce.

**<Dataset id="allofus" />** is the complement: fewer genomes, but deliberately over-sampling
populations that earlier cohorts under-represented, and linked to longitudinal health records
<Citation id="allofus2024" />. Access is controlled — analysis happens inside the Researcher
Workbench and the data does not leave it, which is a design constraint on your pipeline, not
a footnote.

**<Dataset id="thousand-genomes" />** stopped being a useful frequency reference years ago;
2,504 individuals cannot measure what a million can. It remains the default LD and phasing
panel because it is open, small enough to distribute, and everything is benchmarked against
it <Citation id="tgp2015" />.

**<Dataset id="topmed" />** is three different things wearing one name: a controlled
dbGaP dataset, an open allele-frequency browser, and a free imputation server
<Citation id="taliun2021" />. Which access level applies depends entirely on which one you
need — a distinction the single word "controlled" in the table above cannot carry.

**<Dataset id="hgdp" />** is small and sampled for geographic breadth rather than disease
<Citation id="bergstrom2020" />. Its value is showing what a frequency looks like outside
the handful of well-sequenced groups.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Global AF is the wrong number to filter on">
gnomAD reports a global AF and a per-ancestry-group AF. The global figure is dominated by
whichever groups contributed most of the AN — historically European-ancestry samples.

A variant that reached 2% by drift in one population and is absent elsewhere can have a
global AF of $10^{-4}$: rare-looking, and completely uninformative for a patient from that
population. **`grpmax`** — the highest frequency across ancestry groups — is the number that
filters, because a variant common *anywhere* in healthy people is not causing a severe
dominant disease *anywhere*.

This is not a rare edge case. It is the ordinary situation for founder populations, and it
is the mechanism by which frequency filters fail hardest on exactly the patients least
served by the reference data.
</Callout>

## What a count actually establishes

<WorkedExample
  title="The same allele count, two denominators"
  given={[
    { label: 'Allele count', value: 'AC = 3 in both' },
    { label: 'Large cohort', value: 'AN = 1,500,000' },
    { label: 'Small cohort', value: 'AN = 6,000' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the point estimates.**

$$
\text{AF}_{\text{large}} = \frac{3}{1{,}500{,}000} = 2.000 \times 10^{-6},
\qquad
\text{AF}_{\text{small}} = \frac{3}{6{,}000} = 5.000 \times 10^{-4}
$$

Two hundred and fifty times apart, from the identical observation. The numerator carried no
information about the frequency at all; the denominator carried all of it.

**Step 2 — the intervals.** Using the Wilson score interval, which is the right one for a
proportion this small:

$$
\text{large: } [6.80 \times 10^{-7},\; 5.88 \times 10^{-6}]
\qquad
\text{small: } [1.70 \times 10^{-4},\; 1.47 \times 10^{-3}]
$$

**Step 3 — read them.** Both intervals span the same *relative* range — about 2.6 times the
point estimate either way, because three observations is three observations. What differs is
where that range sits. The large cohort has established a frequency near $10^{-6}$; the small
one has established only that the frequency is somewhere in a band that includes values a
thousand times higher.

The lesson is not that the small cohort is wrong. It is that **AC alone is not evidence**,
and an AF quoted without its AN cannot be acted on.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="key" title="When nothing is observed, the bound has a closed form">
Set $k = 0$ in the Wilson interval and the algebra collapses. The centre becomes
$\tfrac{z^2/2}{n + z^2}$ and the half-width becomes exactly the same quantity, so the lower
bound is zero and

$$
\text{AF}_{\text{upper}} = \frac{z^2}{n + z^2} \;\approx\; \frac{z^2}{n}
$$

At 95% confidence $z^2 = 3.8415$, so seeing no carriers in $n$ chromosomes bounds the
frequency at roughly $3.84/n$ — and nothing more. This is the "rule of three" in its exact
form, and it is worth committing to memory: **absence in $n$ chromosomes excludes
frequencies above about $4/n$, and says nothing whatever below that.**
</Callout>

## Turning a disease into a threshold

A frequency is only high or low relative to something. The ACMG/AMP framework
<Citation id="richards2015" /> makes frequency a stand-alone exclusion — its BA1 criterion
is the only one that can classify a variant benign on its own — but leaves the threshold to
judgement. <Citation id="whiffin2017" /> made that threshold explicit: given a disease's prevalence and genetic architecture, there is a
**maximum credible allele frequency** above which no single variant can be causal, because
the arithmetic would require more affected people than the disease has.

For a dominant (monoallelic) disorder, each affected carrier contributes one disease allele
out of two:

$$
\text{AF}_{\max} = \frac{p \times \text{MGC} \times \text{MAC}}{2f}
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="A filtering threshold from first principles"
  given={[
    { label: 'Prevalence p', value: '1 in 500' },
    { label: 'Max genetic contribution', value: '0.20 (this gene)' },
    { label: 'Max allelic contribution', value: '0.02 (one variant)' },
    { label: 'Penetrance f', value: '0.50' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — substitute.**

$$
\text{AF}_{\max} = \frac{0.002 \times 0.20 \times 0.02}{2 \times 0.50}
= \frac{8 \times 10^{-6}}{1}
= 8.000 \times 10^{-6}
$$

**Step 2 — read it as a filter.** Any variant whose `grpmax` frequency exceeds
$8 \times 10^{-6}$ in a reference population is too common to be a monogenic cause of this
disease under these assumptions. That is a hard exclusion, and it is why frequency is such
strong *exculpatory* evidence.

**Step 3 — ask what it takes to apply it.** The filter compares an observed frequency to
$8 \times 10^{-6}$. If the variant is absent, the comparison needs the upper bound to fall
below the threshold, and by the closed form above that needs

$$
\frac{z^2}{n + z^2} < 8 \times 10^{-6}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n > z^2\left(\frac{1}{8 \times 10^{-6}} - 1\right) \approx 480{,}000
$$

chromosomes. Below roughly half a million, "absent from the reference" simply does not
establish the thing the filter needs. gnomAD's 1.6 million clears it comfortably; a
population-specific cohort of a few thousand does not come close.

**Step 4 — note what the threshold is not.** It excludes; it does not implicate. A variant
below $8 \times 10^{-6}$ has passed a filter that the great majority of rare neutral variants
also pass — every genome carries thousands of them.

</WorkedExample>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Read a count" difficulty="warm-up">

A variant has AC = 12 and AN = 800,000. Compute the frequency and its 95% Wilson interval,
and say whether it clears the $8 \times 10^{-6}$ threshold derived above.

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{AF} = \frac{12}{800{,}000} = 1.500 \times 10^{-5},
\qquad
95\% \text{ CI} = [8.58 \times 10^{-6},\; 2.62 \times 10^{-5}]
$$

The point estimate is roughly twice the threshold, and — the part that matters — the **entire
confidence interval lies above it**. The variant is too common to be a monogenic cause of
that disease, and the conclusion is not marginal.

Had the interval straddled the threshold, the honest answer would have been that the data do
not decide, which is a different result from "it passes".

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Absent globally, common locally" difficulty="core">

A variant is absent from gnomAD's non-Finnish European samples (AN ≈ 1,200,000) but present
at AF = 1×10⁻³ among Finnish samples (AN ≈ 40,000). The global AF is about 3×10⁻⁵. Your
patient is Finnish. Which number should the filter use, and what does the pattern suggest?

<div slot="solution">

Use the **Finnish** frequency, $10^{-3}$ — which is what `grpmax` reports. Against the
$8 \times 10^{-6}$ threshold it fails by more than two orders of magnitude, so the variant is
excluded as a monogenic cause.

The global AF of $3 \times 10^{-5}$ is an artefact of averaging: it is the Finnish carriers
diluted by a million non-Finnish chromosomes in which the variant does not segregate. It
describes no actual population, and using it here would have let a common local variant
through the filter.

The pattern itself — absent in a large group, appreciable in a smaller isolated one — is the
signature of a **founder effect**: a bottleneck, then drift lifting a variant that arose once
to appreciable frequency. Finland, Ashkenazi Jewish, Amish and French-Canadian populations
all show it, and it is precisely why single-population reference data generalises badly.

The uncomfortable corollary: this failure mode is worst for the populations with the least
reference data, because a small AN both hides local commonness and produces a global average
dominated by someone else.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="How much cohort would you need?" difficulty="stretch">

You are studying a recessive disease with prevalence 1 in 40,000, where one gene accounts for
at most 60% of cases and one variant for at most 10% of those, with full penetrance. For a
biallelic disease the inheritance factor is 1 rather than 2. Derive the maximum credible
frequency, then the AN needed to exclude it from a zero observation.

<div slot="solution">

**The threshold.** With $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$, MGC $= 0.60$, MAC $= 0.10$, $f = 1$ and an
inheritance factor of 1:

$$
\text{AF}_{\max} = \frac{2.5\times10^{-5} \times 0.60 \times 0.10}{1} = 1.500 \times 10^{-6}
$$

**The cohort.** From the closed form,

$$
n > z^2\left(\frac{1}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}} - 1\right)
= 3.8415 \times (666{,}666.7 - 1)
\approx 2.56 \times 10^{6}
$$

About **2.56 million chromosomes**, or 1.28 million individuals — more than gnomAD currently
holds. So for this disease a zero observation in gnomAD *cannot* exclude the threshold, and
frequency evidence simply is not available at the strength the filter assumes.

That is the general shape of the problem: the rarer the disease, the lower the credible
frequency, and the requirement on AN grows as $1/\text{AF}_{\max}$ — linearly in the
reciprocal, which is brutal. Recessive conditions with small allelic contributions are where
frequency evidence runs out first, and where absence genuinely means nothing.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **AN decides what AF means.** The same AC of 3 is $2\times10^{-6}$ in 1.5 million
  chromosomes and $5\times10^{-4}$ in six thousand. Never read one without the other.
- **A zero observation has an exact bound**, $z^2/(n + z^2) \approx 3.84/n$ at 95%. Absence
  in $n$ chromosomes excludes frequencies above roughly $4/n$ and nothing below.
- **Filter on `grpmax`, not the global AF.** The global figure is an average over whoever
  contributed the AN, and it hides founder variants in exactly the populations with the least
  reference data.
- **A threshold comes from the disease, not the data.** Prevalence, genetic and allelic
  contribution and penetrance give a maximum credible frequency; anything above it is
  excluded, and anything below it has merely passed a filter thousands of neutral variants
  also pass.
- **Joint call sets and the non-UKB subset are not details.** One keeps callability honest;
  the other keeps your evaluation from sharing samples with your filter.
