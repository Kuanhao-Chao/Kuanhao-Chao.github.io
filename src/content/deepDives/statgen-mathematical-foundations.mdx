## The question this lesson answers

The rest of this curriculum is a sequence of specific machines. A mixed model that absorbs
relatedness into a variance component. A regression of $\chi^2$ statistics on LD scores. A
Bayes factor computed over a fine-mapping region. A ratio of two association estimates that
claims to be causal. They are introduced separately, they carry different names, and they
appear to have nothing to do with one another.

They are four arrangements of the same three parts:

1. a **likelihood** — a function that says how well each candidate value of a parameter
   explains the data;
2. a **test** — a way of asking whether some parameter is zero, of which there are exactly
   three classical answers;
3. a **decision rule** — a way of turning that answer into a number you are allowed to act
   on, which in this field means surviving a million simultaneous tests.

This lesson assembles those parts once, on data small enough to check by hand, so that the
later lessons can use them without stopping to re-derive them. It is the only lesson in the
series with no genetics in it, which is deliberate: the statistics is hard enough to learn
without simultaneously learning what a haplotype is.

## Likelihood: one function and its first two derivatives

Write $L(\theta) = \Pr(\mathbf{y} \mid \theta)$ — the probability of the data you actually
saw, read as a function of the parameter rather than of the data. Maximising it is the
estimation principle <Citation id="fisher1922likelihood" /> introduced, and it is the one
this curriculum uses almost everywhere. Work with the logarithm, because products of
independent observations become sums:

$$
\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)
$$

Everything else is a derivative of this one function. The **score** is its slope,

$$
U(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta}
$$

and the maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat\theta$ is where that slope is zero. The
**observed information** is minus its curvature, $J(\theta) = -\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2$,
and the **Fisher information** is what that curvature averages to over repeated samples,
$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[J(\theta)]$. The central asymptotic result is that in
large samples

$$
\hat\theta \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\theta,\; \mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}\right)
$$

so information is literally the inverse of variance: a sharply peaked log-likelihood is a
precise estimate, a flat one is not.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Information is curvature, and curvature is precision">

Picture the log-likelihood as a hill whose summit is the estimate. If the hill is a narrow
spike, moving the parameter even slightly makes the data much less probable — the data have
pinned the parameter down, and the standard error is small. If it is a broad plateau, a wide
range of parameter values explains the data about equally well, and the standard error is
large. The second derivative measures exactly that narrowness. This is why the standard
error printed beside every GWAS effect size is $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}$: it is a statement
about the shape of a hill you never see.

</Callout>

Do this once concretely, because the same three quantities reappear in every later lesson.
For $k$ successes in $n$ independent trials with success probability $p$:

$$
\ell(p) = k\log p + (n-k)\log(1-p)
$$

$$
U(p) = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p},
\qquad
J(p) = \frac{k}{p^2} + \frac{n-k}{(1-p)^2},
\qquad
\mathcal{I}(p) = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}
$$

Setting $U(p) = 0$ gives $\hat p = k/n$, which is the answer you would have written down
anyway. The point is not the answer; it is that the machinery produced it, and that the same
machinery will produce answers in situations where intuition has nothing to offer.

Note that $J$ and $\mathcal{I}$ differ: the first depends on the data through $k$, the second
does not. Which to use for a standard error is a real question, and
<Citation id="efron1978information" /> showed that the observed information is generally the
better choice — it is conditional on the sample you have rather than on samples you did not
take.

## Three tests, one hypothesis

To test $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ you need to compare the null against the fit. There are
three ways to measure the distance, and they use different parts of the same curve.

**The Wald test** <Citation id="wald1943" /> measures the horizontal distance between
$\hat\theta$ and $\theta_0$, scaled by the curvature at the estimate:

$$
W = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^2 \, \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)
$$

**The score test** <Citation id="rao1948score" /> never fits the alternative at all. It
stands at the null and asks how steep the likelihood is there — if $\theta_0$ were correct,
the slope should be near zero:

$$
S = \frac{U(\theta_0)^2}{\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)}
$$

**The likelihood-ratio test** <Citation id="wilks1938lrt" /> measures the vertical drop
between the two heights:

$$
\Lambda = 2\big[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta_0)\big]
$$

All three converge to $\chi^2_d$ under the null, where $d$ is the number of restrictions.

The table, with its margins:

|  | allele A | allele a | total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **cases** | 80 | 20 | 100 |
| **controls** | 40 | 60 | 100 |
| **total** | 120 | 80 | 200 |

**Step 1 — the estimate.** The model is logistic with the intercept as a nuisance parameter,
and for a 2 × 2 table the fit is saturated, so the maximum-likelihood estimate of the log
odds ratio is available in closed form:

$$
\hat\beta = \log \frac{ad}{bc} = \log \frac{80 \times 60}{20 \times 40} = \log 6 = 1.791759
$$

**Step 2 — Wald.** Woolf's standard error is built from the reciprocals of the four cells:

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{80} + \tfrac{1}{20} + \tfrac{1}{40} + \tfrac{1}{60}}
= \sqrt{0.1041667} = 0.322749
$$

$$
W = \left(\frac{1.791759}{0.322749}\right)^2 = 5.551564^2 = 30.8199
$$

**Step 3 — score.** Under independence each cell's expectation is its row total times its
column total over $n$, giving $E = (60, 40, 60, 40)$. The score statistic for this model is
**exactly Pearson's $\chi^2$** — the same statistic introduced in every first course as an
unrelated test of a contingency table:

$$
S = \sum_i \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}
= \frac{400}{60} + \frac{400}{40} + \frac{400}{60} + \frac{400}{40}
$$

$$
= 6.666667 + 10 + 6.666667 + 10 = 33.3333
$$

**Step 4 — likelihood ratio.** Twice the difference in log-likelihood is **exactly the
deviance** $G^2 = 2\sum_i O_i \log(O_i/E_i)$:

$$
\Lambda = 2\big[80(0.287682) + 20(-0.693147) + 40(-0.405465) + 60(0.405465)\big]
$$

$$
= 2\big[23.014566 - 13.862944 - 16.218604 + 24.327906\big]
$$

$$
= 2 \times 17.260924 = 34.5218
$$

**Step 5 — read all three against $\chi^2_1$.**

| test | statistic | $p$-value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Likelihood ratio ($G^2$) | 34.5218 | $4.215\times10^{-9}$ |
| Score (Pearson's $\chi^2$) | 33.3333 | $7.764\times10^{-9}$ |
| Wald | 30.8199 | $2.831\times10^{-8}$ |

Same table, same hypothesis, same asymptotic distribution — and $p$-values spanning a factor
of 6.7. All three clear $5\times10^{-8}$, but Wald clears it by less than a factor of two.
A slightly weaker effect and this variant would be genome-wide significant by one test and
not by another, with nothing to choose between them but convention.

</WorkedExample>

The identities in steps 3 and 4 are worth pausing on, because they collapse three things
most readers learned separately. **Pearson's $\chi^2$ is the score test** for the 2 × 2
table, and **the deviance $G^2$ is the likelihood-ratio test**. They were not invented as
approximations to each other; they are the same construction read at two points on one
curve. `deepDiveMath.ts` proves both against a numerically profiled logistic likelihood
rather than asserting them.

<Callout variant="warning" title="There is no universal ordering of the three">

It is often said that Wald exceeds the likelihood ratio, which exceeds the score test. That
inequality is a theorem for the *linear* model with normal errors, and it does not carry
over. This table gives $\Lambda > S > W$ — the reverse. A binomial proportion far from its
null, as in Exercise 1, gives $W > \Lambda > S$.

The reliable statement is weaker and more useful: the three agree to the precision anyone
cares about when $\hat\theta$ is close to $\theta_0$, and separate unpredictably in the tail.
Since genome-wide significance lives entirely in the tail, **which test produced a borderline
$p$-value is part of the result**, not a footnote to it.

</Callout>

### Which test a method uses, and why

The choice is almost never made on statistical grounds. It is made on what is expensive.

| Test | Needs | Where the curriculum meets it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wald** | The fit under the alternative only | The $\hat\beta$ and SE in every GWAS summary-statistics file; confidence intervals; the Wald ratio in Mendelian randomisation |
| **Score** | The fit under the null only | Genome scans: fit the null model once with all its covariates and its relatedness structure, then test millions of variants cheaply. Also SKAT and burden tests |
| **Likelihood ratio** | Both fits | Variance-component testing in GREML; comparing nested models where neither fit is expensive |

The score test's advantage is the reason it dominates large-scale association testing. A
linear mixed model costs a great deal to fit; fitting one per variant across ten million
variants is not possible. Fitting it *once* under the null and then evaluating a slope at
each variant is, and that is precisely what a score test evaluates.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The Wald statistic is not invariant, and can move the wrong way">

Two things go wrong with Wald tests specifically, and both have bitten real analyses.

First, it is **not invariant to reparameterisation**. Testing $\beta = 0$ and testing
$e^{\beta} = 1$ are the same hypothesis, but they give different Wald statistics, because
the curvature is measured on different scales. The likelihood-ratio test gives the same
answer either way.

Second, the **Hauck–Donner effect**: as an effect size grows very large, the estimated
standard error can grow faster than the estimate, so the Wald statistic *decreases* and a
genuinely enormous effect can fail to reach significance. This appears in logistic
regression when a variant almost perfectly separates cases from controls — exactly the
situation with a very rare, very penetrant variant. The likelihood-ratio and score tests are
untroubled by it.

</Callout>

## The linear model is a projection

The second reusable object is the linear model. Write

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
$$

and choose $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ to minimise $\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \rVert^2$.
Differentiating and setting to zero gives the **normal equations**

$$
\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}
$$

Two readings of the same formula matter later. **Geometrically**, the fitted values are
$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}$ with $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}$,
the orthogonal projection onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$; the residual is what is
left over, perpendicular to everything the model can express. **Probabilistically**, if
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ then the
log-likelihood is $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert^2$
plus a constant, so **least squares is maximum likelihood** and the machinery of the
previous section applies unchanged.

**Step 1 — assemble the cross-products.** With an intercept column,
$\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}]$, so

$$
\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x \\ \sum x & \sum x^2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} \sum y \\ \sum xy \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 20.0 \\ 59.7 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 2 — invert the 2 × 2.** Its determinant is $4(30) - 10(10) = 20$, so

$$
(\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} 30 & -10 \\ -10 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 3 — solve.**

$$
\hat\beta_0 = \frac{30(20.0) - 10(59.7)}{20} = \frac{600 - 597}{20} = 0.15
$$

$$
\hat\beta_1 = \frac{4(59.7) - 10(20.0)}{20} = \frac{238.8 - 200}{20} = 1.94
$$

So $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (0.15,\, 1.94)$. The slope is the quantity of interest; the
intercept is a nuisance parameter that had to be estimated anyway, and the $-10$ off-diagonal
in the inverse is why estimating it changed the slope from what a no-intercept fit would give.
That off-diagonal is the whole reason covariates matter: **estimates in a linear model are
not independent of one another**, and the inverse cross-product matrix is the bookkeeping of
how much each one contaminates the rest.

</WorkedExample>

Everything later is a modification of these normal equations, and it is worth naming the
modifications now so they are recognisable when they arrive:

- Replace $\mathbf{I}$ with a general covariance $\mathbf{V}$ and you get generalised least
  squares, which is what a **linear mixed model** solves once $\mathbf{V}$ carries the
  genetic relatedness.
- Add a penalty $\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert^2$ and you get **ridge regression**,
  whose solution $(\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}$
  is algebraically the same object as BLUP and as the shrinkage applied to polygenic scores.
- Replace the normal likelihood with a binomial one and the closed form disappears, but the
  score and information do not — that is logistic regression, and it is fitted by iterating
  weighted least squares.

## From a statistic to a decision

A $\chi^2_1$ statistic becomes a $p$-value through the tail of its distribution, and for one
degree of freedom the relation to the normal is exact: $\chi^2 = z^2$, so
$p = 2\big[1 - \Phi(|z|)\big]$. This is why summary statistics files sometimes carry $z$,
sometimes $\chi^2$, and sometimes only $p$ — they are three encodings of one number, and
LD score regression in a later lesson consumes the $\chi^2$ form directly.

What that number is *not* is the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is the
probability of data at least this extreme *if the null were true*. The distinction is not
pedantry: a $p$-value of $10^{-6}$ in a scan of a million variants is unremarkable, and
converting it into a belief about the variant requires a prior, which the next section
supplies.

### Why the threshold is 5 × 10⁻⁸

If you test $M$ independent hypotheses at level $\alpha$, the chance of at least one false
positive is $1 - (1-\alpha)^M \approx M\alpha$. Holding that family-wise error at 0.05
requires testing each at $\alpha/M$ — the Bonferroni correction. The genome-wide threshold is
that arithmetic with a specific $M$:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} = \frac{0.05}{M_{\mathrm{eff}}} = \frac{0.05}{10^6} = 5\times 10^{-8}
$$

The number one million is not the number of variants tested, which is far larger. It is the
number of *effectively independent* tests, and it is smaller than the variant count because
LD makes neighbouring variants redundant. <Citation id="peer2008threshold" /> estimated it by
tagging common variants in European samples; <Citation id="dudbridge2008threshold" /> reached
a compatible figure by permutation. On the $\chi^2_1$ scale the threshold is 29.7168, or
$|z| = 5.4513$.

<Callout variant="assumption" title="What the threshold assumes, and when it changes">

$5\times10^{-8}$ is a property of a particular genome, a particular ancestry and a
particular set of variants — not a law of statistics. It moves when any of those change:

- **Ancestry.** LD blocks are shorter in African-ancestry populations, so more independent
  tests fit in the same genome and $M_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is larger.
- **Variant class.** Whole-genome sequencing tests rare variants that tag nothing, so
  $M_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rises again; exome-wide gene-based tests run roughly 20,000 tests and
  use a correspondingly looser threshold.
- **Number of traits.** Testing 100 phenotypes on the same variants multiplies the burden,
  and nothing in the conventional threshold accounts for it.

Quoting $5\times10^{-8}$ for an analysis that satisfies none of these conditions is the most
common way the correction is misapplied.

</Callout>

## The Bayesian alternative

The frequentist tests above never state how plausible the hypothesis was to begin with. The
Bayesian route makes that explicit. Posterior odds are prior odds times a **Bayes factor**:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{\Pr(H_1 \mid \mathbf{y})}{\Pr(H_0 \mid \mathbf{y})}}_{\text{posterior odds}}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\Pr(H_1)}{\Pr(H_0)}}_{\text{prior odds}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\Pr(\mathbf{y} \mid H_1)}{\Pr(\mathbf{y} \mid H_0)}}_{\text{Bayes factor}}
$$

The Bayes factor resembles a likelihood ratio, and for simple hypotheses it *is* one. The
difference is that when $H_1$ contains a free parameter, the Bayes factor averages the
likelihood over a prior on that parameter rather than maximising it — which is what makes it
penalise a model for having somewhere to hide.

This becomes concrete in fine-mapping, where the object of interest is a posterior inclusion
probability (PIP) for each variant $j$ in a region:

$$
\mathrm{PIP}_j = \frac{\pi_j \,\mathrm{BF}_{10,j}}{\pi_0 + \sum_{k} \pi_k \,\mathrm{BF}_{10,k}}
$$

The subscript matters. $\mathrm{BF}_{10}$ is the direction defined above — evidence *for*
the alternative — so it multiplies. The fine-mapping lesson writes the same quantity with
$\mathrm{BF}_{01}$, its exact reciprocal, which therefore divides. Both give the same PIP;
mixing them silently does not.

The term $\pi_0$ in the denominator is the prior probability that the region contains **no**
causal variant, and dropping it — as is often done — forces the PIPs to sum to one and
thereby asserts that some variant in the window must be causal. That assertion is frequently
false, and the later fine-mapping lesson keeps the term explicit for exactly this reason.

## Where these approximations fail

Every result above is asymptotic. Two failures matter enough in genetics to name here,
because both have produced published false positives.

**Sparse counts.** The $\chi^2$ approximation assumes the score is approximately normal,
which needs enough observations in every cell. For a variant carried by twelve people in a
cohort of half a million — an ordinary situation in sequencing studies — it is badly wrong,
and wrong in the anti-conservative direction when the case-control ratio is unbalanced.
Exact tests, permutation, and saddlepoint approximations exist for this; the rare-variant
lesson takes it up properly.

**A parameter on the boundary.** Testing whether a variance component is zero — the central
question in heritability estimation — puts the null value at the edge of the parameter space,
since a variance cannot be negative. Wilks' theorem assumes the null sits in the interior and
simply does not apply. <Citation id="self1987boundary" /> gives the correct reference
distribution: a 50:50 mixture of a point mass at zero and $\chi^2_1$. The practical
consequence is that the naive $p$-value is *twice* what it should be, so using $\chi^2_1$
uncorrected is conservative — the 5% critical value is 2.7055, not 3.8415.

<Callout variant="key" title="What to carry into the rest of the curriculum">

A likelihood, its slope, and its curvature. Three tests that measure the same discrepancy in
three places and agree only asymptotically. Least squares as both a projection and a maximum
-likelihood estimate, with the inverse cross-product matrix recording how estimates
contaminate one another. A threshold that is arithmetic over an effective number of tests,
not a constant of nature. And two named places where all of it stops being true.

</Callout>

**(a)** The margins are 1,000 / 1,000 by 440 / 1,560, so $E = (220, 780, 220, 780)$ and

$\hat\beta = 0.233615$ and $\mathrm{SE} = 0.108316$:

$$
W = 4.6517, \qquad S = 4.6620, \qquad \Lambda = 4.6671
$$

The three now span 0.3% of their own size, against 12% in the worked example. All give
$p \approx 0.031$. This is the general behaviour: the three statistics are built from the
same quadratic approximation to the log-likelihood, and that approximation is good exactly
when the estimate is near the null.

**(b)** For the binomial, $\hat p = 0.40$ and $\mathcal{I}(\hat p) = 50/(0.4 \times 0.6) = 208.3333$:

$$
W = (0.40-0.50)^2 \times 208.3333 = 2.0833,
\qquad
S = \frac{0.01}{0.005} = 2.0000
$$

$$
\Lambda = 2\left[20\log\frac{0.40}{0.50} + 30\log\frac{0.60}{0.50}\right] = 2.0136
$$

**Together.** Part (a) gives $\Lambda > S > W$; part (b) gives $W > \Lambda > S$. The
ordering is not a property of the three tests, it is a property of the problem — which is
precisely why no rule of thumb about "Wald is anticonservative" survives contact with real
data. What does generalise is that the spread shrinks as the estimate approaches the null,
and that all of these examples are far more benign than a genome-wide scan, where the
interesting statistics sit eight orders of magnitude into the tail.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Thresholds for two study designs" difficulty="core">

(a) Confirm the genome-wide threshold and give the corresponding $\chi^2_1$ and $|z|$
critical values. (b) A gene-based exome analysis runs one test per protein-coding gene,
about 20,000 of them. What threshold and critical value does Bonferroni give? (c) Why is
the answer to (b) not simply "the genome-wide threshold, since it is more stringent and
therefore safe"?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $0.05 / 10^6 = 5\times10^{-8}$. The $\chi^2_1$ critical value is 29.7168 and
$|z| = \sqrt{29.7168} = 5.4513$.

**(b)** $0.05 / 20{,}000 = 2.50\times10^{-6}$, a $\chi^2_1$ critical value of 22.1665. The
threshold is fifty times looser than genome-wide because there are fifty times fewer tests.

**(c)** Because a threshold that is too stringent is not "safe" — it is a different error.
Using $5\times10^{-8}$ for 20,000 tests holds the family-wise error rate at 0.001 rather than
0.05, discarding real signal for no stated benefit. The correction is supposed to match the
number of tests actually performed; treating stringency as a free good is how gene-based and
rare-variant analyses come to be systematically underpowered. Note also that both figures
assume independence, and 20,000 gene-based tests are closer to independent than a million
common-variant tests are.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A variance component at the boundary" difficulty="stretch">

A GREML analysis reports a likelihood-ratio statistic of $\Lambda = 2.7055$ for the
hypothesis $\sigma^2_g = 0$. (a) What $p$-value does a naive $\chi^2_1$ give? (b) What is
the correct $p$-value under the Self–Liang mixture? (c) Which direction does the naive
calculation err in, and why does that direction make the error easy to miss?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The upper tail of $\chi^2_1$ at 2.7055 is $p = 0.1000$.

**(b)** Under the null, $\hat\sigma^2_g$ is at the boundary half the time, giving
$\Lambda = 0$; the other half of the time $\Lambda$ behaves like $\chi^2_1$. The reference
distribution is therefore $\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^2_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\chi^2_1$, and the tail
probability is half the naive one:

$$
p = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.1000 = 0.0500
$$

Equivalently, the 5% critical value is the 90th percentile of $\chi^2_1$, which is 2.7055,
rather than the 95th percentile, 3.8415.

**(c)** The naive $p$-value is *larger* than the correct one, so the error is conservative:
it makes you under-report heritability, not over-report it. That is exactly why it survives.
An anti-conservative error announces itself as a flood of implausible findings; a
conservative one produces a slightly quieter result that nobody investigates. It is worth
stating explicitly that "conservative" is not a synonym for "correct" — a boundary-corrected
analysis and a naive one disagree about which traits are heritable at all near the margin.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="A test statistic that falls as the effect grows" difficulty="stretch">

Take the balanced table of Figure 2 — 100 per group, control allele frequency 0.5. At an odds
ratio of 16 the Wald statistic is 34.84; at 128 it is 17.57, while the likelihood ratio has
gone from 53.54 to 78.91. (a) Explain in terms of $\hat\beta$ and its standard error why Wald
can fall. (b) What does that imply for a rare, highly penetrant variant in a case-control
scan? (c) Which of the three tests would you use for such a variant, and what does it cost?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $W = \hat\beta^2/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta)^2$, and Woolf's standard error is
$\sqrt{1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d}$. As the odds ratio grows, one cell empties: at OR = 128 the
case-side minor-allele count is $100(1-0.9922) = 0.78$. $\hat\beta$ grows like $\log(\mathrm{OR})$
— logarithmically — while the standard error grows like $1/\sqrt{b}$, which diverges as
$b \to 0$. The denominator eventually outruns the numerator and the ratio turns over.

**(b)** Exactly that configuration is a rare penetrant variant: nearly all carriers are cases,
so the control-side or case-side count is tiny. The Wald statistic is then at its least
trustworthy precisely where the biology is most interesting, and a variant with an enormous
true effect can fail to reach significance.

**(c)** The likelihood ratio, which is monotone in the evidence here, or the score test, which
is computed entirely under the null and never sees the empty cell. The cost is computational:
Wald comes free from a fit you already did, while the likelihood ratio needs a second fit under
the null and the score test needs the null fit. In a genome-wide scan that difference is
multiplied by a million, which is why Wald is the default despite this — and why software for
rare-variant tests goes back to the score statistic.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} title="Where the three separate" difficulty="stretch">

Using the same table family, the three statistics at an odds ratio of 2 are 5.652, 5.714 and
5.745. (a) Express the spread as a percentage of the mean. (b) At the worked example's table it
is 11.3%. What distinguishes the two cases? (c) A referee asks you to report "the" chi-square
for a locus. What should you report and why?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The mean is 5.704 and the spread is $5.745 - 5.652 = 0.093$, so
$0.093/5.704 = 1.6\%$. (Rounding the three to two decimals first gives 1.8% — the spread is a
difference of nearby numbers, so it loses precision fast, which is why the exercise quotes
three.)

**(b)** Effect size. The three tests are asymptotically equivalent — they agree to first order
around the null — so their disagreement is a second-order effect that grows with the distance
between $\hat\beta$ and $\beta_0$. At OR = 2 that distance is small and the three parabolas
almost coincide; at the worked example's OR = 6 it is large enough for the curvature to differ
noticeably between the null and the estimate.

**(c)** Report which test produced it. The three are different statistics, not three estimates
of one statistic, and at a strong locus they differ enough to matter — 30.82 against 34.52 in
the worked example is a factor of 6.7 in the p-value. A summary-statistics file that does not
say which test it used cannot be combined with another that used a different one, which is a
real problem in meta-analysis.

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A log-likelihood and its first two derivatives are the whole toolkit.** The score
  $U(\theta)$ locates the estimate; the information $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ measures the
  curvature there and is the inverse of the estimate's variance. Observed and expected
  information differ, and the observed form is generally preferable for a standard error.
- **Three tests measure one discrepancy in three places.** Wald uses only the fit under the
  alternative, the score test only the fit under the null, the likelihood ratio both. On one
  2 × 2 table they return 30.8199, 33.3333 and 34.5218 — $p$-values spanning a factor of
  6.7 — and there is no universal ordering among them; a binomial far from its null reverses
  it. They agree only as the estimate approaches the null.
- **Two of the three are old friends.** For a 2 × 2 table the score test *is* Pearson's
  $\chi^2$ and the likelihood-ratio test *is* the deviance $G^2$. Three separately-named
  procedures are one construction read at different points on a single curve.
- **The choice among them is computational.** Genome scans use score tests because the
  expensive null model — covariates, relatedness — is fitted once and reused for every
  variant; GWAS summary statistics report Wald quantities because $\hat\beta$ and its
  standard error are what a per-variant fit produces; GREML uses likelihood ratios because
  both fits are cheap and neither is nested inside a scan.
- **Least squares is a projection and a maximum-likelihood estimate at once**, with
  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}$.
  Mixed models, ridge regression, BLUP and polygenic-score shrinkage are all this equation
  with either the covariance or the penalty changed.
- **$5\times10^{-8}$ is Bonferroni over roughly a million effectively independent tests**,
  not a universal constant. It moves with ancestry, variant class and the number of traits
  tested, and a threshold that is too stringent is an error in its own right.
- **Two failures are worth memorising.** Sparse counts break the normal approximation to the
  score, anti-conservatively when case-control ratios are unbalanced. A variance component
  tested at zero sits on the boundary of its space, where the reference distribution is a
  50:50 mixture and the naive $\chi^2_1$ $p$-value is twice what it should be.
