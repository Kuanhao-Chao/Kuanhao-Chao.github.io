## The map: assumptions buy structure

A strong interview answer does not recite model names. It identifies the **object being
estimated**, the **assumptions that make it estimable**, the **loss that trains it**, and
the **decision that consumes its output**. Classical models make those links unusually
visible.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="One dataset, four different ideas of similarity">
A linear classifier says two examples are alike when they have similar weighted sums. A
Gaussian classifier says they are alike when they have similar probability under a class
density. kNN says they are alike when they are close under a chosen metric. A kernel
machine says they are alike when their inner product is large in some feature space.

Each can draw a boundary. The important interview question is **why that boundary should
generalize** in this dataset.
</Callout>

## Conditional or joint: what is the model trying to learn?

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-generative-discriminative">
**Intuition.** A discriminative classifier draws the border needed to predict $y$ from
$x$. A generative classifier first describes what each class could have generated, then
compares those descriptions. Formally, the former estimates $p(y\mid x)$ or a decision
score; the latter estimates $p(x\mid y)p(y)$ and obtains
$p(y\mid x) \propto p(x\mid y)p(y)$.

This distinction changes what the model can do. A joint model can generate features,
integrate a missing feature, and update the prior explicitly. Its structural assumptions
can make it learn quickly from few examples. A conditional model avoids spending capacity
on irrelevant details of $p(x)$ and often reaches lower classification error with enough
data. Ng and Jordan made this sample-efficiency/asymptotic contrast precise for Naive
Bayes and logistic regression <Citation id="ngjordan2002" />.

**Trap.** “Generative” does not automatically mean a neural image generator, and
“discriminative” does not mean non-probabilistic. Logistic regression is both
discriminative and probabilistic. Always name the distribution or boundary being learned.

**Follow-up.** If $p(x\mid y)$ is wrong but its likelihood ratio ranks the classes
correctly, classification may remain good while its probabilities are badly calibrated.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Linear regression: prediction, identification, and inference

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-linear-regression-assumptions">
Write $y=Xw+\varepsilon$. **Linearity in parameters** says the conditional mean is
represented by $Xw$; nonlinear basis functions may already be columns of $X$. **Full
column rank** makes $w$ identifiable. **Exogeneity**, $E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, prevents
systematic omitted signal from entering the error and gives unbiased coefficients.
Conditional homoscedasticity and uncorrelated errors make
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat w\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}$ the usual covariance; finite
variance is needed for its existence.

The Gauss–Markov conclusion is specific: under the linear model, exogeneity, spherical
errors, and full rank, OLS is the best—minimum-variance—**linear unbiased** estimator. It
does not say OLS is the best predictor among all algorithms. Gaussian errors are an extra
assumption for exact small-sample $t$ and $F$ distributions, not for minimizing squares or
for unbiasedness.

**When assumptions fail.** Heteroscedasticity calls for robust standard errors if the
conditional mean is valid. Dependence calls for clustered/time-series reasoning.
Endogeneity or omitted confounding biases the coefficient itself; a robust standard error
cannot repair it. For prediction, validate out of sample and inspect residual structure
instead of treating every assumption as an all-or-nothing ritual.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-normal-equations">
The squared-error objective is

$$L(w)=\lVert y-Xw\rVert_2^2.$$

Using $\nabla_w L=-2X^T(y-Xw)$ and setting it to zero gives the **normal equations**
$X^TXw=X^Ty$. If $X$ has full column rank, the symbolic solution is
$(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$.

That expression is a derivation, not an implementation recipe. Forming $X^TX$ squares the
condition number: a nearly collinear design loses roughly twice as many digits. Solve the
least-squares problem with QR; use SVD when rank and minimum-norm behavior matter; use an
iterative solver for large sparse $X$. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse supplies the
minimum-norm solution when rank is deficient.

**Dimensions.** $X$ is $n\times d$, $X^TX$ is $d\times d$, $X^Ty$ is $d\times1$, and
$w$ is $d\times1$. Checking these shapes catches the most common whiteboard error before
any calculus does.
</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="Solve and diagnose a two-feature least-squares problem"
  given={[
    { label: 'Design matrix', value: 'X = [[1,0], [1,1], [1,2]]' },
    { label: 'Targets', value: 'y = [1,2,2]' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>
Here the first column is an intercept. Compute

$$
X^TX=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
X^Ty=\begin{bmatrix}5\\6\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is $15-9=6$, so the design is full rank, and

$$
(X^TX)^{-1}=\frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\hat w=\begin{bmatrix}7/6\\1/2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Predictions are $[7/6,5/3,13/6]$ and residuals are $[-1/6,1/3,-1/6]$; their sum is zero
because the intercept makes the residual orthogonal to the all-ones column. Adding a third
column equal to twice the second would make the determinant zero: predictions could still
be identified, but individual coefficients would not be.
</WorkedExample>

## Regularization changes the estimand

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-ridge-lasso">
Ridge solves $\min_w L(w)+\lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2$ <Citation id="hoerl1970ridge" />;
lasso uses $\lambda\lVert w\rVert_1$ <Citation id="tibshirani1996lasso" />. The round L2
constraint touches a loss contour smoothly, so coefficients shrink continuously. The L1
diamond has axis-aligned corners, so optima often land at exactly zero. Elastic net mixes
the penalties and tends to keep groups of correlated features more stably.

**Statistical tradeoff.** Both deliberately add bias to reduce estimation variance. Ridge
is strong when many correlated predictors share weak signal. Lasso is useful when a sparse
approximation is plausible, but it does not prove the selected variables are causal and
can pick one arbitrary member of a correlated group.

**Implementation.** Standardize penalized features, usually leave the intercept
unpenalized, and select $\lambda$ inside cross-validation. The convention for scaling the
loss by $n$ changes the numerical meaning of $\lambda$, so compare definitions before
copying a value across libraries. L1 is nondifferentiable at zero; coordinate descent or a
proximal method handles it cleanly.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Logistic and multinomial models

Generalized linear models separate a random component, a linear predictor, and a link
function <Citation id="nelder1972glm" />. Logistic regression uses a Bernoulli response
and a logit link; Cox's early formulation established the binary regression model
<Citation id="cox1958logistic" />.

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-logistic-loss">
Assume $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ and
$p_i=P(y_i=1\mid x_i)=\sigma(z_i)$, where $z_i=w^Tx_i+b$. The Bernoulli likelihood is
$\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$. Taking its negative log gives

$$
-\log p(y\mid X)= -\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)],
$$

binary cross-entropy. This is not an arbitrary preference: it is maximum likelihood under
the model. Its Hessian is $X^TWX$ with nonnegative diagonal $W$, so the objective is
convex. Its gradient with respect to a logit is simply $p_i-y_i$.

Squared error plus a sigmoid can be optimized, but it does not match the Bernoulli
likelihood and creates flatter, less convenient gradients near saturated predictions.
Cross-entropy also penalizes confident wrong predictions sharply. **Trap:** convexity
does not guarantee a finite unregularized solution—perfectly separable data drives the
coefficient norm toward infinity.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-softmax">
For $K$ classes, logits $z_k=w_k^Tx+b_k$ become
$p_k=e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$. The negative log-likelihood for true class $y$ is
$-z_y+\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$, and $\partial L/\partial z_k=p_k-\mathbb{1}[k=y]$.

Softmax is invariant to adding one constant to all logits. Exploit that identity:

$$
\log\sum_j e^{z_j}=m+\log\sum_j e^{z_j-m},\qquad m=\max_j z_j.
$$

Now every exponent is at most one, preventing overflow. In code, pass raw logits to a
fused cross-entropy or `log_softmax`; do not compute softmax, take logs, and then feed the
result to a loss. Also remember that one class is redundant: shifting all class parameter
vectors equally changes nothing, so implementations fix a reference class or rely on
regularization.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-threshold-calibration">
**Discrimination** asks whether positives rank above negatives. **Calibration** asks
whether predictions near $p$ occur about a fraction $p$ of the time. **Decision-making**
asks which action has the best expected utility. They require different evidence.

For a calibrated probability $p=P(y=1\mid x)$, predict positive when

$$
pC_{FN} > (1-p)C_{FP}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p>\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}
$$

under the simple two-cost setup. Capacity constraints or downstream benefits change the
rule. Pick thresholds on validation data that reflects deployment prevalence, then report
the operating metrics and uncertainty. Platt or isotonic calibration must itself be fit
on held-out predictions.

**Trap.** A default threshold of 0.5 is justified only by a particular cost/prior setup.
Class weights, resampling, and distribution shift can also disconnect training outputs
from deployment probabilities.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Gaussian and local classifiers

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-naive-bayes">
Naive Bayes assumes $p(x\mid y=k)=\prod_jp(x_j\mid y=k)$. At prediction time, sum log
likelihoods and the log prior, avoiding underflow. The independence premise is often
false, yet the class with the largest posterior score can still be correct: classification
only needs the score ordering, while probability estimation needs the joint density to be
much more accurate.

The assumption also replaces a high-dimensional density estimate with many small
one-dimensional estimates, reducing variance and making the model competitive in sparse,
small-data settings. But duplicate or strongly correlated features count the same evidence
multiple times, often causing overconfidence. Smooth categorical counts so unseen values
do not zero an entire class likelihood.

**Follow-up.** Under a multivariate Bernoulli event model, absence can be evidence. Under
a multinomial text event model, token counts are evidence. Naming the event model matters
more than saying “use Naive Bayes.”
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-lda-qda">
Assume $x\mid y=k\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$. Taking the log posterior gives a
quadratic term $-\tfrac12x^T\Sigma_k^{-1}x$, a linear term, a determinant term, and the
log prior. LDA sets every $\Sigma_k=\Sigma$, so the common quadratic term cancels in a
pairwise comparison: the boundary is linear. QDA estimates one covariance per class, so
the quadratic terms remain and the boundary curves.

LDA estimates roughly $Kd+d(d+1)/2$ mean/covariance parameters; QDA needs
$Kd+Kd(d+1)/2$. QDA therefore buys flexibility with much higher variance and can be
singular when a class has fewer observations than dimensions. Shrinkage covariance is a
useful bridge.

**Trap.** “Linear” describes the decision boundary after the equal-covariance assumption;
it does not mean each class density is flat or that features are independent.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-knn">
kNN stores the training set and predicts from the labels or targets of the $k$ closest
points. With small $k$, neighborhoods adapt sharply—low approximation bias, high sampling
variance. Larger $k$ smooths noise but can cross real boundaries. Distance weighting makes
the transition gradual.

The method's real hyperparameter is often the **metric**. Standardize units, avoid mixing
arbitrary categorical encodings into Euclidean distance, and consider learned or
domain-specific distance. In high dimensions, pairwise distances concentrate and the
sample needed to populate a local neighborhood grows rapidly. Cover and Hart established
the classical nearest-neighbor error result <Citation id="coverhart1967" />, but its
asymptotic promise does not remove finite-sample geometry.

Naïve inference costs $O(nd)$ per query and storage $O(nd)$; exact trees help mainly in
lower dimensions, while approximate indexes trade recall for latency. Fit preprocessing
only on training folds: leakage through a global scaler changes every neighborhood.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Margins and kernels

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-svm-margin">
For a hyperplane $w^Tx+b=0$, the signed geometric distance of $(x_i,y_i)$ is
$y_i(w^Tx_i+b)/\lVert w\rVert$. Because multiplying $(w,b)$ changes the numerator and
denominator together, fix the functional constraint $y_i(w^Tx_i+b)\ge1$ and minimize
$\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2$. That maximizes the minimum geometric margin
$1/\lVert w\rVert$.

Nonseparable data introduce slack $\xi_i$:

$$
\min_{w,b,\xi}\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i,
\quad y_i(w^Tx_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,
\quad \xi_i\ge0.
$$

This is regularized hinge-loss minimization. Large $C$ prices violations heavily and
narrows the tolerated margin; small $C$ accepts more violations for a wider, more regular
boundary. Boser, Guyon, and Vapnik introduced the optimal-margin training construction
<Citation id="boser1992svm" />, extended to soft-margin support-vector networks by Cortes
and Vapnik <Citation id="cortes1995svm" />.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-kernel-trick">
The SVM dual depends on examples only through inner products. Replace
$x_i^Tx_j$ with $k(x_i,x_j)=\phi(x_i)^T\phi(x_j)$ to fit a linear separator in an
implicit feature space. Polynomial kernels represent interactions; the RBF kernel
$\exp(-\gamma\lVert x-z\rVert^2)$ represents localized similarity in an infinite-
dimensional space.

A valid real kernel produces a symmetric positive-semidefinite Gram matrix for every
finite set: $a^TKa\ge0$ for every $a$. This ensures it behaves like an inner product and
keeps the dual convex. “It looks like a similarity” is insufficient.

**Tradeoff.** Kernels avoid explicit high-dimensional features, but storing a dense Gram
matrix costs $O(n^2)$ and generic training can approach $O(n^3)$. For very large $n$,
explicit random/Nyström approximations, linear models, or neural representations may be
more practical.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-svm-hyperparameters">
In an RBF SVM, $C$ controls the penalty for violations and $\gamma$ controls the inverse
length scale. Large $C$ tries hard to classify training examples; large $\gamma$ makes
each support vector influential only in a tiny region. Together, large values can create
an intricate memorizing boundary; small $C$ and small $\gamma$ can underfit.

Scale features inside each fold, then search logarithmically over both parameters with
nested or properly held-out validation. Class weights add another cost choice. If calibrated
probabilities are needed, fit calibration after selecting the margin model—an SVM score is
not itself a posterior probability.

**Diagnostic.** Training and validation both poor suggests too little capacity or signal;
training excellent and validation poor suggests an overly local/high-cost fit. Counting
support vectors is useful: almost every point becoming a support vector often signals a
complex boundary, noisy overlap, or a poorly chosen scale.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-support-vectors-dual">
The dual has multipliers $\alpha_i$ and yields
$w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$. Its prediction is

$$f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i k(x_i,x)+b.$$

Complementary slackness says a point strictly beyond the margin has $\alpha_i=0$. Points
on the margin or violating it may have nonzero multipliers: they are the support vectors.
Moving an inactive point a little does not alter the boundary; moving a support vector can.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions also diagnose cases: $0<\alpha_i<C$ lies on the margin,
$\alpha_i=C$ is at or within the soft margin, and $\alpha_i=0$ is outside it, subject to
degeneracies. This sparsity is in **training examples**, not necessarily in input features.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Preprocessing, imbalance, and selection

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-scaling">
Scaling matters whenever units enter geometry or regularization. A one-unit change in age
and a one-unit change in income should not be treated as equal by kNN or an RBF kernel.
L1/L2 penalties compare coefficient magnitudes, so unscaled columns receive unequal
effective penalties. Gradient descent also moves slowly through elongated contours.

Trees are nearly invariant to monotone rescaling because threshold order is unchanged.
Unregularized linear predictions are invariant after coefficients adjust, although
conditioning and coefficient interpretation improve with scaling. Centering sparse
matrices can destroy sparsity; use scale-without-centering where appropriate.

Fit the scaler on each training split and apply it unchanged to validation/test data.
Scaling before the split leaks distribution information and makes cross-validation
optimistic.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-interactions-collinearity">
A “linear model” is linear in $w$, not necessarily in raw $x$. Adding $x^2$, splines, or
$x_1x_2$ lets its conditional mean curve or express effect modification while preserving
linear optimization. Center main effects before constructing interactions when that makes
the intercept and lower-order coefficients easier to interpret.

When columns are nearly dependent, many coefficient vectors make almost identical
predictions. Coefficient variance grows, signs can flip between samples, and individual
$p$-values become fragile even while predictive error stays acceptable. Diagnose the
design spectrum or condition number, not just pairwise correlations.

**Responses.** Collect information that separates the predictors; combine variables when
scientifically justified; use ridge for stable prediction; use sparse penalties cautiously;
or choose an explicit lower-dimensional basis. Dropping one feature blindly can increase
omitted-variable bias if interpretation, rather than prediction, is the aim.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-imbalance-weights">
Weighting positive examples by $a$ changes the empirical loss to behave as though positive
errors cost more—or, in an intercept-capable logistic model, roughly as though the class
prior changed. It can move the fitted boundary and improve minority recall. It does not
manufacture information about rare subtypes, and the resulting raw probability may no
longer match deployment prevalence.

Separate three actions: use stratified/group-aware splits to measure reliably; choose a
loss or sampler to learn the desired ranking; choose the operating threshold from deployment
costs. Report precision–recall behavior and calibration under natural prevalence. If a
sampler creates synthetic points, do it inside the training fold only.

**Trap.** Accuracy can be 99.9% for a useless classifier on a 0.1% event. The remedy is
not automatically class weighting; first define which errors matter and how many alerts
the system can act on.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-models-model-choice">
Start with constraints. For huge sparse $d$ and tight latency, a regularized linear model
is a formidable baseline. If local distance is meaningful, data are modest, and updates
must be immediate, kNN trades training cost for query cost. Kernel SVMs suit medium-sized
nonlinear data with a defensible similarity. A generative classifier is compelling when
its structure fits the mechanism, missing values must be marginalized, or few labels make
strong assumptions useful.

Then compare candidates under one leakage-safe validation protocol on the metric and
latency that deployment uses. Include calibration, memory, training frequency, explanation
needs, behavior under shift, and the cost of maintaining preprocessing. A 0.2-point metric
gain may not justify a 50-fold latency increase.

**Interview structure.** State a simple baseline, name the hypothesis behind each more
complex model, describe an ablation or validation that can falsify it, and say what evidence
would make you change your choice. That is model selection; “try everything and tune” is
only search.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="A penalty depends on units" difficulty="warm-up">
A ridge model uses two predictors: age in years and income in dollars. Why can applying the
same L2 penalty without scaling suppress age much more strongly, and what pipeline fixes it?

<div slot="solution">
A unit coefficient on income changes the score by one per dollar, while a unit coefficient
on age changes it by one per year. Because income values are numerically much larger, its
coefficient can be much smaller for a comparable predictive effect; L2 then penalizes it
less. Fit a scaler on the training fold, transform all splits with those training statistics,
leave the intercept unpenalized, and select the penalty within the same resampling loop.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Costs choose the threshold" difficulty="core">
A calibrated model estimates disease probability. A false positive costs 1 unit and a
false negative costs 9. With no other constraints, at what probability should the action
switch to positive?

<div slot="solution">
Predict positive when $(1-p)C_{FP}<pC_{FN}$. Thus $(1-p)<9p$, so $p>0.1$. The answer is
not 0.5. If action capacity, heterogeneous benefit, or an abstain option exists, the rule
must include those terms rather than blindly retaining 0.1.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A kernel that is only a similarity" difficulty="stretch">
You are given a symmetric similarity function. An engineer proposes inserting it into an
SVM because all diagonal entries equal one. What condition is missing, and how can you test
it on the observed sample?

<div slot="solution">
The Gram matrix must be positive semidefinite: $a^TKa\ge0$ for every vector $a$. Symmetry
and unit diagonal do not imply that. On the sample, form $K$, symmetrize small numerical
error, and inspect its eigenvalues or attempt a stable factorization. A materially negative
eigenvalue means it is not a valid inner-product kernel on that sample; clipping eigenvalues
changes the kernel and should be treated as a modeling decision.
</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- A model's objective, assumptions, geometry, and output interpretation are one contract.
- Least squares is simple to derive but should be solved with numerically stable linear algebra.
- L1 and L2 penalties change bias, variance, sparsity, and the meaning of feature scale.
- Generative classifiers model class densities; discriminative classifiers model the conditional decision directly.
- Nearest-neighbor and kernel methods are only as sound as their metric or kernel.
- Calibration, thresholding, and ranking are separate; deployment costs choose the action.
- Model selection starts from constraints and falsifiable hypotheses, not prestige.
