## The question this lesson answers

A treatment is applied, cells are counted, and monocytes come back at 33% of the sample where
they were 20%. Something happened to the monocytes. What happened to the T cells, which fell
from 40% to 33%?

Nothing. The T cells were not touched, and this lesson is about why the data cannot say so.

The root of it is that the experiment never measures a tissue. It measures whatever survived
dissociation and got loaded, and that number is set by the protocol. Absolute abundance is
therefore unavailable, and everything is a share of a total that means nothing.

## The closure factor

Let population $k$ change by a factor $c$ while every other abundance stays fixed. The total
becomes

$$
\sum_i a_i' = c\,a_k + \sum_{i \neq k} a_i = \left(\sum_i a_i\right)\left[1 + (c-1)p_k\right],
$$

so writing $D = 1 + (c-1)p_k$, the new proportions are

$$
p_k' = \frac{c\,p_k}{D},
\qquad
p_j' = \frac{p_j}{D} \quad \text{for every } j \neq k.
$$

Every untouched population is divided by the **same** $D$. Not proportionally to its size, not
more for the large ones — the identical factor for all of them. A differential-abundance test
comparing proportions will therefore find every other population significantly decreased, with
the same effect size, and every one of those findings is an artefact of the one population that
did move — and comparing cluster proportions between conditions is a one-line operation in both
major toolkits <Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />
<Citation id="hao2021seurat" />.

## The ambiguity is exact

It is worse than an inflated error rate. Two genuinely different experiments produce
numerically identical data.

Take abundances of 40, 20, 10, 8, 20 and 2 thousand cells across six populations. Now take an
experiment that recovered exactly half of everything — 20, 10, 5, 4, 10 and 1. The proportions
are

$$
0.40,\; 0.20,\; 0.10,\; 0.08,\; 0.20,\; 0.02
$$

in **both** cases, to the last decimal place. No statistic computed from proportions can
distinguish them, because there is nothing to distinguish: the closure map sends an entire ray
of abundance vectors to one point.

## What the centred log-ratio does

The standard compositional remedy is the centred log-ratio, $\log(p_i / \tilde{g})$ where
$\tilde{g}$ is the geometric mean. It removes the sum-to-one constraint — the CLR of any
composition sums to zero rather than to one — and it makes the data live in an unconstrained
space where ordinary methods apply.

It does not, however, undo the closure. Under a single population changing by $c$ among $K$
parts, the CLR shifts by exactly

$$
\Delta_k = +\log c \cdot \frac{K-1}{K},
\qquad
\Delta_j = -\frac{\log c}{K},
$$

so the untouched parts still move. For the six populations above with $c = 2$, monocytes shift
by $+0.833333$ and each of the other five by $-0.166667$ — smaller than the raw $-0.263034$,
but not zero, and still identical across five populations that did not change.

The CLR is a genuine improvement because the shifts are *known*, and because ratios between
parts are preserved exactly. It is not a way of recovering absolute abundance, because nothing
computed from proportions can be. Methods built for this data model the composition directly
and report which populations are credibly changed *given* the constraint, rather than testing
each one as though it were free <Citation id="buettner2021sccoda" />.

<WorkedExample
  title="Five significant declines that did not happen"
  given={[
    'Six populations at 40%, 20%, 10%, 8%, 20% and 2%',
    'Monocytes, at 20%, double in absolute number',
    'Nothing else changes at all',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the closure factor.**

$$
D = 1 + (2 - 1)(0.20) = 1.2.
$$

**Step 2 — what the other five do.** Each is divided by 1.2, so each falls to 83.33% of its
former share — a decline of **16.67%** and a log2 change of

$$
\log_2(1/1.2) = -0.263034
$$

for T cells at 40%, for dendritic cells at 2%, and for everything between. Five identical
effect sizes.

**Step 3 — what monocytes do.** $0.20 \times 2/1.2 = 0.333333$, a log2 change of
$+0.736966$.

**Step 4 — what is actually identified.** The difference:

$$
0.736966 - (-0.263034) = 1.000000,
$$

which is exactly $\log_2 2$. Differences of log-proportions are estimable; individual log
fold-changes are not, because they all carry the unknown $-\log_2 D$.

**Step 5 — what a test would report.** Every one of the five untouched populations at
$-0.263034$, all with the same sign and similar magnitude, and with as much statistical
confidence as the cell counts allow. A reader sees a coordinated contraction of five cell types
and a monocyte expansion. Only the second is real.

**The tell.** When several populations move by *the same* log fold-change in the same
direction, and one moves the other way, closure is the first hypothesis. Real biology rarely
produces five identical effect sizes.

</WorkedExample>

## Two further consequences

**Closure induces a correlation whatever you do.** For a multinomial the correlation between
two parts is

$$
\mathrm{corr}(p_j, p_k) = -\sqrt{\frac{p_j p_k}{(1-p_j)(1-p_k)}},
$$

which depends on nothing but the two proportions — not on how many cells were counted, and not
on any overdispersion. It is always negative. Monocytes at 20% and T cells at 40% are
correlated $-0.408248$ before any biology is allowed to act, so two populations appearing to
move in opposite directions is the null and not a finding.

**And the design effect of lesson 12 applies here too.** Real samples differ in composition by
more than multinomial sampling allows, which is a Dirichlet-multinomial with concentration
$\alpha_0$, and its intra-class correlation is $\rho = 1/(1 + \alpha_0)$. So
$\alpha_0 = 19$ gives $\rho = 0.05$, and at 500 cells per sample the design effect is 25.95 —
the same number lesson 12 derived, reached from composition rather than from expression. A
differential-abundance test that treats cells as independent has the same problem as a
differential-expression test that does, and the same fix: aggregate to one composition per
sample <Citation id="crowell2020muscat" />
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The measurement that would settle it">

Everything here follows from not knowing the denominator. If you knew how many cells of each
type there were per gram of tissue, none of this would arise — the closure map would not be
applied, and each population could be tested on its own.

That measurement exists and is not usually made: spike in a known number of foreign cells or
synthetic transcripts, or count nuclei per unit mass before dissociation, and the total becomes
estimable. Hashing gives it for free when samples are pooled, since each hashtag's cell count
is a direct readout <Citation id="stoeckius2018hashing" />.

Absent that, the honest framing is comparative rather than absolute: report ratios between
populations, which are identified, and say plainly that a decline in the others is what a single
expansion looks like. The framing that causes trouble is the one where each population gets its
own p-value <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

## Summary

- A single-cell experiment measures **shares of an arbitrary total**, so abundance is only ever
  known up to a scale factor.
- If one population changes by $c$, every other proportion is divided by the identical
  $D = 1 + (c-1)p_k$. Doubling monocytes from 20% sends five untouched populations to
  $-0.263034$ apiece, whatever their own size.
- **The ambiguity is exact**: abundances of 40, 20, 10, 8, 20, 2 and of exactly half those
  close to the same proportions, so no proportion-based statistic can tell them apart.
- What *is* identified is a difference of log-proportions: $0.736966 - (-0.263034) = 1$, which
  is $\log_2$ of the true change.
- The **CLR** removes the constraint but not the artefact: the untouched parts shift by
  $-\log c/K$, which is $-0.166667$ here rather than zero.
- Closure induces a **negative correlation** between any two parts,
  $-\sqrt{p_jp_k/((1-p_j)(1-p_k))}$, which is $-0.408248$ for 20% and 40%, before any biology.
- Overdispersed composition is Dirichlet-multinomial with $\rho = 1/(1+\alpha_0)$, so lesson
  12's design effect applies unchanged: $\alpha_0 = 19$ gives $\rho = 0.05$ and DE 25.95 at 500
  cells.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading five identical declines" difficulty="warm-up">

A study reports that after treatment, four cell types decline significantly with log2 fold
changes of −0.26, −0.26, −0.27 and −0.26, while one expands with +0.74.

(a) What is the first hypothesis?

(b) Estimate the closure factor and the expanding population's baseline share.

(c) What would you ask the authors for?

<div slot="solution">

(a) That only the expanding population changed, and the four declines are closure. Four nearly
identical effect sizes in the same direction is not what independent biology produces.

(b) The declines imply $\log_2(1/D) = -0.263$, so $D = 2^{0.263} = 1.19997$, essentially 1.2.
The difference between the expansion and the declines is $0.74 - (-0.26) = 1.00$, so the true
fold change is $2^{1.00} = 2$. Then $D = 1 + (c-1)p_k$ gives $p_k = 0.2/1 = 0.20$ — a baseline
share of **20%**, which the paper can confirm directly.

(c) The baseline proportions, so (b) can be checked; and any absolute measurement — spike-ins,
nuclei per gram, hashtag counts — that would fix the denominator. Failing that, ratios between
the four "declining" populations, which should be unchanged if closure is the explanation.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Where the artefact bites hardest" difficulty="core">

(a) Compute the closure factor for a doubling of a population at 2%, at 20% and at 50%.

(b) Convert each to the apparent log2 change of the untouched populations.

(c) A collaborator proposes avoiding the problem by only testing rare populations. Assess.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $D = 1 + (2-1)p_k$: **1.02** at 2%, **1.20** at 20%, **1.50** at 50%.

(b) $\log_2(1/D)$: **-0.028569** at 2%, **-0.263034** at 20%, **-0.584963** at 50%. A doubling
of a rare population barely perturbs anyone; a doubling of a dominant one halves everyone else's
apparent share.

(c) It half-works and creates a worse problem. Testing only rare populations does limit the
artefact those populations *cause* — but it does nothing about the artefact they *suffer*, since
a rare population's proportion is divided by $D$ whenever any abundant population moves. So the
rare types remain the most vulnerable readings in the dataset: at 2% of the sample, a genuine
change is competing with a closure shift driven by whatever the 40% population did. The
asymmetry is the point — a rare population is a weak cause and a sensitive victim.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="What the CLR leaves behind" difficulty="stretch">

Six populations, one of which doubles.

(a) Compute the CLR shift for the changed population and for each other, using the closed form.

(b) Verify the shifts sum to zero, and explain why they must.

(c) The CLR shifts are smaller than the raw ones. Does that make CLR-based testing safe?

<div slot="solution">

(a) With $K = 6$ and $c = 2$: $\Delta_k = \log_2 2 \times 5/6 = +0.833333$ and
$\Delta_j = -\log_2 2/6 = -0.166667$ for each of the other five.

(b) $0.833333 + 5(-0.166667) = 0$. They must, because the CLR of any composition sums to zero
by construction — it is a log vector with its own mean subtracted — so any change to it is a
vector of shifts summing to zero. No transform with that property can leave five parts fixed
while moving the sixth.

(c) No. Smaller is not zero, and the five untouched populations still receive an identical
$-0.166667$, which a test with enough cells will call significant just as readily. What CLR
genuinely buys is that the shift is *known in closed form* — given $K$ and an estimate of $c$
you can say exactly how much of each apparent change is closure — and that ratios between parts
are untouched. Those are real gains, and neither is "the artefact is gone". The artefact is
gone only when the denominator is measured.

</div>

</Exercise>
