## The question this lesson answers

The graph from the last lesson gets partitioned, and the partition is what the paper calls
cell types. Essentially every pipeline does this the same way — Louvain
<Citation id="blondel2008louvain" /> or its better-behaved successor Leiden
<Citation id="traag2019leiden" />, applied to the kNN graph, as introduced to this field by
PhenoGraph <Citation id="levine2015phenograph" /> and defaulted to by both major toolkits
<Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />
<Citation id="hao2021seurat" />.

Both algorithms optimise the same objective, and this lesson is about what that objective
does when you give it a graph whose right answer is known.

## What modularity measures

Modularity scores a partition by how much more internal edge weight it captures than a random
graph with the same degrees would <Citation id="newman2004modularity" />:

$$
Q = \sum_c \left[\frac{l_c}{m} - \gamma\left(\frac{d_c}{2m}\right)^2\right].
$$

The first term rewards communities with many internal edges. The second subtracts what you
would expect by chance: a community holding a fraction $d_c/2m$ of all edge endpoints would,
in a degree-preserving random graph, capture about the square of that fraction internally. The
resolution $\gamma$ weights that subtraction, and is 1 by default.

The important structural fact is in the second term. It is **quadratic in the community's
size**, so merging two communities more than doubles their combined penalty — but merging also
adds whatever edges ran between them to the first term. When a community is small relative to
the whole graph, its penalty is negligible, and any edge at all between two small communities
can make merging them profitable. That is not a failure of the optimiser. It is the objective
preferring the merged partition <Citation id="fortunato2007resolution" />.

## A graph whose right answer is not in doubt

Take $n$ complete graphs on five nodes, and join them in a ring with a single edge between
consecutive cliques. Each clique is a maximally dense community; the links between them are as
sparse as a connected graph allows. If any structure is unambiguous, this is.

Grouping $g$ consecutive cliques into each community, the modularity of a ring of forty is
exactly

$$
Q_{\gamma}(g) = 1 - \frac{1}{11g} - \frac{\gamma g}{40},
$$

a straight line in $\gamma$. At the default $\gamma = 1$ the correct partition, $g = 1$,
scores $389/440 = 0.884091$ — and $g = 2$, which fuses every clique with its neighbour, scores
$199/220 = 0.904545$. The wrong answer wins by exactly $9/440$.

The threshold is exact, and it scales. For a ring of cliques with $l = k(k-1)/2$ internal edges
each, the correct and merged partitions tie precisely at

$$
n = 2l + 2,
$$

and at that point the graph's total edge count satisfies $\sqrt{2m} = n$ exactly. For
five-node cliques $l = 10$, so the tie is at 22 rings and merging strictly wins from 24. For
six-node cliques it is 32 and 34; for ten-node cliques, 92 and 94. Bigger communities survive
longer, but every size has a graph large enough to swallow it — which in single-cell terms
means that **a rare population becomes unresolvable as the dataset grows**, however distinct it
is.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="This is the third mechanism, not a new one">

Three lessons have now each shown a rare population disappearing, by three unrelated routes.

Lesson 7: its eigenvalue is small, because variance is an average over all cells and 99% of
them are unaffected — so it does not survive dimensionality reduction.

Lesson 8: *k* is an absolute count, so a value that gives abundant populations a local view
reaches straight through a small one — so it does not survive graph construction.

This lesson: modularity's null term is quadratic in community size, so a small community's
penalty is negligible and merging it is profitable — so it does not survive clustering.

None of these is a bug and none has a software fix. They are three consequences of averaging
over a population that is mostly not the thing you are looking for. If the experiment exists to
find a rare cell type, the pipeline has to be told that, because none of its defaults will find
one on its own.

</Callout>

**Step 1 — the correct partition.** Each clique has $l_c = 10$ internal edges and degree sum
$d_c = 22$ (four nodes of degree 4, one of degree 6). With $m = 440$,

$$
Q = 40\left[\frac{10}{440} - \left(\frac{22}{880}\right)^2\right] = \frac{389}{440} = 0.884091.
$$

**Step 2 — the merged partition.** Fusing each clique with its neighbour gives 20 communities
of $l_c = 21$ internal edges and $d_c = 44$:

$$
Q = 20\left[\frac{21}{440} - \left(\frac{44}{880}\right)^2\right] = \frac{199}{220} = 0.904545.
$$

**Step 3 — the optimiser prefers the wrong one**, by $9/440 = 0.020455$. Louvain and Leiden
will return 20 communities, and they are right to: 20 communities genuinely scores higher on
the objective they were asked to maximise.

**Step 4 — how wrong that is.** The adjusted Rand index between the returned partition and the
truth is **0.604374**. Every one of the twenty reported communities is two complete cliques
fused, so half of the structure is gone — and nothing in the output says so. The partition is
clean, the modularity is high, and each returned community is internally dense.

**Step 5 — what recovers it.** Raising the resolution. The two lines cross at

$$
\gamma^{*} = \frac{20}{11} = 1.818182,
$$

past which the correct partition scores highest. Nothing in the data indicates that value; it
is a property of this graph's size, and on a real dataset it is unknown.

</WorkedExample>

## Stability is not evidence

The standard defence is to re-run at several resolutions and report the clusters that persist.
On this graph that defence fails completely.

The twenty-community answer is optimal for every $\gamma$ from $5/11$ to $20/11$ — a range
exactly **four-fold** wide. Anyone sweeping "0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5" and finding twenty
communities every time would conclude, reasonably and wrongly, that the result is robust.

What stability across resolutions actually shows is that a partition sits in the interior of a
plateau of the objective. That is a fact about the objective's landscape, not about biology, and
a wrong plateau is exactly as stable as a right one <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

Evidence that a cluster is a real population has to come from outside the objective: it is
reproducible in an independent sample, it is separable by a marker whose behaviour is
independently known, or splitting it produces sub-clusters that differ in something the
clustering did not use.

## Summary

- Clustering means maximising **modularity**, $Q = \sum_c [l_c/m - \gamma(d_c/2m)^2]$, whose
  null term is quadratic in community size.
- On a ring of forty complete five-node cliques the correct partition scores $389/440$ and the
  pairwise-merged one scores $199/220$ — higher by $9/440$. The optimiser is not failing; the
  wrong answer wins.
- The threshold is exact: cliques of $l$ internal edges tie at **$n = 2l + 2$** rings, where
  $\sqrt{2m} = n$. Merging strictly wins from $n = 24$ for five-node cliques, 34 for six-node,
  94 for ten-node. **Every community size has a graph large enough to swallow it.**
- The returned partition has ARI **0.604374** against the truth, and looks entirely healthy.
- **Stability is not evidence.** Twenty communities is optimal across a four-fold range of
  resolution, from $5/11$ to $20/11$. A sweep over the usual range returns the same wrong
  answer throughout.
- This is the third independent mechanism by which a rare population vanishes, after the small
  eigenvalue of lesson 7 and the absolute *k* of lesson 8.

<Exercise n={1} title="Locating the limit" difficulty="warm-up">

(a) A ring of complete six-node cliques: how many internal edges does each have, and at what
ring size do the correct and merged partitions tie?

(b) Verify that $\sqrt{2m} = n$ at that tie.

(c) A colleague says the problem only affects tiny communities. Respond using the ten-node case.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $l = 6 \times 5 / 2 = 15$ internal edges, so the tie is at $n = 2l + 2 = 32$ rings, and
merging strictly wins from $n = 34$.

(b) At $n = 32$ the graph has $m = 32 \times (15 + 1) = 512$ edges, so
$\sqrt{2m} = \sqrt{1024} = 32 = n$. Exactly.

(c) Ten-node cliques have $l = 45$ and tie at $n = 92$ — so a 45-edge community, which is
substantial, is unresolvable in a graph of only 92 such communities. The threshold grows with
community size, but so does any real dataset: a study with 200,000 cells and a graph of a
million edges has $\sqrt{2m} \approx 1{,}414$, so communities below roughly 700 internal edges
are at risk regardless of how distinct they are. The problem is not that communities are tiny.
It is that graphs are large.

</div>

</Exercise>

A paper reports that it swept the resolution over 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2 and 1.5, obtained twenty
clusters every time, and concludes the clustering is robust.

(a) On this lesson's graph, what would that sweep have returned?

(b) What is the ARI of that answer against the truth?

(c) State precisely what the sweep does and does not establish, and give one thing the authors
could have done instead.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Twenty communities at every one of the five settings, since all five lie inside
$(5/11,\ 20/11) = (0.454545,\ 1.818182)$.

(b) **0.604374** — the twenty returned communities are each two complete cliques fused, so the
partition is wrong in a completely systematic way while looking clean.

(c) The sweep establishes that the partition is in the interior of a plateau of the modularity
landscape, which is a statement about the objective and not about cells. It does not establish
correctness, because a wrong plateau is exactly as flat as a right one — and here the wrong
plateau is four-fold wide while the correct answer occupies everything above it. Instead: split
each reported cluster and test whether the halves differ in something the clustering never
used — a held-out gene set, a second sample, a protein measurement. On this graph that test
succeeds immediately, because each of the twenty splits cleanly into two cliques joined by a
single edge.

<Exercise n={3} title="Why raising the resolution is not the fix" difficulty="stretch">

The crossover on this graph is at $\gamma^{*} = 20/11 = 1.818182$.

(a) Why can you not simply set the resolution above the crossover on a real dataset?

(b) Suppose you set it very high anyway. What goes wrong?

(c) Leiden guarantees that every returned community is internally connected, which Louvain does
not. Does that guarantee help here?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Because $\gamma^{*}$ is a property of the graph's size and of the community you are trying
to resolve — $20/11$ here follows from forty cliques of ten edges in a graph of 440. On real
data neither the true partition nor its scale is known, so the crossover cannot be computed. A
resolution chosen without it is a guess, and the lesson's whole point is that the guess is
stable enough to feel like a finding.

(b) The same argument runs in reverse: at high enough $\gamma$ the null term dominates and
splitting becomes profitable for genuinely homogeneous communities. There is no setting that is
safe in both directions, because one number is being asked to fix a scale that varies across the
dataset — abundant and rare populations need different resolutions simultaneously, exactly as
they needed different values of *k* in lesson 8.

(c) No. Leiden's guarantee is that no returned community is internally disconnected, and every
community here is *perfectly* connected — two complete cliques joined by an edge is a connected
subgraph. The defect is in the objective, not in the optimiser's diligence, so a better
optimiser finds the wrong answer more reliably. That is worth stating plainly: on this graph
Leiden is more likely than Louvain to return exactly twenty communities.

</div>

</Exercise>
