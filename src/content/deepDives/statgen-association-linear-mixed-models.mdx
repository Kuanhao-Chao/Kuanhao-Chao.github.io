## The question this lesson answers

A genome-wide association study fits one regression per variant:

$$
y_i = \mu + g_{ij}\,\beta_j + \text{covariates} + \varepsilon_i
$$

and tests $\beta_j = 0$. That is Module 1, applied ten million times. Everything difficult
about a GWAS follows from the number ten million and from the fact that the people carrying
those genotypes are related to one another — sometimes recently and obviously, sometimes
distantly and invisibly.

Three consequences, and this lesson is about all three:

1. **Power.** With a threshold of $5\times10^{-8}$, the effects a study can find are tiny,
   and the sample size required for a given effect is a hard number worth being able to
   compute.
2. **Confounding.** If ancestry differs between cases and controls, every variant whose
   frequency differs with ancestry looks associated. This is not a subtle bias; early
   candidate-gene literature is largely made of it.
3. **Diagnosis.** The standard diagnostic, $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$, cannot tell confounding
   apart from real polygenic signal — a limitation that motivated the next lesson entirely.

## Which test a scan uses

All three tests from Module 1 are available, and the choice is made on cost. Fitting a full
model per variant across ten million variants is not possible when the null model carries
covariates and a relatedness structure; fitting the **null model once** and evaluating a
score at each variant is.

That is why score tests dominate genome scans, and why the modern tools below are organised
around exactly that split. The Wald quantities — $\hat\beta_j$ and its standard error — are
what get *reported*, because a per-variant fit produces them, but the scan itself is a score
test almost everywhere.

## Power: the sample size a variance explained demands

Under the alternative, the test statistic is non-central $\chi^2_1$ with non-centrality
$\lambda = N q^2$, where $q^2$ is the fraction of trait variance the variant explains.
Achieving power $1-\beta$ at threshold $\alpha$ requires

$$
\lambda \ge \left(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta\right)^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
N \ge \frac{\left(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta\right)^2}{q^2}
$$

At $\alpha = 5\times10^{-8}$ and 80% power, $z_{\alpha/2} = 5.451310$ and
$z_\beta = 0.841621$, so the constant is $39.60$ and

$$
\boxed{\;N \ge \frac{39.60}{q^2}\;}
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="This curriculum parameterises power by q², and here is why">

You will also see the same result written

$$
N \ge \frac{19.80}{p(1-p)\beta^2}
$$

These are **not two results**. For an additive variant on a standardised trait,

$$
q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2
$$

so substituting gives $N \ge 39.60 / \left[2p(1-p)\beta^2\right] = 19.80/\left[p(1-p)\beta^2\right]$.
The 19.80 is simply 39.60 divided by the 2 in $2p(1-p)$. The worked example below computes
both and gets the identical integer.

The $q^2$ form is the one this curriculum uses throughout, for a reason worth stating: it
makes the trade-off visible. A variant's detectability depends only on the variance it
explains, so a common variant of small effect and a rare variant of large effect that share
a $q^2$ need the same sample size. The $\beta$ form hides that behind two symbols that move
in opposite directions.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="How many people to find a variant explaining 0.1% of the variance"
  given={[
    { label: 'Threshold α', value: '5 × 10⁻⁸' },
    { label: 'Power', value: '80%' },
    { label: 'Variant', value: 'p = 0.30, β = 0.05 SD per allele' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the constant.** The two-sided normal quantile at $5\times10^{-8}$ is
$z_{\alpha/2} = 5.451310$, and 80% power needs $z_\beta = 0.841621$:

$$
\left(5.451310 + 0.841621\right)^2 = 6.292931^2 = 39.600989
$$

**Step 2 — the variance explained.**

$$
q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2 = 2(0.30)(0.70)(0.05)^2 = 0.42 \times 0.0025 = 1.05\times10^{-3}
$$

The variant explains about a tenth of one percent of the trait.

**Step 3 — the sample size, in $q^2$.**

$$
N \ge \frac{39.600989}{1.05\times10^{-3}} = 37{,}716
$$

**Step 4 — the same thing in the other notation.**

$$
N \ge \frac{19.800495}{p(1-p)\beta^2} = \frac{19.800495}{(0.30)(0.70)(0.0025)} = 37{,}716
$$

Identical, as it must be.

**Step 5 — feel the scaling.** $N$ goes as $1/q^2$, so halving the variance explained
doubles the sample. A variant explaining $10^{-4}$ of the variance — an ordinary effect size
for a complex trait — needs 396,010 people. That number is the whole reason biobanks exist.

</WorkedExample>

## Population stratification

Suppose a sample is drawn from two ancestry groups, and the trait is more common in one of
them for reasons that have nothing to do with the variant being tested — diet, environment,
ascertainment. Then **every** variant whose frequency differs between the groups will show
association, because group membership predicts both.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why structure manufactures association out of nothing">

The mechanism is easier to see with a deliberately silly example than with a real one.

Suppose you sample from two populations, and one of them eats with chopsticks far more often
than the other. Test every variant in the genome against chopstick use. Any variant whose
frequency differs between the two populations will come out associated — thousands of them —
and not one has anything to do with hands, coordination or culture. The variant is
predicting **which population the person came from**, and population predicts the trait.

The trait does not have to be cultural. Anything that differs between the groups for
non-genetic reasons will do: diet, altitude, healthcare access, or simply how the two groups
were recruited into the study.

Two features of this make it dangerous rather than merely annoying. It scales with sample
size like a real effect, so collecting more data makes the spurious associations *more*
significant rather than washing them out. And it is not confined to obviously distinct
groups: ancestry varies continuously within every population, so the same mechanism operates
in a study that looks entirely homogeneous.

What the fixes below have in common is that they give the model a way to know where each
person sits in that continuum, so that the trait's dependence on ancestry can be accounted
for before any variant is tested.

</Callout>

Nothing about this is subtle, and it does not require exotic population structure. Ancestry
varies continuously within every human population, and <Citation id="astle2009structure" />
sets out how cryptic relatedness produces the same effect without any discrete groups at all.

The classical fix is to estimate the axes of ancestry variation directly from the genotypes
and include them as covariates, which is what <Citation id="price2006eigenstrat" />
introduced. The number of axes to include is a judgement call: this curriculum uses **ten**
throughout, which is the common default for a single-ancestry cohort, and the reason to
state a number and stick to it is that the correction is only as good as the structure it
captures.

### λ_GC: the diagnostic, and its limit

<Citation id="devlin1999gc" /> proposed a simple check. Under the null, a
$\chi^2_1$ statistic has median $0.4549364\ldots$ — the exact median of the distribution,
not a rounding of it. So compare the observed median against it:

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = \frac{\mathrm{median}\left(\chi^2_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)}{0.4549364\ldots}
$$

A clean scan gives 1. Inflation gives more.

<WorkedExample
  title="Computing λ_GC, and then failing to interpret it"
  given={[
    { label: 'Statistics', value: 'nine χ² values from a scan' },
    { label: 'Values', value: '0.02, 0.11, 0.28, 0.44, 0.545924, 0.95, 1.51, 2.84, 6.10' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the median.** Nine values, so the median is the fifth: $0.545924$.

**Step 2 — divide by the null median.**

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = \frac{0.545924}{0.4549364} = 1.200000
$$

**Step 3 — the genomic-control correction.** Devlin and Roeder's proposal was to divide
every statistic by $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$. Doing so returns the median to $0.4549364$ and
$\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ to exactly 1, by construction.

**Step 4 — now try to say what caused it.** You cannot, and this is the important step.

A $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ of 1.20 is produced by uncorrected population structure. It is
*also* produced by a genuinely polygenic trait measured in a large clean sample, because
thousands of real small effects lift every test statistic slightly. The median cannot
separate a constant added to every statistic from a scaling of all of them.

Worse, the correction actively harms the second case: dividing every statistic by 1.20 when
the inflation is real signal throws away real discoveries. In modern biobank analyses
$\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ routinely exceeds 1.1 on perfectly clean data, and it rises with
sample size, which is the opposite of how a diagnostic should behave.

</WorkedExample>

## The linear mixed model

Principal components correct for the broad axes of ancestry. They do not correct for close
relatedness, for family structure, or for fine-scale population structure that no small
number of axes captures.

The mixed model handles all of it in one term. Write

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{g}\,\beta_g + \mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{K}\sigma^2_g
$$

This is exactly the model from the BLUP lesson, with one addition: a fixed effect $\beta_g$
for the variant being tested. The random effect $\mathbf{u}$ carries the relatedness
structure $\mathbf{K}$, and because it does, any correlation between the trait and the
genome-wide background is absorbed there rather than being attributed to the variant.

<Citation id="kang2010emmax" /> made this practical for association scans. The cost is
severe — a naive implementation is $O(n^3)$ per variant — and the three tools that made it
routine each attack that differently: <Citation id="loh2015bolt" /> with a Bayesian
iterative scheme that also gains power from non-infinitesimal architectures,
<Citation id="jiang2019fastgwa" /> by exploiting sparsity in the relatedness matrix, and
<Citation id="mbatchou2021regenie" /> with a two-stage ridge scheme that scales to thousands
of phenotypes at once.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The control must not contain what it is controlling for">

If $\mathbf{K}$ is built from all variants, it contains the variant under test. The random
effect then explains part of the signal the fixed effect is trying to detect — the variant
is competing against itself — and the test loses power. This is **proximal contamination**,
and <Citation id="yang2014mlm" /> is the paper that laid out this and the other pitfalls of
applying mixed models to association.

The standard fix is **leave-one-chromosome-out**: when testing a variant on chromosome $k$,
build $\mathbf{K}$ from every chromosome except $k$. Relatedness is still controlled — it is
a genome-wide property and 21 chromosomes estimate it perfectly well — while the tested
variant and everything in LD with it are excluded from the control.

The same logic explains a subtler failure: including the tested chromosome also removes
genuine local signal, so a real association can be attenuated rather than merely
under-powered. LOCO is not a refinement; without it the model answers a different question
than the one asked.

</Callout>

<Exercise n={1} title="Two variants, one sample size" difficulty="warm-up">

Variant A is common with a small effect: $p = 0.40$, $\beta = 0.04$. Variant B is rare with
a large one: $p = 0.02$, $\beta = 0.204$. (a) Compute $q^2$ for each. (b) Compute the sample
size each needs at genome-wide significance and 80% power. (c) What is the point?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Using $q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$:

$$
q^2_A = 2(0.40)(0.60)(0.04)^2 = 0.48 \times 0.0016 = 7.680000\times10^{-4}
$$

$$
q^2_B = 2(0.02)(0.98)(0.204)^2 = 0.0392 \times 0.041616 = 1.631347\times10^{-3}
$$

**(b)** $N_A \ge 39.600989 / 7.68\times10^{-4} = 51{,}564$ and
$N_B \ge 39.600989 / 1.631347\times10^{-3} = 24{,}276$.

**(c)** The rare variant needs **less than half** the sample despite being twenty times
rarer, because its effect is five times larger and $\beta$ enters squared while $p$ enters
linearly. Detectability is a property of $q^2$ alone — which is why writing the formula in
$q^2$ makes the comparison a division and writing it in $p$ and $\beta$ makes it an
argument.

The practical corollary runs the other way, though: rare variants with effects that large
are rare *as a class*, which is why genome-wide scans of common variants dominated the field
until sequencing made the rare tail accessible at all.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="What λ_GC will not tell you" difficulty="core">

A biobank analysis of height in 400,000 people reports $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.31$.
A colleague proposes dividing every test statistic by 1.31 before reporting.
(a) What would that do? (b) Is the inflation evidence of a problem? (c) What should be
reported instead?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Dividing by $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ returns the median statistic to its null value
and $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ to exactly 1. It also shrinks every genuine association by the
same factor, and at genome-wide significance that is not cosmetic: a variant at
$\chi^2 = 33$ falls to $25.2$ and drops below the 29.7168 threshold.

**(b)** No — not on its own. Height is among the most polygenic traits known, and in a
sample this size thousands of real small effects lift every statistic. A
$\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ of 1.31 is exactly what a *clean* analysis of a highly polygenic
trait at $N = 400{,}000$ looks like. The diagnostic rises with sample size under a correct
analysis, which is the opposite of what a diagnostic should do.

**(c)** The **LD score regression intercept**, which is the subject of the next lesson. It
separates the two contributions by using the relationship between a variant's test statistic
and how much LD it tags: real polygenic signal scales with LD score, confounding does not.
An intercept near 1 with a large λ_GC says the inflation is signal; an intercept well above 1
says it is not.

Report $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ if you like — it is cheap and conventional — but never act on
it, and never correct by it.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A mixed model that lost its signal" difficulty="stretch">

An analyst runs a mixed-model GWAS building $\mathbf{K}$ from all variants including the
tested one. A variant with a genuine effect that should reach $\chi^2 = 40$ comes back at
$\chi^2 = 26$. (a) Name the mechanism. (b) Does it clear genome-wide significance either
way? (c) Why does the effect get worse as the study grows, and what else does it distort?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Proximal contamination. The random effect $\mathbf{u}$ has $\mathbf{K}$ built from a
set that includes the tested variant and everything in LD with it, so part of that variant's
own contribution is absorbed into the polygenic term and never reaches the fixed effect.

**(b)** The genome-wide threshold on the $\chi^2_1$ scale is $29.7168$. At 40 the variant
clears it comfortably; at 26 it does not. The contamination is the difference between a
discovery and nothing.

**(c)** It worsens with study size for a specific reason: as $N$ grows, $\sigma^2_g$ is
estimated more precisely and the random effect becomes better at explaining the trait —
including the part attributable to the variant under test. A model that fits the background
better absorbs more of the foreground.

What else it distorts: the effect-size estimate, not only the test. $\hat\beta_g$ is biased
toward zero by the same mechanism, so a contaminated analysis feeding a polygenic score or a
Mendelian randomisation downstream propagates an attenuated effect into results that never
mention mixed models at all. That is the case for treating LOCO as correctness rather than
tuning.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A genome scan is one test from Module 1 run ten million times**, and it uses a score
  test because the expensive null model — covariates, relatedness — can then be fitted once
  and reused. Wald quantities are what get reported.
- **Sample size is $N \ge 39.60/q^2$** at $5\times10^{-8}$ and 80% power. The alternative
  form $N \ge 19.80/[p(1-p)\beta^2]$ is the identical result, since $q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$
  and $19.80 = 39.60/2$. This curriculum uses $q^2$ because detectability depends on the
  variance explained and on nothing else.
- **A variant explaining 0.1% of the variance needs 37,716 people; one explaining 0.01%
  needs 396,010.** The scaling is $1/q^2$, and it is why biobanks exist.
- **Population structure creates association at variants with no causal role**, because
  ancestry predicts both genotype and trait. Principal components correct the broad axes;
  this curriculum uses ten.
- **λ_GC divides the observed median χ² by 0.4549364…**, the exact median of χ²₁. It cannot
  distinguish confounding from polygenic signal — a λ_GC of 1.20 is reachable either way —
  and correcting by it destroys real discoveries. In biobanks it rises with sample size
  under a perfectly clean analysis.
- **The mixed model absorbs relatedness into a random effect with covariance $\mathbf{K}$**,
  and the relatedness matrix must exclude the chromosome being tested. Without LOCO the
  variant competes against itself, losing both power and an unbiased effect estimate — and
  the problem grows with sample size.
