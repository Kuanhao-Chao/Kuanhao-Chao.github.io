## The question this lesson answers

Module 1 supplied the machinery for testing whether a parameter is zero. This lesson supplies
the thing that machinery gets pointed at: a **null model for a population**, without which a
genotype count is just a number.

The null has four layers, and they build on one another:

1. **Hardy–Weinberg** says what genotype frequencies should be, given allele frequencies, in
   an idealised population. It is the assumption every genotype quality-control filter tests.
2. **Wright–Fisher** admits that populations are finite, which makes allele frequencies drift
   at a rate set by one parameter, $N_e$.
3. **The coalescent** reads that same process backwards, turning a model of frequencies into
   a model of genealogies — and explains, as a consequence, why most human variants are rare.
4. **The infinitesimal model** takes many such loci and shows that their sum is a normally
   distributed continuous trait, which is what makes the rest of this curriculum possible.

## Hardy–Weinberg: the null that makes genotype data interpretable

Take a population where allele $A$ has frequency $p$ and allele $a$ has frequency $q = 1-p$.
If gametes unite at random, a zygote's two alleles are two independent draws from that pool,
and the genotype frequencies are simply the terms of $(p+q)^2$:

$$
\underbrace{p^2}_{AA} + \underbrace{2pq}_{Aa} + \underbrace{q^2}_{aa} = 1
$$

This is the result <Citation id="hardy1908" /> published in a half-page letter, irritated that
biologists had convinced themselves dominant alleles must increase in frequency over time.
They do not. In the absence of anything acting on them, allele frequencies do not change at
all, and the genotype frequencies reach their equilibrium in **one generation**.

The five assumptions are worth naming, because a rejected test is only useful if you know
what it could be telling you: random mating, no selection, no mutation, no migration, and
infinite population size. In practice a departure at a single variant in a modern cohort is
almost never any of those things — it is a genotyping artefact, which is precisely why the
test survives as a quality-control filter.

### Testing a departure, and the degrees of freedom

Three genotype counts give two free proportions. One more is spent estimating $p$ from the
same data, so the test has **one degree of freedom, not two** — an instance of the general
rule from Module 1 that estimating a parameter costs a degree of freedom in the statistic
that tests it.

<WorkedExample
  title="The same test on a common and a rare variant"
  given={[
    { label: 'Cohort', value: '1,000 individuals' },
    { label: 'Variant A (common)', value: '298 / 489 / 213' },
    { label: 'Variant B (rare)', value: '940 / 56 / 4' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Variant A.** The minor allele frequency is
$q = (2 \times 213 + 489)/2000 = 0.4575$, so the expected counts are

$$
(p^2 n,\; 2pqn,\; q^2 n) = (294.306,\; 496.388,\; 209.306)
$$

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(298-294.306)^2}{294.306} + \frac{(489-496.388)^2}{496.388}
+ \frac{(213-209.306)^2}{209.306} = 0.2215
$$

On 1 df that is $p = 0.6379$. The exact test of <Citation id="wigginton2005hwe" /> gives
$p = 0.6557$. **They agree**, because every expected count is in the hundreds.

**Variant B.** Now $q = (2 \times 4 + 56)/2000 = 0.0320$, and the expected counts are

$$
(937.024,\; 61.952,\; 1.024)
$$

The third cell is the problem: Hardy–Weinberg expects **one** minor homozygote, and four were
seen.

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(940-937.024)^2}{937.024} + \frac{(56-61.952)^2}{61.952}
+ \frac{(4-1.024)^2}{1.024} = 9.2303
$$

On 1 df that is $p = 2.380\times10^{-3}$ — apparently a clear rejection. The exact test gives
$p = 1.568\times10^{-2}$, **6.6 times larger**, and no longer clears a 0.01 filter.

The entire discrepancy comes from the last term. A cell with an expectation of 1.024
contributes $(4-1.024)^2/1.024 = 8.65$ of the 9.23, and the χ² approximation assumes that
cell's count is roughly normal. It is not; it is a count of about one.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="The direction of the error is the dangerous part">

For rare variants the χ² test returns a **smaller** $p$-value than the truth. It
over-rejects, so a Hardy–Weinberg quality-control filter set at some fixed threshold discards
rare variants more aggressively than it was designed to — and rare variants are exactly the
ones a sequencing study was run to find. This is the sparse-count failure named in Module 1,
in the place it does the most damage.

Two consequences follow. Use the exact test, which is cheap and has no lower limit. And
never apply a Hardy–Weinberg filter to **cases only** in a case-control study: a variant with
a genuinely large effect on disease risk departs from Hardy–Weinberg in cases *because it
works*, and filtering on that discards the real findings along with the artefacts.

</Callout>

## Wright–Fisher: drift is sampling noise that never averages out

Hardy–Weinberg assumed an infinite population. Drop that, and each generation becomes a
finite sample: $2N$ gametes drawn from the previous generation's pool. The Wright–Fisher
model is exactly this, and the count of $A$ alleles in the next generation is binomial:

$$
X_{t+1} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(2N,\; p_t), \qquad p_{t+1} = X_{t+1}/2N
$$

Two properties follow immediately from the binomial. The expectation is unchanged,
$\mathbb{E}[p_{t+1}] = p_t$ — drift has **no direction**. But the variance is not zero:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(p_{t+1} \mid p_t) = \frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N}
$$

so the frequency wanders, and because $p = 0$ and $p = 1$ are absorbing, every variant is
eventually lost or fixed. <Citation id="wright1931drift" /> is where this becomes a
quantitative theory of populations rather than an observation.

The consequence that matters for the rest of the curriculum is the decay of heterozygosity.
Each generation, two alleles drawn from the sample are identical by descent with probability
$1/2N_e$, so

$$
H_t = H_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{2N_e}\right)^{t} \approx H_0 e^{-t/2N_e}
$$

**Real populations are not Wright–Fisher populations**, which is what $N_e$ is for: it is the
size of the idealised population that would drift at the same rate as the real one. Unequal
sex ratios, variance in offspring number, and past bottlenecks all make $N_e$ smaller than
the census size, often by an order of magnitude.

<WorkedExample
  title="How fast does a population forget?"
  given={[
    { label: 'Effective size Nₑ', value: '10,000' },
    { label: 'Starting heterozygosity', value: '0.5 (a variant at q = 0.5)' },
    { label: 'Elapsed time', value: '1,000 generations' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — heterozygosity retained.**

$$
\frac{H_{1000}}{H_0} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{20{,}000}\right)^{1000} = 0.951228
$$

About **95.1%** survives. Drift is slow at this population size — a thousand generations is
roughly 25,000 years of human history, and it costs five percent of the variation.

**Step 2 — the half-life.** Set the ratio to one half and solve:

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\log 0.5}{\log(1 - 1/2N_e)} = 13{,}862.6 \text{ generations}
$$

which is $2N_e \log 2 = 13{,}862.9$ to within the discreteness of the model. **The half-life
of genetic variation is proportional to $N_e$**, and that single fact is why $N_e$ is the
parameter population genetics is organised around.

**Step 3 — the spread in frequency.** After 1,000 generations the standard deviation of $p$
about its unchanged mean of 0.5 is

$$
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(p_t)} = \sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)\left[1 - (1 - 1/2N_e)^t\right]} = 0.11042
$$

So a variant that started at 50% is typically somewhere between 39% and 61% — the mean has
not moved, but any particular population has.

</WorkedExample>

A variant's chance of eventually fixing is just its current frequency, $\Pr(\text{fix}) = p$ —
a striking result that follows from $\mathbb{E}[p_t] = p_0$ and the fact that the process
must end at 0 or 1. <Citation id="kimura1969fixation" /> derived how long that takes on
average, and the answer is long: for a variant currently at 1% in a population of
$N_e = 10{,}000$, conditional on fixing, roughly $4N_e$ generations.

## The coalescent: the same process, read backwards

Everything above follows allele frequencies forwards in time. Almost every question about
real data runs the other way: you have a sample of sequences *now* and want to know about
their shared history. <Citation id="kingman1982coalescent" /> showed that the Wright–Fisher
model, viewed backwards from a sample of $n$ sequences, becomes a simple continuous process.

With $k$ lineages remaining, any particular pair coalesces in the previous generation with
probability $1/2N_e$, and there are $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs. So the waiting time in state $k$ is
exponential, with

$$
\mathbb{E}[T_k] = \frac{2N_e}{\binom{k}{2}} = \frac{4N_e}{k(k-1)}
$$

Two consequences of that lopsided shape run through the rest of this curriculum.

**Most variation is rare.** A mutation falling on one of the many short branches near the
tips of the tree is carried by very few samples. Since most of the tree's *branches* are near
the tips even though most of its *depth* is at the root, the site frequency spectrum is
dominated by rare variants — which is the observed fact that makes rare-variant association
testing a separate discipline.

**Sampling more people does not buy more history.** The total expected depth is
$4N_e(1 - 1/n)$, so going from 20 sequences to 20,000 raises it from $0.95 \times 4N_e$ to
$0.99995 \times 4N_e$. More samples buy more *branches* — more rare variants, better frequency
estimates — but essentially no additional depth.

Mutations fall on this tree at a constant rate, which gives an estimator of
$\theta = 4N_e\mu$ from the number of segregating sites. Because the expected total branch
length is $4N_e \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} 1/i$, <Citation id="watterson1975" /> obtained

$$
\hat\theta_W = \frac{S}{a_1}, \qquad a_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i}
$$

## Tajima's D: two estimators that should agree

There is a second, entirely different estimator of the same $\theta$: the average number of
differences between two sequences picked at random, $\hat\theta_\pi$. Under neutrality and
constant population size both estimate $\theta$, so their difference should be zero.
<Citation id="tajima1989" /> turned that difference into a test by dividing it by its own
standard error — the same construction as any of the statistics in Module 1:

$$
D = \frac{\hat\theta_\pi - \hat\theta_W}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_\pi - \hat\theta_W)}}
$$

The two estimators weight the frequency spectrum differently, and that is what makes the
comparison informative. $\hat\theta_W$ counts a site once however rare it is;
$\hat\theta_\pi$ weights it by $2f(1-f)$, so it barely notices a singleton. **An excess of
rare variants therefore pushes $\hat\theta_W$ up while leaving $\hat\theta_\pi$ alone**, and
$D$ goes negative.

<WorkedExample
  title="Tajima's D for a sequenced locus"
  given={[
    { label: 'Sequences n', value: '50' },
    { label: 'Segregating sites S', value: '120' },
    { label: 'Mean pairwise differences π', value: '15.5' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the harmonic number.**

$$
a_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{49}\frac{1}{i} = 4.479205
$$

**Step 2 — Watterson's estimator.**

$$
\hat\theta_W = \frac{S}{a_1} = \frac{120}{4.479205} = 26.790466
$$

**Step 3 — the difference.** $\hat\theta_\pi = 15.5$, so

$$
\hat\theta_\pi - \hat\theta_W = 15.5 - 26.790466 = -11.290466
$$

**Step 4 — standardise.** Tajima's variance estimate uses two constants derived from $n$,
here $e_1 = 0.027613$ and $e_2 = 0.003705$, giving
$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}} = e_1 S + e_2 S(S-1)$:

$$
D = \frac{-11.290466}{\sqrt{0.027613(120) + 0.003705(120)(119)}} = -1.505723
$$

**Step 5 — read it.** $D = -1.51$ says there are far more segregating sites than the
pairwise diversity can account for: the locus carries an excess of rare variants. Two
explanations produce that, and the statistic cannot distinguish them — a **population
expansion**, which adds recent branches to every locus in the genome, or **purifying
selection** at this locus, which removes deleterious variants before they become common.

Which one it is depends on whether the rest of the genome looks the same. That is the
practical rule: a genome-wide negative $D$ is demography, a locus-specific one is selection.

</WorkedExample>

## Fisher 1918: how Mendelian genes make continuous traits

One question hangs over everything so far. Mendelian inheritance is discrete — alleles come
in whole units — yet height, blood pressure and every trait this curriculum cares about are
continuous and close to normally distributed. For fifteen years after Mendel's rediscovery
this was taken as evidence that the two could not both be true.

<Citation id="fisher1918infinitesimal" /> resolved it in one move: let the trait be the sum
of contributions from **many loci, each of small effect**. The central limit theorem does the
rest, and the sum of many small independent contributions is normal regardless of the
discreteness of each one. Every one of Mendel's critics' observations survives; so does
Mendel.

The paper does far more than that, and the part this curriculum uses is the second half. If
a trait is such a sum, then the covariance between two relatives is determined by the
proportion of alleles they share:

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\text{relatives}) = 2\Phi\, V_A + \ldots
$$

where $\Phi$ is the kinship coefficient. Full siblings share half their alleles on average
and so covary at $\tfrac{1}{2}V_A$; parent and offspring the same; identical twins at $V_A$
plus the whole dominance component. **This is the mechanism every heritability estimate
runs on** — twin studies, pedigree models and the genomic relatedness matrix alike all
estimate $V_A$ by comparing observed covariance against expected sharing.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why the infinitesimal model survives being false">

No trait is really controlled by infinitely many loci of infinitesimal effect. The model is
a limit, and the honest question is why predictions built on it work anyway.

The answer is that the model's *predictions* depend on the sum, not on the loci. What it
asserts is that the distribution of breeding values within a family is normal with a variance
that does not depend on the parents' own values — and <Citation id="barton2017infinitesimal" />
shows this holds under far weaker conditions than infinitely many loci, requiring only that
no single locus dominates. Modern GWAS confirmed the premise directly: for height, thousands
of variants each explain a small fraction of a percent, which is what "many loci of small
effect" looks like when you can finally see them.

Where it does break is where a single variant is large — a monogenic disorder, or a
major-effect locus segregating in a breeding population. There the infinitesimal
approximation fails, and the failure is visible as a non-normal trait distribution.

</Callout>

A cohort of 1,500 people is genotyped at a variant with counts 1,470 / 24 / 6. Compute the

Note also that 158.6678 of the statistic is almost entirely the third term, worth 154.9 on
its own, whose expected count is 0.216. A χ² statistic dominated by a cell expecting a
fifth of an observation should never be reported as a χ² statistic; the exact test's
$1.668\times10^{-8}$ is the defensible number, and it is still overwhelming.

**(a)** Using $t_{1/2} = \log 0.5 / \log(1 - 1/2N_e)$:

| $N_e$ | half-life | $2N_e\log 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 500 | 692.8 | 693.1 |
| 10,000 | 13,862.6 | 13,862.9 |

The approximation is excellent, and it is exact in the limit — the two disagree only by the
difference between $\log(1-x)$ and $-x$.

**(b)** Replace $0.5$ with $0.9$: 105.3 generations at $N_e = 500$, and 2,107.2 generations
at $N_e = 10{,}000$. Note that losing the *first* 10% takes about 15% of the half-life, not
10% of it — the decay is exponential, so early losses are fastest.

**(c)** Solving $105.3$ generations $\to 100$ gives $N_e \approx 475$. The claim is
therefore a statement about effective size, and a fairly precise one. It is worth adding
that this is a small number: a census population of several thousand can easily have
$N_e$ in the hundreds, and it is $N_e$ that sets the rate.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Reading two loci with the same S" difficulty="stretch">

Two loci are sequenced in the same 20 individuals. Both show $S = 45$ segregating sites.
Locus 1 has $\pi = 12.4$ and locus 2 has $\pi = 8.2$. Compute Tajima's D for each and say
what distinguishes them.

<div slot="solution">

Both share $a_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{19} 1/i = 3.547740$ and therefore the same Watterson estimate:

$$
\hat\theta_W = \frac{45}{3.547740} = 12.684133
$$

The two constants for $n = 20$ are $e_1 = 0.024396$ and $e_2 = 0.004508$, so the denominator
is $\sqrt{0.024396(45) + 0.004508(45)(44)} = 3.1661$ for both loci.

$$
D_1 = \frac{12.4 - 12.684133}{3.1661} = -0.089741,
\qquad
D_2 = \frac{8.2 - 12.684133}{3.1661} = -1.416281
$$

**Locus 1 is indistinguishable from neutral.** $D_1$ is the gap between the two estimators
measured in standard errors, so they agree to within a tenth of one — which is exactly what
the neutral constant-size model predicts. (In $\theta$ units the gap is $0.284133$ against
an estimate of $12.684133$, about 2%; the two framings are different quantities and it is
worth not mixing them.)

**Locus 2 has an excess of rare variants.** Same number of segregating sites, but they are
carried by far fewer individuals each, so pairwise diversity is low while the site count is
not.

The instructive part is that $S$ alone cannot tell these apart, and neither can $\pi$ alone
without knowing what to compare it to. Only the discrepancy between two estimators of the
same quantity carries the information — which is the same idea as the three tests in Module
1, used in the opposite direction: there, agreement was assumed and the discrepancy was
error; here, the discrepancy is the signal.

Two cautions on interpreting $D_2$. Its null distribution is not standard normal — it is
bounded and skewed, so significance must come from simulation or from tabulated critical
values rather than from $\pm 1.96$. And a single locus cannot separate selection from
demography; that requires knowing whether the rest of the genome shows the same thing.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Hardy–Weinberg** fixes all three genotype frequencies from one allele frequency and is
  reached in a single generation of random mating. Its test has one degree of freedom because
  the allele frequency is estimated from the same data.
- **Use the exact test for rare variants.** The χ² approximation over-rejects when the
  minor-homozygote expectation is small — 6.6-fold in the worked example — which is the
  Module 1 sparse-count failure landing on the variants a sequencing study exists to find.
  And never filter on Hardy–Weinberg in cases only: a real risk variant departs from it
  *because* it is real.
- **Drift is directionless but not harmless.** $\mathbb{E}[p_{t+1}] = p_t$, yet variance
  accumulates until every variant is lost or fixed. Heterozygosity decays with a half-life of
  $2N_e\log 2$ — 13,863 generations at $N_e = 10{,}000$, 693 at $N_e = 500$.
- **The coalescent is Wright–Fisher read backwards**, with $\mathbb{E}[T_k] = 4N_e/k(k-1)$.
  The final join takes 53% of the depth of a 20-sample genealogy; total depth is
  $4N_e(1-1/n)$, so more sequences buy more branches and essentially no more history. This
  branch-heavy, shallow shape is why most variants are rare.
- **Tajima's D is a discrepancy between two estimators of $\theta$** that weight the
  frequency spectrum differently. Negative means an excess of rare variants; whether that is
  expansion or purifying selection depends on whether the whole genome shows it.
- **The infinitesimal model** makes many small Mendelian effects sum to a normal trait, and
  gives the covariance between relatives as a function of allele sharing. That relation is
  what every heritability estimate in Module 3 measures.
