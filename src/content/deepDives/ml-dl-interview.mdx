## What this curriculum is for

An interview answer has to work at several resolutions. The first sentence must be useful
to an interviewer who stops you after thirty seconds. The next minute should expose the
intuition. If the interviewer keeps digging, the assumptions, derivation, tensor shapes,
failure modes and engineering trade-offs must still hold together.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Prepare a zoom lens, not a script">
Do not memorize one paragraph per concept. Prepare a compact claim you can defend, then a
sequence of increasingly precise layers. “Regularization discourages solutions that fit
noise” is a reasonable opening. A senior answer can then distinguish explicit penalties,
implicit optimization bias, data augmentation and early stopping, and explain when each
fails.
</Callout>

This course is organized around that zoom lens. The mathematical sequence is informed by
*Mathematics for Machine Learning* <Citation id="deisenroth2020mml" /> and probabilistic
machine learning <Citation id="murphy2022pml" />. Classical modeling follows the applied
discipline of *An Introduction to Statistical Learning* <Citation id="james2023isl" />;
the neural-network sequence uses the primary literature and the *Deep Learning* reference
<Citation id="goodfellow2016deep" />. The modular shape also follows the practical lesson
boundaries used by the current Google ML curriculum <Citation id="google2026mlcc" />.

## Choose a study path

<WorkedExample
  title="Turn fourteen days into an interview loop"
  given={[
    { label: 'Available time', value: '14 days × 90 min/day' },
    { label: 'Current target', value: 'Senior ML engineer' },
    { label: 'Weakest rounds', value: 'metrics and system design' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>
The available budget is $14\times90=1{,}260$ minutes, or 21 hours. With one 90-minute
session per day and every third session reserved for synthesis, that gives fourteen primary
sessions, including four designated synthesis sessions.

Start with the **Core** filter rather than reading every specialist question. Spend the
first pass speaking each 30-second answer aloud and mark it `Confident`, `Learning`, or
`Review again`. On the second pass, filter to the last two states and work the derivations
or examples without looking. Reserve every third session for synthesis: compare two
methods, diagnose a failure, or design a complete system. The filter therefore becomes a
feedback loop rather than a completion counter.
</WorkedExample>

- **Seven-day rapid review:** the 72 `Must know` questions from the first ten foundational
  lessons, spoken aloud, plus selected coding and design drills.
- **Two-week core review:** `Must know` and `Common`, with derivations for weak topics.
- **Four-week complete review:** every published question, including specialist follow-ups.

The question styles are original. External interview guides are used only to check that
the course represents real interview rounds rather than becoming a list of textbook
definitions <Citation id="huyen2022mlinterviews" />.

## Before you begin

<Exercise title="Build a baseline answer">
Choose one familiar concept—cross-validation, regularization, or gradient descent—and
record a thirty-second spoken answer. Then write the assumptions you omitted.
<div slot="solution">
A useful baseline names the purpose, mechanism and one failure mode. For cross-validation:
“It estimates performance across repeated held-out partitions when one validation split is
too noisy. The split unit must match deployment—grouped or temporal data cannot be shuffled
as IID—and the final test set remains untouched.” Missing assumptions become the first
items in your review queue.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Audit the dimensions">
For a batch $X\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times128}$ and weights
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times10}$, state the logit shape and the gradient shape for $W$.
<div slot="solution">
$XW\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times10}$. Because a parameter gradient has the same shape as its
parameter, $\nabla_WL\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times10}$. Saying both aloud catches many
backpropagation mistakes before any algebra begins.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Correct an underspecified question">
An interviewer asks, “Is accuracy a good metric?” Give the clarification you would ask
before answering.
<div slot="solution">
Ask about class prevalence, error costs, whether a threshold must be selected, and whether
the deployment distribution matches the evaluation set. Accuracy is meaningful only after
those facts make the costs of false positives and false negatives interpretable.
</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

Use the hub as a map and the question bank as a feedback system. Lead with a defensible
thirty-second answer, then expose intuition, assumptions, mathematics, evidence and
trade-offs as the interviewer asks for more depth. The complete 23-lesson track stays
linkable by concept, role, question type and confidence so review follows your gaps rather
than a generic checklist.
