## The question this lesson answers

Once the principal components are computed, the expression matrix is set aside and never
touched again. What the rest of the pipeline consumes is a graph: each cell joined to its *k*
nearest neighbours in PC space. Clustering partitions that graph
<Citation id="levine2015phenograph" />, UMAP lays it out
<Citation id="mcinnes2018umap" />, and label transfer walks it
<Citation id="stuart2019seurat3" />. Every one of them is a function of the graph and of
nothing else.

So the graph is a ceiling. A structure it fails to represent cannot be recovered by any later
step, because no later step can see the data. And it is built from two numbers — how many
dimensions, and *k* — that are almost never reported and almost always left at their defaults
<Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />
<Citation id="hao2021seurat" />.

This lesson measures what those two numbers do.

## Distances stop distinguishing

The reason a graph built on raw genes fails is not that the signal is weak. It is that in
enough dimensions, *every pair of points is about equally far apart*.

Take a query point and a cloud of others, and measure the **relative contrast**

$$
\frac{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}{d_{\min}}.
$$

In two dimensions this is enormous — the nearest point is far nearer than the farthest. As
dimensions are added, each contributes its own independent squared difference, and by the law
of large numbers every distance converges on the same value. The numerator grows like
$\sqrt{d}$ while the denominator grows like $d$, so the contrast decays as roughly
$d^{-1/2}$ and the notion of "nearest" empties out
<Citation id="beyer1999nearest" />
<Citation id="aggarwal2001distance" />.

For a thousand points the contrast is 1.14 at thirty dimensions and **0.09 at two thousand**:
the most distant point in the cloud is 9% further away than the closest one. Nothing is near
anything.

## What that does to the graph

Contrast is the mechanism; the graph is the consequence, and the consequence can be measured
directly. Label every cell with the type it truly belongs to, build the graph, and count the
fraction of edges that join two cells of the same type. Call it **purity**. Its floor is the
rate you would get by joining cells at random — with $C$ equal types among $n$ cells,

$$
\Pr(\text{same label by chance}) = \frac{n/C - 1}{n - 1}.
$$

Now the experiment that makes the point. Ninety cells, three types of thirty, separated by
three standard deviations — in **two** dimensions. Every further dimension is pure noise: it
contains no information about type whatsoever, and the two informative dimensions are
untouched no matter how many noise dimensions are added beside them.

That is the result worth carrying: **adding columns that contain nothing degrades the graph
almost to chance.** Not because the biology got weaker — it is bit-for-bit the same — but
because the noise dimensions each contribute distance, and in aggregate they swamp the two
dimensions that carry the signal.

This is the argument for running PCA before building the graph, and it is a quantitative one
rather than a matter of speed.

**Step 1 — the chance floor.** Three equal types among ninety cells: of the eighty-nine other
cells, twenty-nine share your label, so

$$
\frac{29}{89} = 0.325843,
$$

**32.58%**. A graph at that purity contains no information at all.

**Step 2 — the best case.** Built on the two informative dimensions alone, purity is
**96.4%** — headroom above chance of 63.82 percentage points.

**Step 3 — the raw-gene case.** Built on all two thousand dimensions, purity is **41.5%**.
The headroom is 8.89 points, against 63.82. So

$$
1 - \frac{8.89}{63.82} = 0.861
$$

— **86.1%** of what the graph knew has been destroyed, by dimensions containing nothing.

**Step 4 — what PCA recovers.** Built on thirty dimensions, purity is **79.7%**, a headroom
of 47.12 points. Against the 8.89 it started from and the 63.82 available:

$$
\frac{47.12 - 8.89}{63.82 - 8.89} = 0.696,
$$

so reducing to thirty components recovers **69.6%** of what the noise destroyed.

**What this does not say.** It does not say thirty is the right number — that was lesson 7's
question, and the answer there was that the noise floor moves with the shape of the matrix.
What it says is that the reduction step is not a convenience. It is the difference between a
graph that carries most of the structure and one that is a quarter of the way from chance.

</WorkedExample>

## What k trades

The second parameter is *k*, and it is a bias–variance choice in disguise.

Small *k* gives a graph whose edges are almost all correct, because a cell's very nearest
neighbours are the ones most likely to be its own type — but the graph is sparse and easily
fragmented, so a single population can break into several disconnected pieces that clustering
will report as several types.

Large *k* gives a well-connected graph whose edges are increasingly wrong. At thirty
dimensions, purity falls from 85.9% at $k = 3$ to 79.7% at $k = 10$ and 65.6% at $k = 30$ —
because reaching further from each cell eventually reaches past its own population.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="k is measured in cells, and populations are not the same size">

The trap is that *k* is an absolute count while the thing it should adapt to is relative.

Set $k = 15$ in a dataset whose smallest real population has 20 cells, and every cell in that
population is joined to three quarters of its own kind plus whatever else is closest —
the graph cannot represent it as a separate group because *k* is comparable to its size. The
same $k = 15$ in a population of 5,000 reaches only its immediate locality and represents it
perfectly.

So a single *k* is simultaneously too large for the rare populations and too small for the
abundant ones, and the rare populations are usually the reason the experiment was run. This is
the third mechanism in three lessons by which a rare cell type disappears — lesson 7 showed its
eigenvalue is small because variance averages over all cells, and the next lesson shows that
modularity maximisation merges it outright <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

## Summary

- After PCA the pipeline consumes **only the kNN graph**. Clustering, UMAP and label transfer
  are all functions of it, so its quality caps everything downstream.
- **Distances concentrate.** Relative contrast decays as about $d^{-1/2}$: at 2,000 dimensions
  the farthest of a thousand points is 9% further than the nearest, against 114% at thirty.
- Graph quality is measurable as **purity** — the fraction of edges joining cells of one type —
  against a chance floor of $(n/C - 1)/(n - 1)$, which is 32.58% for three equal types of thirty.
- **Uninformative dimensions destroy the graph.** With the signal held bit-for-bit identical,
  purity falls 96.4% → 41.5% as noise dimensions are added: **86.1%** of its excess over chance.
  Reducing to thirty components recovers **69.6%** of that.
- **k trades correctness against connectivity**, and because it is an absolute count it is
  always wrong for populations of different sizes at once.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading the floor" difficulty="warm-up">

A dataset has 90 cells.

(a) What is the chance purity if they form 2 equal types? 6 equal types? 9 equal types?

(b) A graph on this dataset scores 41.5% purity and the dataset has 3 equal types. How much of
the available headroom above chance does it hold?

(c) A colleague reports 55% purity as evidence their graph is good. What do you need to know
before agreeing?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $(n/C - 1)/(n - 1)$ with $n = 90$: two types give $44/89 = 49.44\%$, six give
$14/89 = 15.73\%$, nine give $9/89 = 10.11\%$.

(b) Chance is $29/89 = 32.58\%$ and the ceiling is 100%, so the headroom is 67.42 points and
the graph holds $41.5 - 32.58 = 8.92$ of them, or about **13%**.

(c) The number of types, and their sizes. At two types, 55% purity is *worse than chance*
(49.44% is free). At nine types it is excellent. Purity is uninterpretable without its floor,
and the floor depends entirely on the composition — which is also why purity cannot be compared
between datasets.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Choosing k against a rare population" difficulty="core">

At thirty dimensions the purity of this dataset is 85.9% at $k = 3$, 84.0% at $k = 5$, 79.7%
at $k = 10$, 72.9% at $k = 20$ and 65.6% at $k = 30$.

(a) Describe the trend and give the mechanism.

(b) Given only these numbers, why can you not conclude that $k = 3$ is the right choice?

(c) The dataset also contains a population of 25 cells that you care about. What does that
imply for $k$, and what does it imply for the populations of 5,000?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Purity falls monotonically with *k*, from 85.9% to 65.6% — a loss of 20.3 points. Each
additional neighbour is further away than the last, and past some radius the next-nearest cell
is more likely to belong to another population than to your own.

(b) Because purity measures only edge correctness, not whether the graph is *connected* enough
to represent a population as one object. At $k = 3$ a population can fragment into several
disconnected components, and clustering will report those as separate cell types — an error
purity cannot see, because every one of those edges is correct. Optimising purity alone drives
$k$ towards 1, which is obviously wrong.

(c) For the 25-cell population, $k$ must be comfortably below 25 or its cells are forced to
connect outside it — $k = 10$ already reaches 40% of the way through it. For the 5,000-cell
populations, $k = 10$ is a very local view and they will be represented well but may fragment.
No single $k$ serves both, which is an argument for checking that a reported population
survives a range of $k$ rather than for finding the best one.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Why the reduction cannot be skipped" difficulty="stretch">

A collaborator proposes skipping PCA, arguing that principal components discard information and
the kNN graph should be built on all 2,000 genes so that nothing is lost.

(a) Address the argument on its own terms: is it true that PCA discards information?

(b) Use the purity numbers to say what the proposal costs.

(c) Give the circumstance under which the collaborator would nevertheless be right, and say what
you would measure to find out.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Yes, strictly. Projecting onto thirty components discards everything orthogonal to them,
and if a real biological axis lies in the discarded subspace it is gone. The argument is not
wrong about the mechanism; it is wrong about the balance.

(b) The full-gene graph scores 41.5% purity against a 32.58% floor — it holds 8.89 of 63.82
available points. The thirty-dimensional graph holds 47.12. So the proposal discards nothing in
principle and destroys 69.6% of the recoverable structure in practice, because the 1,998
uninformative dimensions contribute distance without contributing signal, and distance is all
the graph sees.

(c) When the discarded subspace is not uninformative — that is, when the structure of interest
is genuinely low-variance and PCA drops it. A rare population is exactly this case: lesson 7
showed its eigenvalue is small because variance averages over all cells. To find out, measure
whether the population of interest survives: cluster at several component counts and check
whether it appears at all of them. If it appears only without reduction, the collaborator is
right for this dataset — and the answer is then not "skip PCA" but "use a reduction that does
not rank by global variance".

</div>

</Exercise>
