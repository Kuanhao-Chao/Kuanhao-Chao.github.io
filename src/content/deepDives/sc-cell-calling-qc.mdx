## The question this lesson answers

The instrument returns several hundred thousand barcodes and perhaps five thousand of them are
cells. Deciding which, and then deciding which of those to keep, is the step everyone describes
in one sentence of a methods section and nobody reports the consequences of.

The framing that causes the trouble is that QC is *hygiene* — a neutral tidying-up before the
real work. It is not neutral. Every threshold encodes a claim about what a good cell looks
like, and cells of different types look different in exactly the properties being thresholded.
A filter that removes 15% of cells has not removed 15% of each type; it has removed most of
one type and none of another, and which is which is decided by the threshold, not by the
biology.

## The knee, and why it is not one knee

Rank every barcode by its total UMI count and plot rank against depth on log axes. Real cells
sit on a high plateau at the left, empty droplets on a low one at the right, and the drop
between them is the **knee**. The traditional method picks the knee and calls everything above
it a cell <Citation id="macosko2015dropseq" /><Citation id="zheng2017chromium" />.

This works when there is one population of cells and they are all much bigger than the
droplets' background. It fails whenever a tissue contains cells of genuinely different sizes —
which is most tissues. Lymphocytes carry an order of magnitude less RNA than hepatocytes;
platelets, granulocytes and many neurons are similarly light. Their plateau sits *below* where
the knee is usually drawn, and no single horizontal line separates them from the soup.

Figure 1 makes this concrete with a mixture whose composition is known exactly: 100,000
barcodes made of 4,000 large cells, 6,000 small cells and 90,000 empty droplets, with depths
Gamma-distributed about means of 5,000, 400 and 60 UMIs. Because the depth at each rank is
just the corresponding quantile of that mixture, the curve is computed rather than simulated,
and every retention rate quoted below follows from it.

The modern answer abandons the threshold. **EmptyDrops** estimates the ambient profile from the
very low-count barcodes — which are unambiguously soup — and then tests every remaining barcode
against it: not *is this barcode deep enough*, but *is this barcode's expression profile
different from the soup* <Citation id="lun2019emptydrops" />. A small cell with 400 UMIs of a
distinctive profile is retained; an empty droplet with 400 UMIs of soup is not. That is exactly
the discrimination a depth cutoff cannot make, and it is the same ambient profile that lesson 3
used for contamination correction <Citation id="young2020soupx" />.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Ask what the filter would do to a cell type you care about">

The useful habit is not to ask "is this threshold standard?" but "what fraction of each cell
type does it remove?"

That question is answerable. Annotate first with permissive filters, then look at what a
proposed threshold would delete, broken down by cluster. If a filter removes 3% of every
cluster, it is doing what QC is supposed to do. If it removes 90% of one cluster and 2% of the
rest, it is not a quality filter — it is a decision to exclude a cell type, and it should be
reported as one.

This inverts the usual order, which is why it is rarely done. But QC-then-cluster hides
precisely the information needed to judge the QC.

</Callout>

## The mitochondrial threshold is a cell-type filter

The standard reasoning: a dying cell's membrane becomes permeable, cytoplasmic mRNA leaks out,
mitochondrial mRNA is retained inside the mitochondria, so the mitochondrial *share* rises.
Filter cells above 5% or 10% and you remove the dying ones <Citation id="ilicic2016lowquality" />.

The reasoning is sound and the threshold is not, because baseline mitochondrial share is a
property of the cell type. Mitochondrial content tracks energy demand, and it varies across
tissues by a factor of ten: cardiomyocytes, hepatocytes, kidney proximal tubule cells and
skeletal muscle sit far above 10% when perfectly healthy, while most lymphocytes sit below 5%
<Citation id="osorio2021mito" />.

Applying one number across all of them does not remove the dying cells. It removes the aerobic
ones.

The fix is not a different number but a different unit: set the threshold **per cluster**,
relative to that cluster's own distribution, so that "unusually high mitochondrial share *for a
cardiomyocyte*" is what gets removed. That again requires clustering before filtering.

**Step 1 — the headline number.** Filters compose multiplicatively on what reaches them:

$$
10{,}000 \to 9{,}600 \to 9{,}312 \to 8{,}567 \to 8{,}139,
$$

so **81.4%** of cells survive. That is the number that appears in the methods section, and it
sounds unremarkable.

**Step 2 — where the losses fall.** The 400 cells lost to the UMI filter are not a random 4%.
From Figure 1, a 500-UMI cutoff keeps 99.92% of a 5,000-UMI population and 26.50% of a
400-UMI one. If the small population was 15% of the sample — 1,500 cells — then it loses

$$
1{,}500 \times 0.734974 = 1{,}102
$$

cells on its own, which is more than the 400 the filter is recorded as removing. The
arithmetic only reconciles if the small population is smaller than assumed: at a 4% overall
loss, the small cells can be at most $400/0.734974 = 544$ of the original 10,000. **The filter's
own retention rate tells you the composition it implies**, and if that contradicts what the
tissue is known to contain, the filter is deleting a population.

**Step 3 — the mitochondrial filter, done twice.** 8% of 9,312 is 745 cells. Under a global
10% cutoff, Figure 2 says those 745 are drawn almost entirely from the high-mitochondrial
types. If the sample contains 800 cardiomyocytes, essentially all 800 are gone — the recorded
"8% removed" *is* the cardiomyocyte population, and the finished atlas contains none.

**Step 4 — the order that makes this visible.** Run the permissive pipeline first, cluster,
then apply each candidate threshold and tabulate removals by cluster. The cost is one extra
clustering run. The benefit is that step 3 becomes a table instead of an inference.

**What the 81.4% conceals.** Nothing in the headline number distinguishes a pipeline that
removed 1,861 damaged cells from one that removed a cell type and some debris. The retained
fraction is not a quality metric.

</WorkedExample>

## An order of operations

The filters interact, so the sequence matters — not for the final count, which is nearly
order-independent, but for what can be diagnosed.

1. **Call cells against the ambient profile**, not a depth cutoff <Citation id="lun2019emptydrops" />.
2. **Remove obvious debris only** — barcodes with almost no genes detected — using a threshold
   loose enough that no real population is near it.
3. **Cluster.** This is the step usually placed last.
4. **Detect doublets** with a simulation-based method <Citation id="wolock2019scrublet" />, which
   needs the clustering to define what a heterotypic mixture looks like.
5. **Apply mitochondrial and complexity thresholds per cluster**, relative to each cluster's own
   distribution.
6. **Re-cluster** on the filtered matrix.

The standard order — filter everything, then cluster once — is faster and produces a number for
the methods section. It cannot tell you what the filters did <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

## Summary

- QC thresholds are **biological claims**, not hygiene. A filter's headline retention rate says
  nothing about which cells it removed.
- A barcode-rank plot has as many shoulders as the tissue has size classes. A 500-UMI cutoff
  keeps **99.9%** of a 5,000-UMI population and **26.5%** of a 400-UMI one.
- **EmptyDrops** replaces the depth question with a profile question — is this barcode different
  from the soup — which is the only version that separates small cells from empty droplets.
- A global mitochondrial cutoff is a **cell-type filter**: at 10%, cardiomyocytes keep 0.0% and
  lymphocytes keep 88.5%. Threshold per cluster instead.
- Filters compose multiplicatively: 4%, 3%, 8% and 5% leave **81.4%**. That number is
  compatible with both a careful pipeline and one that deleted a population.
- Cluster **before** the aggressive filters, so that every threshold can be priced by cluster.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading a cutoff off the populations" difficulty="warm-up">

A sample contains a population averaging 5,000 UMIs with depth $\Gamma(4, 1250)$ and one
averaging 400 UMIs with depth $\Gamma(4, 100)$.

(a) What fraction of each survives a 200-UMI cutoff?

(b) What fraction survives a 1,000-UMI cutoff?

(c) A collaborator argues that 1,000 is "safely above the noise". Respond.

<div slot="solution">

(a) At 200 UMIs: **100.0%** of the large population and **85.7%** of the small one.

(b) At 1,000 UMIs: **99.1%** of the large population and **1.0%** of the small one.

(c) It is safely above the noise and also above the signal. Moving from 200 to 1,000 costs the
large population 0.9% of its cells and costs the small population 98.8% of its own — from 85.7%
retained to 1.0%. After this filter the small population is present at roughly one cell in a
hundred of its true abundance, which will read as a rare type rather than a common one. "Safely
above the noise" is only meaningful relative to a stated claim about the smallest cell the
experiment intends to see.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="What a retention rate implies about composition" difficulty="core">

A pipeline reports that a 500-UMI cutoff removed 6% of 20,000 called cells. The tissue is known
to contain a small-cell population with depth $\Gamma(4, 100)$, and the rest of the cells are
$\Gamma(4, 1250)$.

(a) What fraction of each population does the cutoff retain?

(b) If the small population is a fraction $f$ of the sample, write the overall loss in terms of
$f$ and solve for it.

(c) The tissue is known from histology to be about 25% small cells. What does that imply?

<div slot="solution">

(a) From the Gamma distributions: **99.92%** of the large population and **26.50%** of the
small one survive, so they lose 0.000776 and 0.734974 of their cells respectively.

(b) The overall loss is

$$
f(0.734974) + (1 - f)(0.000776) = 0.06,
$$

so $0.734198f = 0.059224$ and

$$
f = 0.080665,
$$

about **8%**.

(c) The observed 6% loss is only consistent with the small cells being 8% of the *called*
barcodes. If histology says 25%, then most of the small cells were never called in the first
place — they were classified as empty droplets, before this filter ever ran. The 6% figure is
measuring the tail of a population that was already largely lost at the cell-calling step, and
the fix is upstream: call cells against the ambient profile rather than by depth.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A per-cluster mitochondrial threshold" difficulty="stretch">

Cardiomyocytes have mitochondrial share roughly normal with mean 30% and standard deviation 5
percentage points; lymphocytes mean 4% and standard deviation 5 points.

(a) What fraction of each survives a global 10% cutoff?

(b) Replace it with a per-cluster rule removing cells above the cluster mean plus two standard
deviations. What fraction of each survives, and what are the two thresholds?

(c) Give one situation in which the per-cluster rule fails, and say what it would take to
detect it.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Cardiomyocytes: $\Phi((0.10 - 0.30)/0.05) = \Phi(-4) = 0.0032\%$, so **0.0%** to one
decimal. Lymphocytes: $\Phi((0.10 - 0.04)/0.05) = \Phi(1.2) = 0.8849$, so **88.5%**.

(b) Two standard deviations above each cluster's own mean is 40% for cardiomyocytes and 14% for
lymphocytes. Both retain $\Phi(2) = 0.9772$, so **97.7%** of each — by construction the rule
removes the same fraction everywhere, which is exactly the property a global threshold lacks.

(c) It fails when a whole cluster is damaged. If dissociation destroyed most cardiomyocytes,
their cluster's mean mitochondrial share is elevated *as a cluster*, and a rule defined
relative to that mean removes only the tail of an already-bad population — it normalises away
the very signal that should have condemned the sample. Detecting it needs an external
reference: the healthy mitochondrial share for that cell type in that tissue
<Citation id="osorio2021mito" />, or the same cell type from a sample known to have dissociated
well. A rule that only ever looks within a dataset cannot tell a healthy cardiomyocyte
distribution from a damaged one.

</div>

</Exercise>
