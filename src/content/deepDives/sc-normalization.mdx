## The question this lesson answers

One cell yields 20,000 UMIs and its identical neighbour yields 2,000, for reasons that have
nothing to do with either cell: how much of the droplet's reagent it got, where it sat in the
amplification, how the reads happened to be distributed. Counts are not comparable across cells
until that is divided out.

Almost every pipeline divides it out the same way, and then applies a transform whose usual
justification does not survive being checked. This lesson separates the two steps, because the
first is necessary and the second is a choice — and the choice is usually made by convention.

## Size factors, and the composition problem

The obvious size factor is the library size itself: $s_j = n_j$, giving

$$
\tilde{c}_{gj} = \frac{c_{gj}}{n_j} \times 10^4,
$$

counts per ten thousand. It is right in spirit and wrong in a specific, well-understood way,
which is worth naming because it is the same failure that bulk RNA-seq spent years on.

A count matrix is **compositional**: the counts within a cell sum to $n_j$, so they are shares
of a fixed budget, not independent quantities. If one gene is genuinely up tenfold in a cell,
it consumes more of that budget, and every other gene's share falls even though nothing
happened to them. Dividing by the total propagates that: the library size is inflated by the
very gene whose change caused it, and the normalised values of everything else are pushed down.

In bulk this is severe enough to require median-of-ratios or trimmed-mean estimators. In
single-cell it is milder — no single gene usually dominates — but it is not absent, and it is
worst exactly where it matters, in cells with a strongly expressed lineage marker. The
single-cell answer pools cells into groups large enough for a robust ratio estimate, computes
size factors on the pools, and deconvolves them back to individual cells
<Citation id="lun2016pooling" />. Where capture efficiency varies with expression level in a
gene-dependent way, quantile-based methods condition on that too
<Citation id="bacher2017scnorm" />.

This lesson takes the size factor as solved and looks at what happens next.

## The log transform is biased, and does not do what it is for

The standard recipe finishes by adding one and taking the log. Two claims are usually made for
it: that it makes the data roughly normal, and that it stabilises the variance. The first is
approximate and unimportant. The second is the reason it is chosen, and it is false.

**It is biased low.** $\log(1+x)$ is concave, so by Jensen's inequality
$\mathbb{E}[\log(1+X)] < \log(1+\mathbb{E}[X])$ for any non-degenerate $X$. The gap is not
academic at single-cell means: for a Poisson count of mean 0.1, the expected transformed value
is 0.067904 against $\log(1.1) = 0.095310$, so the transform returns **71.25%** of what the
mean would give. At a mean of 100 it returns 99.89%. The bias is a function of expression
level, which means it is a systematic distortion of exactly the axis every downstream
comparison runs along.

**It does not stabilise variance.** A transform is variance-stabilising when the standard
deviation of the transformed value is flat in the mean — that is the definition, and it is
checkable. It is not flat for log1p.

The consequence is concrete. Under log1p a change of a given biological size produces a
*different* apparent effect depending on how expressed the gene is — large for a mid-expression
gene, compressed for a highly expressed one. Any method that treats the transformed values as
comparable across genes, which includes principal components and every distance-based
clustering, is weighting genes by an artefact of the transform.

## The alternatives, and what they assume

**Pearson residuals** take the count model from lesson 2 seriously. Under a null model where a
cell's expected count is its size factor times the gene's overall rate, the residual

$$
z_{gj} = \frac{c_{gj} - \mu_{gj}}{\sqrt{\mu_{gj} + \mu_{gj}^2/\theta_g}}
$$

is standardised by construction: the denominator is the negative binomial standard deviation,
so $z$ has variance 1 whatever the mean. That is the flat line in Figure 1, and it is flat by
definition rather than by luck <Citation id="hafemeister2019sctransform" />
<Citation id="lause2021pearson" />. The assumption it carries is the model — get $\theta_g$
wrong and the residuals are mis-scaled, which is why the analytic form with a shared $\theta$
is more stable than fitting one per gene.

**Anscombe's transform**, $2\sqrt{x + 3/8}$, is the classical variance-stabiliser for a
Poisson and needs no fitted model at all. It reaches a standard deviation of 1 above a mean of
about five and is within a few percent well below that. It gets much less use than it deserves.

**Modelling counts directly** avoids the question. A GLM-based dimension reduction works on the
counts under a multinomial or negative binomial likelihood and never transforms anything
<Citation id="townes2019glmpca" />.

The honest summary is that the choice matters less than the literature's volume suggests for
clustering, and more than it suggests for anything quantitative — a systematic comparison found
that the simple transforms perform comparably for neighbour-based tasks while differing
substantially in what they do to effect sizes <Citation id="ahlmanneltze2023transformations" />.

<WorkedExample
  title="A difference that is entirely depth"
  given={[
    'Two cells of the same type, with the same true expression',
    'Cell A sequenced to 2,000 UMIs, cell B to 20,000',
    'The gene is 5 in 100,000 of each cell’s transcripts',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — expected counts.** The gene's expected count is depth times true proportion:

$$
\mu_A = 2{,}000 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} = 0.1,
\qquad
\mu_B = 20{,}000 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} = 1.0.
$$

**Step 2 — how often it is seen at all.** By the Poisson model,

$$
1 - e^{-0.1} = 0.095163,
\qquad
1 - e^{-1.0} = 0.632121,
$$

so the gene is detected in **9.5%** of A-like cells and **63.2%** of B-like cells — a ratio of
**6.64**. Nothing biological differs between them.

**Step 3 — what normalisation does about it.** Suppose A returns 0 and B returns 1, both
typical outcomes. Dividing by library size and scaling to 10,000:

$$
\tilde{c}_A = \frac{0}{2{,}000} \times 10^4 = 0,
\qquad
\tilde{c}_B = \frac{1}{20{,}000} \times 10^4 = 0.5.
$$

Normalisation has correctly put them on one scale. It has not made them equal, because zero
divided by anything is zero. After log1p, A is 0.0000 and B is 0.4055.

**Step 4 — where this ends up.** If depth is correlated with cell type — and it always is,
because cell types differ in RNA content — then every gene shows a detection gradient aligned
with cell type, and any test based on the fraction of cells expressing a gene reports it as
differential. This is the mechanism behind marker lists dominated by housekeeping genes.

**What actually helps.** Compare *levels* under a model that knows each cell's depth, not
presence or normalised means: put the size factor in the model as an offset rather than
dividing by it first. Dividing throws away the information that A's zero was a *shallow* zero
and carries much less evidence than B's would have.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Divide for display, model for inference">

The two uses of normalisation pull in opposite directions and are routinely conflated.

For **looking** — heatmaps, embeddings, a marker's expression across clusters — you need one
scale, and dividing by a size factor is the right move. Precision does not matter much because
nothing is being tested.

For **inference**, dividing is a loss. A normalised value of 0.5 derived from one count in
20,000 UMIs and one derived from ten counts in 400,000 UMIs are not equally certain, and the
ratio has thrown that away. Keep the counts and put $\log s_j$ in the model as an offset; the
likelihood then knows how much each observation is worth.

The rule of thumb: if a number is going into a figure, normalise it. If it is going into a
test, do not <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

It is worth being clear about what those zeros are, because the temptation at this point is to
impute them. They are not failures. Lesson 2 established that droplet UMI counts need no
zero-inflation term — the zeros are what Poisson sampling of a small mean produces
<Citation id="svensson2020zeroinflation" /> — so the shallow cell's zero is a correct
observation carrying weak evidence, not a missing value awaiting repair. Normalisation cannot
strengthen that evidence and imputation only disguises how weak it was.

## Summary

- Counts must be put on a common scale, but a count matrix is **compositional**, so dividing by
  the raw library size is biased by whichever genes are strongly expressed. Pooled size-factor
  estimation fixes that <Citation id="lun2016pooling" />.
- **log1p is biased low** by Jensen's inequality, returning **71.25%** of $\log(1+\mu)$ at a
  mean of 0.1 and 99.89% at a mean of 100 — a distortion that varies along the expression axis.
- **log1p does not stabilise variance**, which is the reason it is chosen. Its standard
  deviation runs 0.5123 to 0.0997 across the range, a factor of **5.14**. Pearson residuals are
  flat at 1 by construction; Anscombe reaches 1 above a mean of about five.
- **No normalisation repairs a zero.** The same gene at the same true level is detected in
  **9.5%** of cells at 2,000 UMIs and **63.2%** at 20,000 — a 6.64-fold gap from depth alone,
  which survives any rescaling.
- **Divide for display, model for inference.** Put $\log s_j$ in the model as an offset instead
  of dividing, so the likelihood knows what each observation is worth.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading the bias off the transform" difficulty="warm-up">

(a) For a Poisson count of mean 1, compare $\mathbb{E}[\log(1+X)]$ with $\log(1+1)$.

(b) State the direction of the bias and the reason for it, in one sentence.

(c) A colleague proposes fixing it by adding a smaller pseudocount than 1. Does that work?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\mathbb{E}[\log(1+X)] = 0.573403$ against $\log 2 = 0.693147$, a ratio of **0.8272**.

(b) The transformed value is biased **low**, because $\log(1+x)$ is concave and Jensen's
inequality sends the expectation of a concave function below the function of the expectation.

(c) No, and it makes things worse. Shrinking the pseudocount increases the curvature of the
transform near zero, which increases the Jensen gap rather than reducing it — and it sends
$\log(\epsilon)$ towards $-\infty$ for the zeros, which are the majority of the matrix. The
pseudocount is not a bias-correction parameter; it is there to keep zeros finite. Correcting
the bias requires a different transform, not a different constant.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Testing a stabilisation claim" difficulty="core">

A method's documentation states that its transform "stabilises the variance so that genes are
comparable".

(a) Write down the test that claim implies.

(b) Apply it to log1p across means of 2 and 100.

(c) Apply it to Anscombe's transform across the same two means, and state which claim survives.

<div slot="solution">

(a) A transform stabilises variance when the standard deviation of the transformed count does
not depend on the mean. So: compute $\mathrm{SD}[f(X)]$ for $X$ Poisson across a range of
means, and check whether it is flat.

(b) log1p gives 0.5123 at a mean of 2 and 0.0997 at 100 — a factor of **5.14**. Not flat, and
the direction matters: highly expressed genes are *compressed*, so they contribute less
variance and are systematically underweighted by PCA.

(c) Anscombe gives 0.9614 at a mean of 2 and 1.0000 at 100, a ratio of **1.04**. That claim
survives; the log1p one does not. Note this is a property of the transform alone and needs no
data to check — the documentation's claim was falsifiable on paper.

</div>

</Exercise>

(b) A test compares the fraction of cells expressing the gene and reports it as strongly
enriched in Q. Is the test wrong?

(c) What analysis would not have made this error, and what does it cost?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\mu_P = 1{,}000 \times 2 \times 10^{-4} = 0.2$ and $\mu_Q = 10{,}000 \times 2 \times
10^{-4} = 2.0$, so detection is $1 - e^{-0.2} = 0.181269$ and $1 - e^{-2.0} = 0.864665$:
**18.1%** against **86.5%**.

(b) The test is not wrong about what it measured — the gene really is detected in more Q cells
— but the conclusion is wrong. Detection is a function of expression *and* depth, and only one
of those differs. The test answered a question nobody wanted the answer to.

(c) A model of the counts with $\log s_j$ as an offset, so that observing zero in a
1,000-UMI cell and zero in a 10,000-UMI cell carry their correct, different weights. Under such
a model the two clusters' fitted rates are equal and no difference is reported. The cost is
that it requires the count model of lesson 2 to be roughly right, and it is slower than
comparing two proportions — which is the entire reason the proportion test remains popular.
