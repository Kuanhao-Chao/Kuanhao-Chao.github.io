## The question this lesson answers

A polygenic score is arithmetic a spreadsheet could do:

$$
\mathrm{PRS}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j\,G_{ij}
$$

Count each person's risk alleles, weight them, add up. Every genuinely hard question about
polygenic scores is a question about $w_j$ — and the obvious answer, "use the effect sizes
GWAS already reported", is wrong in a way that matters enormously.

<Callout variant="intuition">
GWAS tests one variant at a time. A variant that is merely *correlated* with a causal one
still shows an association, so the reported effect is not that variant's own contribution
— it is the causal signal, seen through whatever window of linkage disequilibrium that
variant provides.

Add up a hundred correlated markers around one causal site and you have not added a
hundred pieces of evidence. You have added the same piece of evidence a hundred times.

Everything below is a way of undoing that: either by deleting the redundant markers
(clumping) or by shrinking every weight toward zero in proportion to how much of it is
likely to be noise and duplication (Bayesian shrinkage).
</Callout>

## Why the marginal estimate is the wrong weight

Write the true model with causal effects $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and standardised genotypes.
The quantity GWAS reports for variant $j$ is not $\beta_j$ but its LD-weighted sum with
every other variant's effect:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \;\approx\; \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\beta} \;+\; \text{noise},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\hat{\beta}_j \approx \sum_k r_{jk}\,\beta_k
$$

Reading this equation is the whole lesson. If variant $j$ is the only causal one in its
region, $\hat{\beta}_j = \beta_j$ and all is well. If four neighbours each have
$r_{jk} \approx 1$ with it, all five report $\hat\beta \approx \beta_j$ — and summing all
five into a score contributes $5\beta_j$ where the truth is $\beta_j$.

The correct weights solve $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ — a joint
rather than marginal fit. That system is $M \times M$ with $M$ in the millions, is
ill-conditioned, and is estimated from a reference panel rather than known. The methods
below are all ways of approximating its solution.

## Clumping and thresholding

The oldest approach, and still a reasonable baseline <Citation id="isc2009" />, refuses to
model LD and deletes it instead. Two steps:

1. **Clump.** Walk the variants in order of significance. Keep the most significant one,
   discard everything within 250 kb that exceeds an $r^2$ threshold (commonly $0.1$), and
   repeat. What survives is a set of approximately independent index variants.
2. **Threshold.** Keep those with $P_j < P_T$ and use the marginal estimate as the weight:

$$
w_j = \hat{\beta}_j \cdot \mathbb{I}(P_j < P_T) \cdot \mathbb{I}(j \text{ survives clumping})
$$

$P_T$ is chosen by cross-validation in an independent tuning cohort — typically sweeping
from $5\times10^{-8}$ up to $1.0$, and the best value is usually far looser than
genome-wide significance, which is itself informative: most of the predictive signal sits
below the detection threshold <Citation id="choi2020" />.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Clumping solves the right problem the wrong way">
Deleting correlated markers does remove the double-counting. But an LD block usually
contains real signal at more than one site, and clumping keeps exactly one variant per
block regardless. It also makes a hard $0/1$ decision about every variant, when the
evidence is continuous — a variant at $P = 0.051$ contributes nothing while its neighbour
at $P = 0.049$ contributes its full unshrunk estimate.

That discontinuity is what the Bayesian methods remove.
</Callout>

## Shrinkage, and where its factor comes from

Instead of deciding which variants are real, put a prior on all of them and let the
posterior mean be the weight. Under the infinitesimal model — every variant causal, effects
normal — the derivation is the standard normal–normal conjugate update.

**Step 1 — the prior.** If $M$ variants share heritability $h^2$ equally, each carries

$$
\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \frac{h^2}{M}\right)
$$

**Step 2 — the likelihood.** With standardised genotypes and phenotype, the sampling
variance of a marginal estimate from $N$ individuals is approximately $1/N$:

$$
\hat\beta_j \mid \beta_j \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\beta_j,\; \tfrac{1}{N}\right)
$$

**Step 3 — combine.** For a normal prior and normal likelihood the posterior mean is the
observation scaled by the ratio of prior variance to total variance:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\beta_j \mid \hat\beta_j]
= \hat\beta_j \cdot \frac{h^2/M}{h^2/M + 1/N}
= \hat\beta_j \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{M}{N h^2}}
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="One ratio governs everything">
The shrinkage factor depends on the data only through $M/(Nh^2)$ — variants per unit of
heritable information.

- **$M/(Nh^2) \gg 1$** (small study, many variants, low heritability): the factor goes to
  zero. Nearly all of every estimate is noise, and the correct weight is nearly nothing.
- **$M/(Nh^2) \ll 1$** (large study): the factor goes to one, and the marginal estimate is
  used almost unshrunk.

This is why polygenic scores were nearly useless in 2009 and are clinically discussed
today without any change in method: $N$ grew by three orders of magnitude.
</Callout>

Real methods generalise this in two directions. **LDpred** <Citation id="vilhjalmsson2015" />
and **LDpred2** <Citation id="prive2020" /> replace the infinitesimal prior with a
spike-and-slab, $\beta_j \sim \pi\,\mathcal{N}(0, h^2/(M\pi)) + (1-\pi)\delta_0$, letting a
fraction $1-\pi$ of variants be exactly zero, and they condition on $\mathbf{R}$ by Gibbs
sampling rather than treating variants as independent. **PRS-CS**
<Citation id="ge2019" /> instead keeps every variant non-zero but gives each its own
variance:

$$
\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \tfrac{\sigma^2}{N}\phi\psi_j\right),
\qquad \psi_j \sim \mathcal{G}(a, \delta_j), \qquad \delta_j \sim \mathcal{G}(b, 1)
$$

a continuous-shrinkage hierarchy that shrinks small effects hard while letting genuinely
large ones escape — and, unlike the spike-and-slab, has a smooth unimodal posterior that
Gibbs sampling explores reliably. **PRS-CS-auto** puts a half-Cauchy prior on $\sqrt{\phi}$
and learns the global shrinkage from the summary statistics themselves, removing the need
for a tuning cohort; **PRS-CSx** <Citation id="ruan2022" /> extends the hierarchy across
several ancestries at once, coupling the effect sizes while keeping each population's own
LD structure.

**Step 1 — form the governing ratio.**

$$
\frac{M}{N h^2} = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{100{,}000 \times 0.50} = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{50{,}000} = 20
$$

**Step 2 — the shrinkage factor.**

$$
\frac{1}{1 + M/(Nh^2)} = \frac{1}{1 + 20} = \frac{1}{21} = 0.047619
$$

A marginal estimate of $\hat\beta = 0.10$ enters the score with weight $0.0048$. The
posterior keeps **4.8%** of what GWAS reported and discards the rest as noise — and it is
right to, because with 20 variants per unit of heritable information, that is what the
evidence supports.

**Step 3 — see the dependence on N.** Nothing about the trait changed; only the study did:

| $N$ | $M/(Nh^2)$ | shrinkage factor |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 200 | 0.004975 |
| 100,000 | 20 | 0.047619 |
| 1,000,000 | 2 | 0.333333 |

Two orders of magnitude in sample size buy nearly two orders of magnitude in how much of
each estimate survives.

</WorkedExample>

## The accuracy ceiling, and what it costs to approach it

The same ratio governs how well the finished score can possibly perform. Under the
infinitesimal model the expected squared correlation between score and phenotype is
<Citation id="daetwyler2008" />

$$
R^2_{\mathrm{PRS}} \;=\; \frac{h^2}{1 + \dfrac{M}{N h^2}}
$$

Two features of this are worth stating plainly. As $N \to \infty$ the accuracy approaches
$h^2$ and stops — **an additive score cannot explain more variance than the additive
variance exists to explain**. And the approach is hyperbolic, so each additional increment
of accuracy costs disproportionately more data.

  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — invert the relation.** Starting from $R^2 = h^2/(1 + M/(Nh^2))$,

$$
1 + \frac{M}{Nh^2} = \frac{h^2}{R^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N = \frac{M}{h^2\left(\dfrac{h^2}{R^2} - 1\right)}
$$

**Step 2 — half the ceiling, $R^2 = 0.25$.** Here $h^2/R^2 = 2$, so

$$
N = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{0.50 \times (2 - 1)} = 2{,}000{,}000
$$

**Step 3 — eighty percent of it, $R^2 = 0.40$.** Now $h^2/R^2 = 1.25$, so

$$
N = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{0.50 \times (1.25 - 1)} = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{0.125} = 8{,}000{,}000
$$

**Step 4 — read the ratio.** Going from half the ceiling to four-fifths of it costs

$$
\frac{8{,}000{,}000}{2{,}000{,}000} = 4\times
$$

the sample size. This is the structural reason GWAS consortia keep growing, and the reason
the last increments of polygenic prediction are so expensive. It is also why reporting a
score's $R^2$ without its $h^2$ is uninformative: a score at $R^2 = 0.05$ is poor for a
trait with $h^2 = 0.5$ and close to optimal for one with $h^2 = 0.06$.

</WorkedExample>

## Evaluating a score honestly

- **Incremental $R^2$**, $\Delta R^2 = R^2_{\text{covariates}+\text{PRS}} - R^2_{\text{covariates}}$.
  Reporting the full-model $R^2$ credits the score with age and sex, which is not a genetic
  finding.
- **Odds ratio per standard deviation**, $\exp(\beta_{\mathrm{PRS}})$ from a logistic fit on
  the standardised score. Comparable across studies in a way that a top-decile odds ratio
  is not, because a decile contrast depends on the shape of the tail as well as the
  score's quality <Citation id="khera2018" />.
- **Calibration**, not just discrimination. The Brier score
  $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i(y_i - \hat p_i)^2$ penalises predicted probabilities that
  are systematically wrong even when the ranking is right — and a score whose ranking is
  fine but whose absolute risks are inflated is dangerous in exactly the clinical setting
  where it would be used <Citation id="wray2013" />.

## The portability problem

A score trained in one ancestry loses most of its accuracy in another. In European-trained
scores the relative accuracy falls to roughly one half in East and South Asian samples and
to roughly a quarter in African-ancestry samples <Citation id="martin2019" />
<Citation id="duncan2019" />. Since something like three quarters of GWAS participants are
of European ancestry, this is not a corner case; it is the default outcome.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Four mechanisms, only one of which more data can fix">
1. **LD differs.** A weight assigned to a tag SNP is only valid where that tag has the same
   $r^2$ with the causal variant. African populations have shorter LD blocks, so European
   tags carry less of the causal signal there.
2. **Allele frequencies differ.** A variant common in one population and rare in another
   contributes almost nothing to variance in the second, whatever its weight.
3. **Effect sizes can genuinely differ**, through gene–environment interaction or epistasis
   with a different genetic background.
4. **Confounding differs.** Residual population stratification in the discovery cohort
   produces weights that partly encode ancestry, and those transfer worst of all.

Only the first is fully addressable by better methods — PRS-CSx and its relatives exist to
borrow strength across ancestries while respecting each one's LD. The rest requires
recruiting the populations themselves. Deploying a European-trained score clinically
without this caveat would widen health disparities rather than narrow them, which is the
argument Martin and colleagues make directly.
</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Compute a score by hand" difficulty="warm-up">

An individual carries dosages $G = (2, 1, 0, 2)$ at four variants with weights
$w = (0.05,\, -0.02,\, 0.10,\, 0.03)$. What is their polygenic score, and what does the
number mean on its own?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\mathrm{PRS} = (0.05)(2) + (-0.02)(1) + (0.10)(0) + (0.03)(2)
= 0.10 - 0.02 + 0 + 0.06 = 0.14
$$

On its own it means nothing. A raw polygenic score has no natural scale: it depends on how
many variants were included, how they were weighted, and which allele was designated the
effect allele. Scores are interpreted only after standardising against a reference
distribution — as a percentile or a $z$-score — and that reference must come from the same
ancestry, or the percentile is meaningless.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Shrinkage across three studies" difficulty="core">

For a trait with $h^2 = 0.50$ and $M = 1{,}000{,}000$, compute the infinitesimal shrinkage
factor at $N = 10{,}000$, $100{,}000$ and $1{,}000{,}000$. By what factor does a weight
grow between the smallest and largest study, and why is that not a hundredfold?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\frac{M}{Nh^2} = 200,\; 20,\; 2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{1}{1+M/(Nh^2)} = 0.004975,\; 0.047619,\; 0.333333
$$

The weight grows by $0.333333 / 0.004975 \approx 67$ times, not $100$.

The reason is that the factor is $1/(1 + M/(Nh^2))$, not $N/M \cdot h^2$. At small $N$ the
$1$ in the denominator is negligible and the factor is very nearly proportional to $N$; as
$N$ grows the $1$ starts to dominate and the factor saturates at one. The hundredfold
increase in data is spent partly on escaping proportionality — which is the same
diminishing return the accuracy curve shows.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The cost of the last increment" difficulty="stretch">

For $h^2 = 0.50$ and $M = 1{,}000{,}000$, how many samples are needed for
$R^2 = 0.45$ — ninety percent of the ceiling? Compare with the eight million needed for
$R^2 = 0.40$, and state the general behaviour as $R^2 \to h^2$.

<div slot="solution">

Using $N = M / \left(h^2(h^2/R^2 - 1)\right)$ with $h^2/R^2 = 0.50/0.45 = 1.1\overline{1}$:

$$
N = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{0.50 \times 0.1\overline{1}} = 18{,}000{,}000
$$

So $0.40 \to 0.45$ costs another ten million samples, having already spent eight to reach
$0.40$.

In general, write $R^2 = (1-\varepsilon)h^2$ for a small shortfall $\varepsilon$. Then
$h^2/R^2 - 1 = \varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)$ and

$$
N = \frac{M}{h^2}\cdot\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} \;\sim\; \frac{M}{h^2\,\varepsilon}
$$

The required sample size diverges as $1/\varepsilon$. Closing the last tenth of the gap to
the ceiling costs as much as everything before it, and closing the last hundredth costs ten
times that again. The ceiling is approached but never reached, and the practical limit is
set by budget rather than by biology.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A polygenic score is $\sum_j w_j G_{ij}$**, and every difficulty is in $w_j$. Marginal
  GWAS estimates are not causal effects but LD-weighted sums, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}
  \approx \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol\beta$, so adding them up counts one signal once per marker
  that tags it.
- **Clumping and thresholding** removes the redundancy by deletion — effective, but it
  keeps one variant per block and makes a hard call at an arbitrary $P$ threshold.
- **Bayesian shrinkage** keeps every variant and scales it by $1/(1 + M/(Nh^2))$ under the
  infinitesimal model. LDpred2 generalises the prior to spike-and-slab; PRS-CS uses a
  continuous-shrinkage hierarchy with a smoother posterior; PRS-CSx couples several
  ancestries.
- **The ceiling is $h^2$**, approached as $R^2 = h^2/(1 + M/(Nh^2))$. Half of it costs two
  million samples for a trait with $h^2 = 0.5$; four-fifths costs eight million; the
  requirement diverges as $1/\varepsilon$ in the remaining shortfall.
- **Report $\Delta R^2$, an odds ratio per SD, and calibration** — and always alongside
  $h^2$, since accuracy is only interpretable against its own ceiling.
- **Portability is a data problem, not only a method problem.** LD, allele frequency,
  effect heterogeneity and confounding all differ across ancestries, and only the first is
  substantially fixable without recruiting the populations concerned.
