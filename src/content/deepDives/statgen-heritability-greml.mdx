## The question this lesson answers

Twin studies put the heritability of human height at about 80%. By 2010, every variant
that had cleared genome-wide significance together accounted for roughly 10% of the
variance <Citation id="langoallen2010" />. That gap — christened the **missing
heritability** <Citation id="manolio2009" /> — was large enough that people seriously
proposed the twin estimates were simply wrong.

They were not. The variance was there; the method of looking for it was the problem.
This lesson builds the machinery that found it.

<Callout variant="intuition">
Heritability is a ratio, and almost every misuse of the word comes from forgetting what
is in the denominator.

It answers: *of all the variation in this trait, in this population, right now, what
fraction tracks genetic differences between people?* It does not say what fraction of
**your** height is genetic — that question has no answer, in the way that asking which
half of a rectangle's area comes from its length has no answer. And because the
denominator is the variation actually present, heritability rises when you standardise
the environment and falls when you diversify it, without a single allele changing.

The reason it is still worth measuring: a high $h^2$ means relatives resemble each other
for genetic reasons, so genotypes carry predictive information — which is the entire
premise of polygenic scores.
</Callout>

## What heritability is a ratio of

Write each individual's phenotype as a genetic part plus an environmental part, and take
variances. If the two are uncorrelated, the variance decomposes:

$$
V_P \;=\; V_G + V_E \;=\; \underbrace{V_A + V_D + V_I}_{V_G} \;+\; V_E
$$

The two heritabilities take different numerators:

$$
H^2 = \frac{V_G}{V_P} = \frac{V_A + V_D + V_I}{V_P},
\qquad
h^2 = \frac{V_A}{V_P}
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="Why h², not H², is the one that matters">
Parents pass on **alleles**, not genotypes. A parent heterozygous at a locus transmits one
allele; whichever dominance interaction that allele had with its former partner does not
travel with it, because it will meet a new partner in the offspring. The same argument
applies to epistasis across loci, which meiosis breaks up.

So $V_D$ and $V_I$ contribute to how much genetic variation exists, but not to how much
of it is *transmitted linearly*. $h^2$ is the slope of the offspring-on-midparent
regression; $H^2$ is not the slope of anything useful.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="assumption" title="What is assumed">
The decomposition above needs $\operatorname{Cov}(G, E) = 0$ and no gene–environment
interaction folded into $V_G$. Both fail in realistic settings — parents supply both
genes and environment, which correlates them — and when they fail the surplus is absorbed
into whichever component the model does allow. Every estimate below is therefore
population- and time-specific, and comparing an $h^2$ across populations compares two
different denominators as much as two different biologies <Citation id="visscher2008" />.
</Callout>

## The twin estimator, and the assumption it rests on

Monozygotic twins are genetically identical; dizygotic twins share half their segregating
alleles on average. If both kinds of twin pair share their rearing environment to the same
degree, the difference between their phenotypic correlations isolates the genetic part.

**Step 1 — model each correlation.** Under an ACE model, phenotype is additive genetic
($a^2$), common environment ($c^2$) and unique environment ($e^2$) as fractions of $V_P$,
with $a^2 + c^2 + e^2 = 1$. Two individuals correlate only through the components they
share. MZ twins share all additive variance and all common environment; DZ twins share
half the additive variance and all common environment:

$$
r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = a^2 + c^2, \qquad r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = \tfrac{1}{2}a^2 + c^2
$$

**Step 2 — subtract.** The $c^2$ terms cancel, which is the whole trick:

$$
r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = \tfrac{1}{2}a^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
h^2 = a^2 = 2\left(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}}\right)
$$

**Step 3 — recover the rest.** Substituting back gives the other two components:

$$
c^2 = 2r_{\mathrm{DZ}} - r_{\mathrm{MZ}}, \qquad e^2 = 1 - r_{\mathrm{MZ}}
$$

<Callout variant="warning" title="The equal-environments assumption">
Step 2 cancels $c^2$ only if it is *the same number* for MZ and DZ pairs. If identical
twins are treated more alike than fraternal twins — dressed alike, kept in the same class,
sharing more friends — then $c^2_{\mathrm{MZ}} > c^2_{\mathrm{DZ}}$, the cancellation
leaves a positive residue, and it is counted as genetic.

This is not a hypothetical: it is the standard criticism of twin heritability, and it
biases $h^2$ **upward**. A meta-analysis of essentially every twin study ever published
puts the average across 17,800 traits at $h^2 \approx 0.49$ <Citation id="polderman2015" />,
and that average inherits the assumption.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Falconer's estimator on a twin cohort"
  given={[
    { label: 'MZ correlation r_MZ', value: '0.85' },
    { label: 'DZ correlation r_DZ', value: '0.50' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — heritability.**

$$
h^2 = 2(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}}) = 2(0.85 - 0.50) = 0.70
$$

**Step 2 — shared environment.**

$$
c^2 = 2r_{\mathrm{DZ}} - r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 2(0.50) - 0.85 = 0.15
$$

**Step 3 — unique environment.**

$$
e^2 = 1 - r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 1 - 0.85 = 0.15
$$

**Step 4 — check the constraint.** The three fractions must exhaust the variance:

$$
0.70 + 0.15 + 0.15 = 1.00 \;\checkmark
$$

That check is worth doing every time, because it is not automatic — it is guaranteed by
construction only when the ACE model actually fits. When it does not, the arithmetic
returns components outside $[0,1]$, and that is diagnostic rather than a rounding problem.
Exercise 2 works through such a case.

</WorkedExample>

## Missing heritability

By 2010 the arithmetic was stark. Twin studies said 80% for height; the 180 loci that had
reached $P < 5\times10^{-8}$ explained about 10% <Citation id="langoallen2010" />. The
candidate explanations were all substantive — rare variants of large effect, structural
variation, epistasis, or simply inflated twin estimates — and all of them assumed the
missing variance was somewhere the studies had not looked.

Yang and colleagues <Citation id="yang2010common" /> proposed something duller and
correct: the variance was in the *same common SNPs already on the array*, distributed
across thousands of effects each far too small to clear a threshold designed to control
one million simultaneous tests. Testing variants one at a time cannot see it. Estimating
the variance they collectively tag can.

Whole-genome sequencing later closed most of what remained: including rare variants that
common SNPs cannot tag brings height to about 68% <Citation id="wainschtein2022" />. The
missing heritability was never missing. It was unresolvable by single-marker testing, and
partly untagged by arrays.

## The genomic relatedness matrix

The idea that makes GREML work is to stop treating "related" as a pedigree category and
start treating it as a continuous measurement. Two nominally unrelated people still share
a little more or a little less of the genome than average, and if the trait is heritable,
the pairs who share more should resemble each other more.

**Step 1 — standardise each genotype.** Let $X_{ij} \in \{0,1,2\}$ count reference alleles
for individual $i$ at SNP $j$, with allele frequency $p_j$. Under Hardy–Weinberg the
count has mean $2p_j$ and variance $2p_j(1-p_j)$, so

$$
W_{ij} = \frac{X_{ij} - 2p_j}{\sqrt{2p_j(1-p_j)}}
$$

has mean zero and unit variance. The standardisation is not cosmetic: it is what makes
every SNP contribute equally to the sum below, rather than letting common SNPs dominate
by virtue of having more variance to contribute.

**Step 2 — average the products.** The relatedness of individuals $i$ and $k$ is the
average standardised product across all $M$ SNPs <Citation id="vanraden2008" />:

$$
A_{ik} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{(X_{ij}-2p_j)(X_{kj}-2p_j)}{2p_j(1-p_j)}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}
$$

The matrix form makes two things obvious that the sum does not: $\mathbf{A}$ is $N\times N$
regardless of how many SNPs went into it, and it is positive semi-definite, so it is a
legitimate covariance structure.

Off-diagonal entries estimate twice the kinship coefficient — about $0.5$ for full
siblings, and scattered around zero with a small variance for unrelated people. Diagonal
entries are $A_{ii} \approx 1 + F_i$, where $F_i$ is the inbreeding coefficient.

<Callout variant="warning" title="GREML estimates h²_SNP, not h²">
$\mathbf{A}$ is built from the SNPs you genotyped. It therefore captures causal variance
only to the extent that causal variants are in LD with those SNPs — which is exactly the
$r^2$ from the previous lesson, and exactly why a rare causal variant contributes almost
nothing.

The quantity estimated is $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$, a *lower bound* on $h^2$ whose tightness
depends on array density and on the frequency spectrum of the causal variants. Reporting
it as "the heritability" is the single most common misreading of a GCTA result. GREML
studies also routinely drop one of each pair with $A_{ik} > 0.025$, because close
relatives share environment as well as genome and would reintroduce exactly the
confounding the method is designed to avoid.
</Callout>

## Heritability as a variance component

**Step 1 — the model.** Treat the aggregate genetic value as a random effect whose
covariance is the relatedness matrix:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{g} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_g^2\mathbf{A}),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_e^2\mathbf{I})
$$

$\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ holds fixed effects — age, sex, ancestry principal
components. The individual SNP effects are never estimated; only the variance they
generate is.

**Step 2 — the implied phenotypic covariance.** Since $\mathbf{g}$ and
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ are independent,

$$
\mathbf{V} = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{y}) = \sigma_g^2\mathbf{A} + \sigma_e^2\mathbf{I},
\qquad
h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}} = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_e^2}
$$

Read as an estimator, this says: regress the phenotypic similarity of every pair on their
genomic similarity, and the slope is $\sigma_g^2$. The signal comes entirely from the
*variance* of the off-diagonal entries — which is why GREML needs tens of thousands of
individuals, and why its precision depends on sample size so brutally (Exercise 3).

**Step 3 — fit by REML, not ML.** Maximum likelihood estimates of variance components are
biased downward, because they do not pay for the degrees of freedom spent estimating
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ — the same reason the sample variance divides by $n-1$. Restricted
maximum likelihood <Citation id="patterson1971" /> maximises the likelihood of contrasts
orthogonal to the fixed effects. The projection is

$$
\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{V}^{-1} - \mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
$$

$$
\ln L_R = -\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\ln|\mathbf{V}| + \ln\left|\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\right| + \mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}\right]
$$

**Step 4 — climb it with AI-REML.** Newton–Raphson needs the Hessian, whose entries
involve traces like $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{PAPA})$ — $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$, and
prohibitive at biobank scale. Averaging the observed and expected information matrices
makes those traces cancel <Citation id="gilmour1995" />, leaving quadratic forms:

$$
\mathrm{AI}_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\sigma_i^2}\mathbf{P}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\sigma_j^2}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}
$$

Each is a handful of matrix–vector products. This is the step that made GREML practical,
and it is what GCTA implements <Citation id="yang2011gcta" />.

## Case-control studies need a scale change

For a binary disease the model above is fitted to a 0/1 phenotype, which gives an
**observed-scale** heritability. That number is not comparable across studies, because it
depends on how many cases were recruited — and case-control studies deliberately
oversample cases, often to 50% against a population prevalence of 1%.

The fix is to assume the familiar liability-threshold model <Citation id="falconer1965" />:
an unobserved normally distributed liability, with disease above a threshold $T$ set by
prevalence $K$. Lee and colleagues <Citation id="lee2011estimating" /> derived the exact
conversion:

$$
h^2_l = h^2_o \,\frac{K^2(1-K)^2}{z_K^2\,P(1-P)},
\qquad
z_K = \varphi\!\left(\Phi^{-1}(1-K)\right)
$$

where $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and CDF, so $z_K$ is the height
of the density at the disease threshold.

<WorkedExample
  title="Observed scale to liability scale"
  given={[
    { label: 'Observed-scale h²_o', value: '0.20' },
    { label: 'Population prevalence K', value: '0.01' },
    { label: 'Case fraction P', value: '0.50' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — locate the threshold.** Disease is the top 1% of liability, so

$$
T = \Phi^{-1}(1 - K) = \Phi^{-1}(0.99) = 2.326348
$$

**Step 2 — density height there.**

$$
z_K = \varphi(2.326348) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-2.326348^2/2} = 0.026652
$$

**Step 3 — the conversion factor.** Numerator and denominator separately:

$$
K^2(1-K)^2 = (0.01 \times 0.99)^2 = 0.00009801
$$

$$
z_K^2\,P(1-P) = 0.026652^2 \times 0.25 = 0.000177584
$$

$$
\frac{K^2(1-K)^2}{z_K^2 P(1-P)} = \frac{0.00009801}{0.000177584} = 0.5519
$$

**Step 4 — apply it.**

$$
h^2_l = 0.20 \times 0.5519 = 0.1104
$$

**Step 5 — read the direction.** The correction almost halves the estimate. Recruiting
50% cases for a disease that afflicts 1% makes the sample far more informative per person
than the population is, and the observed-scale figure inherits that. Reporting $0.20$
without the conversion would overstate the population heritability by more than 80%.

</WorkedExample>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="When the model breaks" difficulty="core">

A study reports $r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 0.90$ and $r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = 0.30$. Apply Falconer's
formulas. What goes wrong, and what does it tell you?

<div slot="solution">

$$
h^2 = 2(0.90 - 0.30) = 1.20, \qquad c^2 = 2(0.30) - 0.90 = -0.30
$$

Both are impossible: a variance fraction cannot exceed one, and cannot be negative.

The failure is diagnostic. The ACE model assumes DZ twins share exactly half the
trait-relevant genetic variance, which holds only if that variance is purely additive.
When **dominance** contributes, DZ twins share only a quarter of $V_D$ against the MZ
pairs' whole of it, so $r_{\mathrm{DZ}}$ falls further below $r_{\mathrm{MZ}}$ than the
additive model can accommodate — and the surplus is pushed into a negative $c^2$.

The standard response is to fit an ADE model instead, with a dominance component in place
of shared environment. Note that ACE and ADE cannot both be identified from MZ and DZ
correlations alone: two equations cannot determine three unknowns, so which model is
fitted is a choice, not a finding.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="How large a sample does GREML need?" difficulty="stretch">

For unrelated individuals the standard error of a GREML estimate is approximately
$\mathrm{SE}(\hat{h}^2) \approx 316/N$. How many individuals are needed for a 95%
confidence interval of half-width $0.05$? Why is the requirement so severe?

<div slot="solution">

A 95% interval has half-width $1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$, so the target is

$$
\mathrm{SE} = \frac{0.05}{1.96} = 0.02551
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
N = \frac{316}{0.02551} \approx 12{,}400
$$

For the more demanding half-width of $0.02$ the same formula gives $N \approx 31{,}000$.

The severity has a clear source. All the information about $\sigma_g^2$ lives in the
*variance of the off-diagonal entries* of $\mathbf{A}$, and among unrelated people that
variance is tiny — relatedness is scattered narrowly around zero. The estimator is
regressing phenotypic similarity on a predictor with almost no spread, which is
statistically expensive. Pedigree studies get away with far smaller samples precisely
because a sibling pair at $A_{ik} \approx 0.5$ supplies enormously more leverage than a
thousand pairs near zero — at the cost of the shared environment that GREML was built to
escape.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Heritability is a ratio**, and its denominator is the variance actually present in a
  particular population at a particular time. It says nothing about any individual, and
  nothing about whether an environmental intervention would work.
- **$H^2$ counts all genetic variance; $h^2$ counts only the additive part.** Only the
  additive part is transmitted linearly, so $h^2$ is what predicts resemblance between
  relatives and what polygenic scores can hope to capture.
- **Falconer's estimator**, $h^2 = 2(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}})$, cancels shared
  environment only under the equal-environments assumption, which biases it upward when it
  fails, and returns out-of-range components when non-additive variance is present.
- **GREML replaces pedigree categories with a measured relatedness matrix**,
  $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}/M$, and estimates the variance component
  $\sigma_g^2$ by REML — recovering height's heritability from 10% to 45% without
  identifying a single new locus.
- **What GREML estimates is $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$**, a lower bound set by what the
  genotyped SNPs tag. Sequencing rare variation closes most of the remaining gap.
- **Binary traits need the liability-scale conversion**, or an ascertained case fraction
  silently inflates the estimate — by more than 80% in the worked example above.
