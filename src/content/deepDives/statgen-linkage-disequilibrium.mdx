## The question this lesson answers

A genotyping array measures roughly a million positions. The genome has about three
billion. GWAS nonetheless finds causal signals it never directly measured — routinely,
and reliably. That only works because the unmeasured positions are not independent of
the measured ones.

**Linkage disequilibrium** is the name for that dependence, and the whole of this lesson
is an attempt to make one quantity precise: *how much does knowing one variant tell you
about its neighbour?*

<Callout variant="intuition">
Picture a chromosome as a long sentence copied by hand each generation. Recombination is
a scissors cut that swaps the remainder of the sentence with the other copy. Two letters
sitting next to each other are almost never separated by a cut, so they travel together
for thousands of generations. Two letters at opposite ends are separated almost every
time, and after a few generations knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

Linkage disequilibrium measures how far that "travelling together" has survived. It is
a property of a *population's history*, not of any individual — and it decays, which is
what makes it a clock.
</Callout>

## Setting up: two loci, four haplotypes

Take two biallelic loci on the same chromosome. Locus 1 carries alleles $A$ and $a$;
locus 2 carries $B$ and $b$. A **haplotype** is the pair of alleles that sit on the *same
physical chromosome copy* — so there are four, $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$ and $ab$, and their
population frequencies are the four numbers everything below is built from.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { symbol: 'p_A,\; p_a', meaning: 'Frequencies of the two alleles at locus 1; p_a = 1 − p_A', dims: 'in (0,1)' },
    { symbol: 'p_B,\; p_b', meaning: 'Frequencies of the two alleles at locus 2', dims: 'in (0,1)' },

    { symbol: 'r^2', meaning: 'Squared correlation between the two loci, scored as 0/1', dims: 'in [0, 1]' },
    { symbol: '\\theta', meaning: 'Recombination fraction: chance of a crossover between the loci per meiosis', dims: 'in [0, 0.5]' },

    { symbol: 't', meaning: 'Generations elapsed', dims: 'count' },

/>

The four haplotype frequencies and the four allele frequencies are related by summing
across the table:

| | $B$ | $b$ | row total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | $p_{AB}$ | $p_{Ab}$ | $p_A$ |
| $a$ | $p_{aB}$ | $p_{ab}$ | $p_a$ |
| **column total** | $p_B$ | $p_b$ | $1$ |

<Callout variant="assumption" title="What is assumed, and what is being estimated">
Everything below describes **one randomly mating population** at **one point in time**,
with the two loci on the **same chromosome**, and treats the four haplotype frequencies
as known exactly. The estimand is a property of that population, not of a sample.

Three consequences are worth stating now, because they are the usual sources of
confusion later:

- LD is **not** the same as physical linkage. Loci on different chromosomes can be in LD
  (through population structure or recent admixture), and tightly linked loci can be in
  equilibrium if enough generations have passed.
- Everything is **frequency-dependent**. Two of the three measures below cannot reach
  their maximum unless the two loci have similar allele frequencies.
- In real data the haplotype frequencies are *estimated*, usually from unphased genotypes
  by EM or by statistical phasing, and that estimation error propagates into every number
  computed here.
</Callout>

## Three measures, and why they disagree

### D — the raw departure from independence

If the two loci were inherited independently, the frequency of the $AB$ haplotype would
be the product of the marginals. The **gametic disequilibrium** <Citation id="robbins1918" />
is exactly the gap between what is observed and that product:

$$
D \;=\; p_{AB} - p_A\,p_B \;=\; p_{AB}\,p_{ab} - p_{Ab}\,p_{aB}
$$

The second form is the determinant of the 2×2 table, and it is the more useful one: it
shows immediately that $D = 0$ precisely when the table has rank one, i.e. when the rows
are proportional and the loci are independent.

$D$ has the right sign structure — positive when $A$ and $B$ co-occur more than chance,
negative when they repel — but it is almost useless as a *magnitude*, because its range
depends on the allele frequencies. If $p_A = 0.01$, then $p_{AB} \le 0.01$, so $D$ cannot
exceed $0.01$ no matter how tightly the loci are linked. A value of $D = 0.01$ therefore
means something completely different at $p_A = 0.01$ than at $p_A = 0.5$.

The two standard fixes both divide $D$ by something. They divide by *different* things,
and that is the entire reason they disagree.

### D′ — normalised by what the frequencies allow

Lewontin <Citation id="lewontin1964" /> divides by the largest $|D|$ that the marginal
frequencies could possibly produce. Since all four haplotype frequencies must be
non-negative, $D$ is bounded, and the bound depends on its sign:

$$
D' = \frac{D}{D_{\max}}, \qquad
D_{\max} = \begin{cases}
\min(p_A p_b,\; p_a p_B) & D > 0 \\[4pt]
\min(p_A p_B,\; p_a p_b) & D < 0
\end{cases}
$$

$|D'| = 1$ has a sharp interpretation: it means **at least one of the four haplotypes is
absent from the population**. And a haplotype can only be missing if no recombination has
ever separated the two alleles since the younger of them arose. So $D'$ measures
*historical recombination* — it answers "has this pair ever been split?"

### r² — the correlation, and the one that predicts power

Hill and Robertson <Citation id="hill1968" /> instead score each locus $0/1$ by allele
count and take the squared Pearson correlation between them. Because a Bernoulli
variable with frequency $p$ has variance $p(1-p)$, and the covariance of the two
indicators is exactly $D$:

$$
r^2 \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}^2}{\operatorname{Var}_1 \operatorname{Var}_2}
      \;=\; \frac{D^2}{p_A\,p_a\,p_B\,p_b}
$$

This is the one that matters for study design, because it is a statement about
*information*. If a causal variant is not on the array but a genotyped tag SNP correlates
with it at $r^2$, then testing the tag instead of the cause costs power exactly as if the
sample had shrunk: you need $N/r^2$ samples to recover what $N$ samples would have given
by testing the cause directly <Citation id="pritchard2001" />.

<Callout variant="warning" title="D′ = 1 does not mean the variant is tagged">
These two measures answer different questions and routinely give opposite impressions of
the same pair of SNPs.

$D'$ asks a question about *history*: has recombination ever separated these alleles?
$r^2$ asks a question about *information*: does knowing one tell me the other?

A rare variant sitting inside a common haplotype has never been separated from it — so
$D' = 1$ — while telling you almost nothing about it, because the common allele is
present on many chromosomes the rare one is not. The worked example below is exactly this
case, and the stretch exercise shows the ceiling is not a curiosity but a hard bound.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Complete LD with almost no tagging"
  given={[
    { label: 'AB haplotypes', value: '100' },
    { label: 'Ab haplotypes', value: '0' },
    { label: 'aB haplotypes', value: '400' },
    { label: 'ab haplotypes', value: '500' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

A sample of 1,000 phased chromosomes. Note that the $Ab$ haplotype never occurs — $A$ is
found only alongside $B$.

**Step 1 — haplotype and allele frequencies.** Divide through by 1,000, then sum:

$$
p_{AB} = 0.10,\quad p_{Ab} = 0.00,\quad p_{aB} = 0.40,\quad p_{ab} = 0.50
$$

$$
p_A = 0.10 + 0.00 = 0.10, \qquad p_B = 0.10 + 0.40 = 0.50
$$

so $p_a = 0.90$ and $p_b = 0.50$. Allele $A$ is rare; allele $B$ is common.

**Step 2 — D.** Using the definition and then the determinant form as a check:

$$
D = p_{AB} - p_A p_B = 0.10 - (0.10)(0.50) = 0.05
$$

$$
D = p_{AB}p_{ab} - p_{Ab}p_{aB} = (0.10)(0.50) - (0.00)(0.40) = 0.05 \;\checkmark
$$

**Step 3 — D′.** $D > 0$, so take the positive branch:

$$
D_{\max} = \min(p_A p_b,\; p_a p_B) = \min(0.10 \times 0.50,\; 0.90 \times 0.50) = \min(0.05,\, 0.45) = 0.05
$$

$$
D' = \frac{0.05}{0.05} = 1.000
$$

Complete LD — as it must be, since a haplotype is missing.

**Step 4 — r².** Same $D$, different denominator:

$$
r^2 = \frac{D^2}{p_A p_a p_B p_b} = \frac{0.05^2}{0.10 \times 0.90 \times 0.50 \times 0.50}
    = \frac{0.0025}{0.0225} = 0.1111
$$

**Step 5 — what it costs.** The two measures could hardly disagree more: $D' = 1.000$ and
$r^2 = 0.1111$. Recombination has *never* separated these alleles, and the tag still
carries only 11% of the information. To detect the causal variant through this tag you
would need

$$
\frac{N}{r^2} = \frac{N}{0.1111} = 9N
$$

— nine times the sample size. The frequency mismatch, not the recombination history, is
what destroyed the power.

</WorkedExample>

## How LD decays

LD is not a fixed property of a pair of loci; it is the residue of history, and
recombination erodes it at a rate we can derive exactly.

**Step 1 — where next generation's haplotypes come from.** Consider a gamete produced in
generation $t+1$. With probability $1-\theta$ no crossover fell between the two loci, and
the gamete carries an intact parental haplotype — drawn from the parental frequencies, so
it is $AB$ with probability $p_{AB}^{(t)}$. With probability $\theta$ a crossover did
fall between them, and the two alleles are drawn from *different* parental chromosomes,
independently: $AB$ with probability $p_A p_B$. Hence

$$
p_{AB}^{(t+1)} \;=\; (1-\theta)\,p_{AB}^{(t)} \;+\; \theta\, p_A p_B
$$

**Step 2 — subtract independence from both sides.** Allele frequencies do not change under
recombination alone (it reshuffles alleles, it does not create or destroy them), so $p_A$
and $p_B$ carry no generation index. Subtracting $p_A p_B$:

$$
p_{AB}^{(t+1)} - p_A p_B \;=\; (1-\theta)\,p_{AB}^{(t)} + \theta p_A p_B - p_A p_B
= (1-\theta)\left(p_{AB}^{(t)} - p_A p_B\right)
$$

which is exactly

$$
D_{t+1} = (1-\theta)\,D_t
$$

**Step 3 — iterate.** A constant multiplier applied $t$ times is a geometric decay, and
for small $\theta$ it is very nearly exponential:

$$
D_t = D_0\,(1-\theta)^t \;\approx\; D_0\,e^{-\theta t}
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="LD decays geometrically, and never recovers">
$D$ shrinks by a constant *fraction* every generation. Nothing in this derivation can
increase it: recombination only ever moves the population toward independence. LD is
therefore created by other forces — mutation putting a new allele on one specific
background, drift, selection, admixture, population bottlenecks — and then ground down by
recombination at a rate set entirely by $\theta$.
</Callout>

### Half-life

Set $D_t/D_0 = 1/2$ and solve for $t$:

$$
(1-\theta)^{t_{1/2}} = \tfrac{1}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(1-\theta)} \;\approx\; \frac{0.693}{\theta}
$$

The approximation uses $\ln(1-\theta) \approx -\theta$, which is good for the small
$\theta$ that matters in practice and noticeably loose above a few percent.

Two markers 0.1 cM apart still show half the disequilibrium they started with. How long
ago was the ancestral haplotype intact?

**Step 1 — exact half-life.**

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(1 - 0.001)} = \frac{-0.693147}{-0.001000\,50} = 692.8 \text{ generations}
$$

**Step 2 — compare with the approximation.** $0.693/\theta = 0.693/0.001 = 693.0$
generations, which is high by $0.2$ generations, or $0.03\%$. At $\theta = 0.1$ the same
approximation gives $6.93$ against an exact $6.58$ — high by $5.3\%$. The approximation
is excellent where it is used and poor where it is not.

**Step 3 — convert to years.** At 29 years per human generation,

$$
692.8 \times 29 \approx 20{,}000 \text{ years}
$$

So the LD around a 0.1 cM interval carries information on roughly the last twenty
millennia — which is why LD-based methods can speak to post-glacial demography, and why
they say very little about events much older than that.

</WorkedExample>

## From recombination fraction to map distance

$\theta$ is not additive. Over a long interval, two crossovers can occur and cancel,
returning a non-recombinant gamete; $\theta$ therefore saturates at $0.5$ (free
assortment) however far apart the loci are. **Genetic distance** $d$, measured in Morgans
— one Morgan being one expected crossover per meiosis — *is* additive, and a **mapping
function** converts between them.

Haldane <Citation id="haldane1919" /> assumed crossovers fall as a Poisson process with
no interference. A gamete is recombinant when an odd number of crossovers occurred, and
summing the odd terms of a Poisson with mean $2d$ gives

$$
\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 - e^{-2d}\right)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
d = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 - 2\theta)
$$

Real chromosomes show **crossover interference**: one chiasma suppresses another nearby.
Kosambi <Citation id="kosambi1943" /> built that in empirically:

$$
\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\tanh(2d)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
d = \tfrac{1}{4}\ln\!\left(\frac{1+2\theta}{1-2\theta}\right)
$$

Both reduce to $\theta \approx d$ for small $d$, which is why "1 cM ≈ 1% recombination"
is a safe shorthand at the scale of a haplotype block and a bad one across a whole
chromosome arm.

## Why recombination is not uniform

The derivation above treats $\theta$ as a property of an interval, which quietly implies
crossovers are spread evenly along the chromosome. In mammals they are emphatically not.
More than 80% of human crossovers fall in narrow 1–2 kb **hotspots** occupying a few
percent of the sequence, and their placement is directed by a single rapidly evolving
zinc-finger protein, PRDM9 <Citation id="baudat2010" /><Citation id="myers2010" />.

<Callout variant="note" title="The hotspot cascade">
1. **Sequence recognition.** PRDM9's C2H2 zinc-finger array binds a degenerate 13-bp
   motif. The array is one of the fastest-evolving coding sequences in the genome, so
   hotspot *locations* differ between human populations and are almost entirely
   non-overlapping between human and chimpanzee.
2. **Epigenetic marking.** Its SET domain trimethylates flanking nucleosomes — H3K4me3
   together with H3K36me3, a combination that distinguishes hotspots from promoters.
3. **Programmed breakage.** SPO11, a topoisomerase-like transesterase, is recruited and
   cuts both DNA strands.
4. **Repair.** RAD51 and DMC1 drive homologous strand invasion. The break resolves either
   as a **crossover** — reciprocal exchange, which is what breaks LD — or as a
   **non-crossover gene conversion**, a short patch copied across with no exchange of
   flanking sequence.

The last point matters for the mathematics: only crossovers contribute to $\theta$ as
used above, and they are the *minority* outcome of a double-strand break.
</Callout>

## Haplotype blocks, tag SNPs and imputation

Punctate recombination has a direct structural consequence: the genome is partitioned
into segments of 10–100 kb within which recombination is rare, separated by hotspots
where it is concentrated. Gabriel and colleagues <Citation id="gabriel2002" /> made this
operational using confidence bounds on $D'$ — a marker pair is in strong LD when the
upper 95% bound on $D'$ is at least $0.98$ and the lower bound at least $0.70$, and a
block is a run in which at least 95% of informative pairs meet that criterion. The HapMap
Project <Citation id="hapmap2005" /> then mapped these blocks genome-wide, and the 1000
Genomes Project <Citation id="tgp2015" /> extended the reference to the full frequency
spectrum.

Within a block of 50 SNPs there are typically only three to five common haplotypes rather
than the $2^{50}$ that free assortment would allow. Genotyping a few **tag SNPs** is
therefore enough to identify which haplotype a chromosome carries, and the rest can be
filled in statistically. That is precisely what **imputation** does: a hidden Markov model
treats the target chromosome as a mosaic of reference haplotypes and infers the unobserved
genotypes <Citation id="das2016" /><Citation id="browning2018" />. A million measured
markers become tens of millions of tested ones, and the entire procedure rests on the
block structure that PRDM9 and recombination together produce.

It also explains the characteristic shape of a GWAS result. An association signal is
never a single point; it is a *block* of correlated markers rising and falling together,
because they are all tagging the same underlying haplotype. Separating the causal variant
from its correlated neighbours is a distinct statistical problem — fine-mapping — and one
that LD makes unavoidable <Citation id="slatkin2008" />.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Compute all three measures" difficulty="warm-up">

A sample of 200 phased chromosomes gives $AB = 90$, $Ab = 10$, $aB = 30$, $ab = 70$.
Compute $p_A$, $p_B$, $D$, $D'$ and $r^2$.

<div slot="solution">

Frequencies: $p_{AB} = 0.45$, $p_{Ab} = 0.05$, $p_{aB} = 0.15$, $p_{ab} = 0.35$.

Marginals: $p_A = 0.45 + 0.05 = 0.50$ and $p_B = 0.45 + 0.15 = 0.60$, so $p_a = 0.50$,
$p_b = 0.40$.

$$
D = 0.45 - (0.50)(0.60) = 0.15
$$

$D > 0$, so $D_{\max} = \min(p_A p_b,\, p_a p_B) = \min(0.20,\, 0.30) = 0.20$ and
$D' = 0.15/0.20 = 0.75$.

$$
r^2 = \frac{0.15^2}{(0.50)(0.50)(0.60)(0.40)} = \frac{0.0225}{0.06} = 0.375
$$

Here the two measures broadly agree, because the allele frequencies are similar. That
agreement is the special case, not the rule.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="A missing haplotype forces |D′| = 1" difficulty="core">

Show that if any one of the four haplotypes has frequency zero, then $|D'| = 1$. Take
$p_{Ab} = 0$ and work through the positive branch.

<div slot="solution">

With $p_{Ab} = 0$ the marginals collapse: $p_A = p_{AB}$ and $p_b = p_{ab}$, while
$p_a = p_{aB} + p_{ab}$ and $p_B = p_{AB} + p_{aB}$.

The determinant form gives $D = p_{AB}p_{ab} - 0 = p_{AB}p_{ab} > 0$, so
$D_{\max} = \min(p_A p_b,\, p_a p_B)$.

The first candidate is $p_A p_b = p_{AB}\,p_{ab} = D$. For the second, expand:

$$
p_a p_B = (p_{aB} + p_{ab})(p_{AB} + p_{aB}) = p_{AB}p_{ab} + p_{aB}p_{AB} + p_{aB}^2 + p_{aB}p_{ab}
$$

Every added term is non-negative, so $p_a p_B \ge p_A p_b$. The minimum is therefore
$p_A p_b = D$, giving $D' = D/D = 1$.

The interpretation is the point: a haplotype can be missing only if no recombination has
ever put those two alleles together, so $|D'| = 1$ is a statement about history rather
than about how well one marker predicts the other.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The ceiling on tagging a rare variant" difficulty="stretch">

A causal variant has $p_A = 0.05$. The best available tag SNP has $p_B = 0.50$ and is in
complete LD with it ($D' = 1$, $D > 0$). What is $r^2$, and what does it imply for a study
powered at $N = 10{,}000$ for direct testing?

<div slot="solution">

$D' = 1$ with $D > 0$ means $D = D_{\max} = \min(p_A p_b,\, p_a p_B)$:

$$
D = \min(0.05 \times 0.50,\; 0.95 \times 0.50) = \min(0.025,\, 0.475) = 0.025
$$

$$
r^2 = \frac{0.025^2}{(0.05)(0.95)(0.50)(0.50)} = \frac{0.000625}{0.011875} = 0.0526
$$

So $r^2 \le 0.0526$ — and since $D' = 1$ is the *best possible* case, no tag SNP at
frequency $0.50$ can do better. Reaching the power of direct testing needs
$N/r^2 = 10{,}000/0.0526 = 190{,}000$ samples, a nineteen-fold increase.

In general, when $D' = 1$ and $p_A < p_B$, the algebra above reduces to

$$
r^2 = \frac{p_A(1 - p_B)}{(1 - p_A)\,p_B}
$$

which goes to zero as the frequencies diverge. This is the structural reason arrays are
poor at rare variants no matter how dense they are, and why rare-variant work moved to
sequencing rather than to better tagging.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **$D = p_{AB} - p_A p_B$** is the raw departure from independence, and is not
  interpretable on its own because its range depends on the allele frequencies.
- **$D'$ normalises by the maximum the frequencies permit.** $|D'| = 1$ means a haplotype
  is absent, hence no historical recombination — a statement about the past.
- **$r^2$ normalises by the variances.** It is the squared correlation, and it converts
  directly into study cost: tagging at $r^2$ multiplies the required sample size by
  $1/r^2$. The two measures diverge whenever allele frequencies differ, which is why
  $D' = 1$ and $r^2 = 0.11$ can describe the same pair of SNPs.
- **LD decays geometrically**, $D_t = D_0(1-\theta)^t$, with half-life
  $t_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(1-\theta) \approx 0.693/\theta$. Recombination only ever destroys
  LD; other forces create it.
- **Recombination is punctate**, directed by PRDM9 to narrow hotspots, which partitions
  the genome into haplotype blocks — the structure that makes tag SNPs, imputation and
  therefore GWAS itself possible, and that makes fine-mapping necessary.
