## The question this lesson answers

A standard pipeline keeps about two thousand genes out of twenty thousand and throws the rest
away. Everything after — the principal components, the neighbour graph, the clusters, the
embedding — is computed on that tenth of the genome, so the selection is not a preprocessing
detail. It decides what structure the analysis is capable of finding.

It is also made before the question is asked. Nothing about "highly variable" refers to the
comparison the experiment was designed for; the criterion is unsupervised, and a gene that
separates the two conditions perfectly but varies little overall will not be selected. That
trade is usually worth making, because supervised selection on the same data that is later
tested is circular. But it is a trade, and it is worth knowing precisely what the unsupervised
criterion is measuring.

The answer, for every simple criterion, is: expression level.

## Every simple criterion ranks by expression

The cleanest way to see this is to feed each criterion genes that are, by construction,
equally interesting: pure Poisson counts with no biological variance at all. A criterion that
measures biology should rank them equally. None of them does.

**Raw variance ranks by mean, ascending.** For a Poisson count, $\sigma^2 = \mu$ exactly. So a
gene at a mean of 100 has fifty times the variance of one at a mean of 2, and a
variance-ranked list is simply a list of the most-expressed genes. This is why nobody uses raw
variance — but it is worth stating, because it establishes that the problem is structural
rather than a flaw in one method.

**Variance after log1p ranks by mean, descending.** Lesson 5 showed that the standard deviation
of a log1p-transformed Poisson count is not flat: it peaks near a mean of 1.72 and falls away
on both sides. Squaring, the variance of our mean-2 gene is 0.262448 and of our mean-100 gene
0.009950 — so the criterion now prefers the *lower*-expressed gene by a factor of **26.38**.
Two genes that are statistically identical are ranked in opposite orders by the two most
obvious criteria, which is a useful demonstration that neither is measuring what it claims.

**The Fano factor does not rescue it.** The classical fix is to divide out the mean:
$\mathrm{Fano} = \sigma^2/\mu$, which is exactly 1 for any Poisson regardless of level. On our
two noise genes it correctly returns 1 and 1.

But real genes are overdispersed, and for a negative binomial

$$
\mathrm{Fano} = \frac{\mu + \mu^2/\theta}{\mu} = 1 + \frac{\mu}{\theta},
$$

which grows linearly in the mean. Two genes with *identical* dispersion $\theta = 1$ — the same
biological variability by any reasonable definition — have Fano factors of 3.0 at a mean of 2
and 101.0 at a mean of 100. The correction works on the null and fails on the signal, which is
the worst possible place for a correction to fail.

## What is actually scale-free

Rearranging the same expression isolates the quantity that does not depend on the mean:

$$
\frac{\sigma^2 - \mu}{\mu^2} = \frac{1}{\theta}.
$$

The excess variance over Poisson, divided by the *square* of the mean, is exactly the inverse
dispersion — and it is the same number for both genes above. That is the quantity a selection
criterion should be ranking on, and it is the same $\theta$ that lesson 2 fitted from the
moments.

In practice the trend is fitted rather than assumed. The methods differ in how:

- **Fit the technical trend from spike-ins** and rank by the excess above it. The original
  approach, and the most defensible where spike-ins exist <Citation id="brennecke2013hvg" />.
- **Fit a smooth curve of variance against mean across all genes** and rank by the residual.
  This is what `vst` does, and it makes the reasonable assumption that most genes are not
  variable, so the bulk of them define the null <Citation id="stuart2019seurat3" />.
- **Fit a count model directly** and use its dispersion, or skip selection and let a GLM-based
  dimension reduction weight genes by their likelihood contribution
  <Citation id="hafemeister2019sctransform" />
<Citation id="townes2019glmpca" />. Pearson
  residual variance is a particularly clean version: under the null it is 1 for every gene, so
  the excess above 1 is directly interpretable <Citation id="lause2021pearson" />.

Benchmarks of the many available methods find they agree on the strongest genes and diverge
substantially in the middle of the ranking <Citation id="yip2019hvgbenchmark" />, which matters
precisely because the cut is usually taken at a fixed count rather than at a threshold.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Two thousand is a budget, not a discovery">

The number 2,000 is not estimated from anything. It is a default, and it propagates: papers
report "the 2,000 most variable genes" as though the data had nominated them.

What the number actually controls is a bias–variance trade on the geometry. Too few genes and
rare populations lose the features that distinguish them, because a cell type that is 1% of the
sample contributes little to any global variance ranking. Too many and the distances are
dominated by genes carrying only noise, which is the distance concentration problem of
lesson 8.

The useful discipline is to check that the finding survives the choice. Re-run at 1,000 and
5,000 genes; if the clusters you are about to build a paper on appear at 2,000 and not at
5,000, that is a result about the selection, not about the tissue
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Ranking two genes that are the same gene"
  given={[
    'Gene A: mean 2 UMIs per cell',
    'Gene B: mean 100 UMIs per cell',
    'Both pure Poisson — no biological variance whatsoever',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — raw variance.** For a Poisson, variance equals the mean, so $\sigma^2_A = 2$ and
$\sigma^2_B = 100$. Gene B ranks **50.0×** above gene A.

**Step 2 — variance after log1p.** From lesson 5's machinery, the variance of the transformed
count is 0.262448 for gene A and 0.009950 for gene B. Gene B now ranks

$$
\frac{0.009950}{0.262448} = 0.0379
$$

times gene A — that is, gene A ranks **26.38×** above gene B. The ordering has reversed.

**Step 3 — the Fano factor.** $\sigma^2/\mu = 1$ for both, since both are Poisson. On these two
genes the correction works.

**Step 4 — the Fano factor on genes that are actually variable.** Give both genes the same real
dispersion $\theta = 1$. Then $\sigma^2 = \mu + \mu^2$, so

$$
\mathrm{Fano}_A = \frac{2 + 4}{2} = 3.0,
\qquad
\mathrm{Fano}_B = \frac{100 + 10{,}000}{100} = 101.0.
$$

Identical dispersion, and the criterion separates them **33.7×**. It was flat only on the null.

**Step 5 — the scale-free quantity.**

$$
\frac{\sigma^2 - \mu}{\mu^2} = \frac{6 - 2}{4} = 1,
\qquad
\frac{10{,}100 - 100}{10{,}000} = 1,
$$

both exactly $1/\theta = 1$. This is the number that does not care how expressed the gene is,
and it is the one a trend-residual criterion is estimating.

**The moral.** Three of the four criteria are ranking by expression level. That is not a subtle
statistical point: it means a highly variable gene list built with any of them is substantially
a list of highly *expressed* genes, and the enrichment analyses run on such lists inherit that.

</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Feature selection is **unsupervised and irreversible**: everything downstream sees only the
  selected genes, and the criterion never refers to the question being asked.
- **Raw variance ranks by mean** — for a Poisson, $\sigma^2 = \mu$ exactly, so a mean-100 gene
  beats a mean-2 gene **50×** with no biological difference between them.
- **log1p variance ranks by mean in the opposite direction**, preferring the mean-2 gene by
  **26.38×**, because the transform compresses high counts (lesson 5).
- **The Fano factor is flat on the null and not on the signal**: $1 + \mu/\theta$, so two genes
  with the same $\theta = 1$ score 3.0 and 101.0.
- The scale-free quantity is $(\sigma^2 - \mu)/\mu^2 = 1/\theta$ — the same dispersion lesson 2
  fitted. Trend-residual methods estimate it by fitting the bulk of genes as the null.
- **2,000 is a budget, not a discovery.** Re-run at 1,000 and 5,000; a cluster that appears at
  only one of them is a fact about the selection.

<Exercise n={1} title="A criterion on the null and on the signal" difficulty="warm-up">

Two genes have means 5 and 50.

(a) If both are pure Poisson, what is each one's Fano factor?

(b) If both have dispersion $\theta = 2$, what is each one's Fano factor?

(c) State in one sentence what (a) and (b) together show about the Fano factor.

<div slot="solution">

(a) A Poisson has $\sigma^2 = \mu$, so both have Fano **1.0**. The criterion is correctly flat.

(b) $\mathrm{Fano} = 1 + \mu/\theta$, so the mean-5 gene gives $1 + 2.5 = 3.5$ and the mean-50
gene gives $1 + 25 = 26.0$ — a separation of **7.43×** between genes of identical dispersion.

(c) The Fano factor is scale-free exactly when there is nothing to detect, and acquires a
mean-dependence proportional to the overdispersion it is supposed to be measuring.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Recovering the dispersion from two genes" difficulty="core">

Gene P has observed mean 5 and variance 20. Gene Q has observed mean 50 and variance 2,550.

(a) Compute each gene's Fano factor, and say which looks more variable.

(b) Compute $(\sigma^2 - \mu)/\mu^2$ for each, and say which is more variable.

(c) Which answer should go in the paper, and why do the two disagree?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\mathrm{Fano}_P = 20/5 = 4.0$ and $\mathrm{Fano}_Q = 2{,}550/50 = 51.0$. By this criterion
Q is **12.75×** more variable.

(b) $(20 - 5)/25 = 0.6$ and $(2{,}550 - 50)/2{,}500 = 1.0$, so $\theta_P = 1/0.6 = 1.6667$ and
$\theta_Q = 1.0$. Q is more variable, but only by a factor of **1.67**, not 12.75.

(c) The second. The two disagree because the Fano factor carries a $\mu/\theta$ term that grows
with expression, so most of the apparent 12.75-fold gap is Q simply being ten times more
expressed. The genes do differ — Q really is more overdispersed — but by less than a fifth of
what the first criterion reported.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="What a fixed gene count commits you to" difficulty="stretch">

A tissue contains a population that is 1% of the cells and differs from its neighbours in
forty genes, all of moderate expression.

(a) Why might those forty genes fail to enter a top-2,000 list, even under a well-behaved
trend-residual criterion?

(b) Propose a check that would reveal the problem, and state what a negative result would and
would not establish.

(c) Explain why simply raising the count to 10,000 genes is not a general fix.

<div slot="solution">

(a) A trend-residual criterion measures variance across *all* cells. A gene that differs
sharply between a 1% population and everything else has, across the whole dataset, a mixture
distribution that is 99% one component — so its total variance is inflated only slightly. The
gene is genuinely variable *within the comparison that matters* and barely variable overall,
and the criterion is blind to the distinction because it never sees the comparison.

(b) Re-run the selection and clustering on progressively larger gene sets — 2,000, 5,000,
10,000 and all genes — and watch whether a new cluster appears. If the rare population only
resolves at 10,000 genes, the 2,000-gene analysis was missing it. A negative result establishes
that no *additional* population is recovered by relaxing the gene budget; it does not establish
that none exists, because a population differing in a handful of genes may not resolve at any
gene count without a targeted comparison.

(c) Because the trade runs both ways. Adding genes adds their noise to every distance, and in
high dimensions that noise accumulates faster than the signal does — the distance concentration
problem. Past some point, extra genes make all cells look equidistant and rare populations
resolve *worse*, not better. There is no single count that is right for every dataset, which is
why the check in (b) is the answer rather than a larger default. This is also why methods that
weight genes continuously by their likelihood contribution, rather than admitting a fixed
number, avoid the question entirely <Citation id="ahlmanneltze2023transformations" />.

</div>

</Exercise>
