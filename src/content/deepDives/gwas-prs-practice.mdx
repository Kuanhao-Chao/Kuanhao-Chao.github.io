## The question this lesson answers

You have summary statistics. Somebody wants a number per person, and somebody else wants to
know what that number means.

The estimator theory — shrinkage, the accuracy ceiling, why $R^2$ approaches $h^2$ and never
reaches it — is in
[Polygenic Risk Scores](/deep_dives/statgen-polygenic-risk-scores/). This lesson is the
pipeline and the reporting, and the reporting is where most of the damage happens.

<Callout variant="intuition">

A polygenic score is a weighted sum, and almost all the difficulty is in two places that have
nothing to do with the sum. **Which variants get a weight**, because the raw summary
statistics contain thousands of correlated copies of each signal and every weight near the
significance threshold is inflated by the winner's curse. And **what the resulting number
means**, because a score's spread is easy to report in a way that is technically true and
practically misleading.

</Callout>

## Building it

**Clump first.** The previous lessons established why: raw summary statistics carry one signal
many times over, and a score that sums fifty correlated copies of the same locus weights it
fifty-fold. Clumping <Citation id="purcell2007plink" /> keeps the strongest variant per LD
neighbourhood — signal-aware, unlike pruning.

**Then either threshold or shrink.**

*Clumping and thresholding* keeps variants below a p-value cut and uses their raw effect
estimates. It is simple, transparent, and inherits two problems: the cut is arbitrary, and
the raw estimates near it are inflated <Citation id="palmer2017winners" />.

*Bayesian shrinkage* keeps everything and shrinks each weight toward zero in proportion to how
uncertain it is <Citation id="vilhjalmsson2015" />. It usually wins, and the reason connects
directly to the previous point — shrinkage corrects the winner's curse as a side effect,
because the weights it shrinks hardest are exactly the marginal ones the curse inflates most.

**Then tune, and this is where scores go wrong.** The p-value threshold, or the shrinkage
prior, has to be chosen somehow, and choosing it means trying several and keeping the best.
That requires a cohort — and it must not be the cohort you report accuracy in.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Three cohorts, not two">

**Discovery** produces the summary statistics. **Tuning** picks the threshold or the prior.
**Testing** reports the accuracy, and is touched exactly once.

Collapsing tuning and testing is the most common methodological error in the polygenic score
literature <Citation id="choi2020" />, and it is easy to commit without noticing: trying ten
thresholds and reporting the best $R^2$ in the same cohort is selecting the maximum of ten
noisy estimates and calling it an estimate. Overlap between *discovery* and testing is worse
still and produces accuracies that are impossible — the score is partly predicting samples it
was fitted on.

</Callout>

## What a percentile actually means

<WorkedExample
  title="A score explaining 10% of liability, for a disease affecting 2% of people"
  given={[
    { label: 'Prevalence', value: 'K = 0.02' },
    { label: 'Score accuracy', value: 'r² = 0.10 of liability variance' },
    { label: 'Model', value: 'liability threshold, liability standard normal' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — where the threshold sits.** Under the liability-threshold model
<Citation id="lee2011estimating" />, liability is standard normal and disease occurs above

$$
T = \Phi^{-1}(1 - K) = \Phi^{-1}(0.98) = 2.053749 .
$$

**Step 2 — what the score does to it.** A score explaining $r^2$ of liability shifts an
individual's expected liability by $r z$ and leaves residual variance $1 - r^2$, so

$$
\text{risk}(z) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{r z - T}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}\right), \qquad r = \sqrt{0.10} = 0.316228 .
$$

**Step 3 — read it at the extremes.** The 99th centile has $z = 2.3263$:

$$
\text{risk} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{0.316228 \times 2.3263 - 2.053749}{\sqrt{0.90}}\right) = 0.082357 .
$$

The 1st centile, at $z = -2.3263$, gives $0.001640$. The 90th centile gives $0.041136$.

**Step 4 — the two sentences.** Dividing the extremes:

$$
\frac{0.082357}{0.001640} = 50.2 .
$$

*"People in the top 1% have fifty times the risk of those in the bottom 1%."* True.

Subtracting from one:

$$
1 - 0.082357 = 0.917643 .
$$

*"People in the top 1% have a 91.76% chance of never developing the disease."* Also true, of
the same score, at the same centile.

**Step 5 — which to report.** Both, always. A fold-change without an absolute risk is
uninterpretable, because the same 50-fold ratio describes a score that moves risk from 0.16%
to 8.2% and one that moves it from 1% to 50%. Those are different clinical objects and the
ratio cannot distinguish them. This is not a subtle statistical point; it is the single most
common way polygenic scores are oversold, and the fix costs one extra number.

</WorkedExample>

## Where the accuracy goes when the ancestry changes

A score fitted in one population loses accuracy in another, and the loss decomposes into
three parts with different prospects <Citation id="martin2019" />.

**Different LD.** The score's weights sit on tag variants, and lesson 7 showed that tagging
is population-specific: a tag with $r^2 = 0.64$ to the causal variant in the discovery
population may have $r^2 = 0.08$ in the target. The weight is then applied to a variant that
no longer stands for what it stood for. This part is fixable in principle — fine-map to the
causal variant and weight *that* — and is one of the practical arguments for the previous
lesson.

**Different allele frequencies.** A variant common in one population and rare in another
contributes almost nothing to score variance in the second, regardless of its weight. Also
partly fixable, by re-estimating in the target population.

**Different effects.** Gene-by-environment interaction and differences in the trait's
architecture mean the underlying effect may genuinely differ. Not fixable by any
reweighting — it requires data from the target population.

The practical consequence is blunt: a score's reported accuracy is a property of the
population it was tested in, and carrying it across is not conservative. Reporting a
European-derived score's $R^2$ as though it were a property of the score
<Citation id="khera2018" /> is how scores end up deployed where they do not work.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A different disease" difficulty="warm-up">

A score explains $r^2 = 0.10$ of liability for a disease with prevalence $K = 0.10$.

(a) Where is the liability threshold?

(b) What is the absolute risk at the 99th centile?

(c) Compare the fold-change with the 2%-prevalence case in the worked example.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $T = \Phi^{-1}(0.90) = 1.281552$.

**(b)** $\text{risk} = \Phi((0.316228 \times 2.326348 - 1.281552)/\sqrt{0.90}) = 0.282502$, so
about 28.3%.

**(c)** The 1st centile gives $0.016738$, so the ratio is $0.282502/0.016738 = 16.9$ — much
*smaller* than the 50.2 of the worked example, for exactly the same score. Fold-changes shrink
as prevalence rises, because the threshold moves into the denser part of the liability
distribution where a given shift in liability changes risk proportionally less. A headline
fold-change therefore says as much about the disease's rarity as about the score's quality,
which is another reason it cannot stand alone.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="A suspiciously good score" difficulty="core">

A paper reports a score with $R^2 = 0.21$ for a trait whose SNP heritability is estimated at
0.18. The authors tried 12 p-value thresholds and report the best.

(a) What is impossible about this?

(b) Name two mechanisms that could produce it.

(c) What would you ask for?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $R^2$ cannot exceed $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$. A score built from additive SNP effects
predicts, at best, the additive SNP component — that is what $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$ is. An
accuracy above it is not an impressive result; it is evidence of an error.

**(b)** First, **sample overlap** between discovery and testing: the score is partly
predicting individuals whose phenotypes contributed to its weights. Second, **tuning on the
test set**: reporting the best of 12 thresholds evaluated in the same cohort selects the
maximum of twelve noisy estimates, which is biased upward by construction and by more when
the cohort is small.

**(c)** The cohort membership lists, or at least a statement that discovery, tuning and
testing are disjoint; the $R^2$ at all 12 thresholds rather than the best one; and the size of
the test cohort, since the selection bias in (b) grows as it shrinks. If the 12 values are
tightly clustered and the cohorts are disjoint, the heritability estimate is the thing to
re-examine instead.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Deploying a score" difficulty="stretch">

A health system proposes screening the top 5% of a polygenic score for a disease with
prevalence 1%, using a score with $r^2 = 0.15$ of liability. The screening test costs money
and has a false-positive rate that causes follow-up procedures.

(a) What is the absolute risk in the top 5%, and how many of those screened would develop the
disease?

(b) The score was developed in a European cohort and the health system's population is 40%
African-ancestry. What happens?

(c) What would you need before recommending this?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $T = \Phi^{-1}(0.99) = 2.326348$ and $r = \sqrt{0.15} = 0.387298$. The 95th centile
has $z = 1.644854$, giving $\text{risk} = 0.033453$ — about 3.3%. So roughly 33 in every
1,000 screened would develop the disease and about 967 would not. Whether that justifies
screening depends entirely on the procedure's harms, not on the score.

**(b)** The score's accuracy in the African-ancestry portion is lower — by the three
mechanisms above, and typically substantially. So the "top 5%" in that group is a less
enriched group than in the European portion, and the programme delivers less benefit to them
while exposing them to the same follow-up harms. A single population-wide threshold makes
the disparity invisible in the aggregate numbers, because the European majority dominates
the average.

**(c)** Validation in each ancestry group the system serves, reported separately, with
absolute risks rather than fold-changes; the number needed to screen and the harms of
follow-up in each group; and a decision about whether to use ancestry-specific thresholds,
which improves equity of benefit but raises its own questions. Absent per-group validation,
the honest position is that the score's performance in 40% of the population is unknown.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Clump before scoring.** Raw summary statistics carry each signal many times over, and a
  score that sums the copies weights the locus by however many tags it had.
- **Shrinkage usually beats thresholding**, and part of the reason is that it corrects the
  winner's curse as a side effect — the weights it shrinks hardest are the marginal ones the
  curse inflates most.
- **Three cohorts: discovery, tuning, testing.** Reporting the best of several thresholds in
  the cohort you report accuracy in is selecting a maximum and calling it an estimate.
- **A fold-change needs an absolute risk beside it.** The same score gives 50.2× between
  extreme centiles and a 91.76% chance of never developing the disease, and a 50-fold ratio
  equally describes moving risk from 0.16% to 8.2% or from 1% to 50%.
- **Fold-changes shrink as prevalence rises**, so a headline ratio reports the disease's
  rarity as much as the score's quality.
- **$R^2$ above $h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}$ is an error, not a result** — usually sample overlap or
  tuning on the test set.
- **Accuracy is a property of the population tested in.** The loss on transfer comes from LD,
  allele frequency and genuine effect differences; the first two are fixable with work, the
  third only with data from the target population.
