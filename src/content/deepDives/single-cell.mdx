## What this track is for

Measuring transcription one cell at a time turned a twenty-year-old average into a
distribution. Tissues that looked homogeneous in bulk turned out to hold a dozen states; rare
populations that bulk RNA-seq had diluted into invisibility became findable; and questions
about *which* cells respond to a treatment became askable at all. The methods that made this
routine arrived within a few years of each other <Citation id="macosko2015dropseq" />
<Citation id="zheng2017chromium" />, and the software that analyses their output is now mature
and well documented <Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" /><Citation id="hao2021seurat" />.

That maturity is the problem this track is written against. The standard pipeline runs to
completion on almost any input and returns clusters, markers and p-values whether or not any
of them mean anything. Nearly every default is defensible; the trouble is that defending a
default requires knowing what it assumes, and the tutorials that teach the steps rarely teach
the assumptions <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

So this track derives rather than prescribes. Seventeen lessons, five modules, each lesson
owning one decision — what a count is, what to filter, how to normalise, how many dimensions
to keep, how to decide there are seven clusters rather than twelve, whether a difference
between conditions is real. The order is the order of the pipeline, because the errors
compound in that order.

## The result the whole track is arranged around

Here is the finding that decides whether a single-cell paper is right, and it is arithmetic
rather than biology.

A typical experiment has a handful of donors per condition and thousands of cells per donor.
The obvious analysis treats each cell as an observation, which gives tens of thousands of
observations and correspondingly tiny p-values. But cells from one donor share that donor's
genotype, age, treatment history, batch, dissociation stress and sequencing run. They are
correlated, and correlated observations carry less information than independent ones.

Write $\rho$ for the share of a gene's variance that sits *between* donors rather than between
cells within a donor. Then the variance of a group mean computed from $m$ cells per donor is
larger than the independence assumption allows by the **design effect**

$$
\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (m - 1)\rho,
$$

which is linear in cells per donor and unbounded. Every test statistic computed as though the
cells were independent is inflated by $\sqrt{\mathrm{DE}}$, so a two-sided test at nominal
$\alpha$ actually rejects true nulls at

$$
\mathrm{FPR} = 2\Phi\!\left(\frac{-z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{1 + (m-1)\rho}}\right).
$$

At $\rho = 0.05$ — a modest value — and 500 cells per donor, a nominal 5% test rejects **70.0%**
of genes that do not differ at all. At 5,000 cells per donor it rejects **90.2%**. The test
gets worse as the experiment gets bigger, which is the opposite of how statistics is supposed
to behave, and it is why lists of thousands of "differentially expressed" genes with
astronomically small p-values are the field's most reproducible artefact
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Ask how many times the experiment was done">

The cure is a question, not a method: **how many times was this experiment performed?** If a
treatment was given to four mice and a vehicle to four others, the experiment was performed
eight times, and no amount of sequencing changes that. Twenty thousand cells is a very precise
measurement of those eight animals.

Everything in the statistical treatment follows from taking that seriously. Average each
animal's cells to one value per gene per cell type — a **pseudobulk** profile — and test those
eight numbers with an ordinary bulk method <Citation id="crowell2020muscat" />. It feels
wasteful, throwing away 20,000 observations to get 8. It is not: the 20,000 were never 20,000.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="What forty thousand cells are actually worth"
  given={[
    'Eight donors, 5,000 cells assayed from each',
    'Intra-donor correlation rho = 0.05 for the gene in question',
    'A per-cell test at nominal alpha = 0.05',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the design effect.**

$$
\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (5{,}000 - 1)(0.05) = 250.95.
$$

The variance of each group's mean is 251 times what the per-cell analysis believes it to be.

**Step 2 — what that does to the test.** Statistics are inflated by
$\sqrt{250.95} = 15.841$, so the nominal 5% critical value of 1.96 is really being applied at
$1.96/15.841 = 0.12372$ standard errors:

$$
\mathrm{FPR} = 2\Phi(-0.12372) = 0.9015.
$$

**90.2% of genes with no true difference will be called significant.** Not 5%.

**Step 3 — the effective sample size.** The 40,000 cells are worth

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{8 \times 5{,}000}{250.95} = 159.39
$$

independent cells — **0.398%** of the headcount.

**Step 4 — where the next money goes.** The effective count saturates at $n/\rho = 160$, so
the ceiling is already 99.6% reached. Doubling the cells per donor to 10,000 raises 159.39 to
159.70, a gain of **0.30**. Doubling the donors to sixteen instead raises it to 318.79, a gain
of **159.39** — over five hundred times as much, for a design change rather than a deeper
sequencing run.

**The rule this gives you.** Cells per donor buy *resolution* — the ability to see rarer cell
types and describe their states more finely. Donors buy *power*. They are different currencies
and they are not interchangeable, and almost every underpowered single-cell study in the
literature spent the whole budget on the first one.

</WorkedExample>

## How the track is organised

**Module 1 · What a Count Is** (lessons 1–3) builds the matrix from the bench up: the physical
path from tissue to integer, the count distribution that path implies, and the two artefacts —
ambient RNA and doublets — that manufacture cell types out of nothing. The headline is that
most zeros are not a separate biological phenomenon but ordinary counting statistics
<Citation id="svensson2020zeroinflation" />.

**Module 2 · Building a Usable Matrix** (4–6) covers the decisions that turn raw counts into
something analysable: distinguishing cells from empty droplets, quality filters priced in cells
retained, normalisation and why the standard log-transform is biased at low counts, and how
choosing 2,000 variable genes shapes everything downstream.

**Module 3 · Geometry** (7–10) is where the analysis becomes geometry: principal components and
how many to keep, the neighbour graph everything else is built on, clustering and the fact that
there is no true number of clusters, and what a UMAP does and does not preserve.

**Module 4 · Meaning** (11–13) attaches biology to structure: annotating clusters as cell
types, differential expression done correctly — where the result above is derived in full — and
compositional analysis, where the constraint that proportions sum to one makes one population's
expansion look like every other population's decline.

**Module 5 · Beyond One Snapshot** (14–17) handles the harder designs: integrating batches
without erasing the biology, trajectories and pseudotime, RNA velocity and the assumptions its
arrows rest on, and a closing survey of multi-omic and spatial assays with the analysis
assumption each one breaks.

## Summary

- A count matrix records **molecules recovered from a physical process**, not abundances.
  Module 1 builds it; every later module undoes one step of it.
- Cells within a sample are correlated, so the variance of a mean is inflated by the design
  effect $\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (m-1)\rho$ — linear in cells per sample and unbounded.
- A per-cell test at nominal $\alpha$ therefore has false-positive rate
  $2\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt{\mathrm{DE}})$, which **rises with the number of cells**: 29.1% at
  50 cells per sample, 70.0% at 500 and 90.2% at 5,000, for $\rho = 0.05$.
- Effective sample size saturates: $nm/\mathrm{DE} \to n/\rho$. Eight donors at 5,000 cells
  each are worth 159.39 independent cells against a ceiling of 160.
- **Cells buy resolution; samples buy power.** The two are not interchangeable, and the design
  effect is how you tell which one a study bought.

<Exercise n={1} title="A test that cannot be rescued by a smaller alpha" difficulty="warm-up">

A study has 200 cells per donor and a gene with intra-donor correlation $\rho = 0.1$.

(a) Compute the design effect and the factor by which test statistics are inflated.

(b) What fraction of true nulls does a nominal 5% per-cell test reject?

(c) The analyst proposes fixing this with Bonferroni correction across 20,000 genes. Say why
that does not work.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\mathrm{DE} = 1 + 199(0.1) = 20.9$, so statistics are inflated by
$\sqrt{20.9} = 4.5717$.

(b) $\mathrm{FPR} = 2\Phi(-1.96/4.5717) = 2\Phi(-0.4287) = 0.6681$, so **66.8%**.

(c) Bonferroni controls the family-wise error rate *given correct p-values*. Here the p-values
themselves are wrong — each one is computed from a standard error that is 4.57 times too small,
so it is not a valid p-value at any threshold. Correcting for multiplicity fixes the number of
tests, not the calibration of each test. Concretely, at a Bonferroni threshold of
$0.05/20{,}000 = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ the per-cell test still rejects far more than that
fraction of nulls, and no threshold restores calibration because the inflation applies to
every quantile at once.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="How many cells before the design effect bites" difficulty="core">

(a) For a general $\rho$, how many cells per sample make the design effect exceed 10?

(b) Evaluate that for $\rho = 0.01$, $0.05$ and $0.1$.

(c) A reviewer says a study is fine because it used "only" 500 cells per donor. Respond.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Solve $1 + (m-1)\rho > 10$, giving $m > 1 + 9/\rho$.

(b) $\rho = 0.01$ needs 901 cells; $\rho = 0.05$ needs 181; $\rho = 0.1$ needs 91.

(c) Five hundred cells per donor is well past all three thresholds. At $\rho = 0.05$ the design
effect at 500 cells is $1 + 499(0.05) = 25.95$, a nearly fivefold inflation of every statistic
and a 70.0% false-positive rate. There is no cell count at which a per-cell test becomes safe —
the quantity that has to be small is $(m-1)\rho$, and $m$ is the thing single-cell experiments
are designed to make large. The number to ask about is the number of donors.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Designing to a target" difficulty="stretch">

(a) Show that $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = nm/(1 + (m-1)\rho)$ approaches $n/\rho$ as $m$ grows, and
interpret the limit.

(b) A study needs 400 effective cells and expects $\rho = 0.05$. What is the minimum number of
donors, regardless of sequencing budget?

(c) With four donors and $\rho = 0.05$, what is the largest effective sample size obtainable at
any cell count? How close does 1,000 cells per donor get?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Divide numerator and denominator by $m$:

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{n}{1/m + (1 - 1/m)\rho} \xrightarrow{\ m \to \infty\ } \frac{n}{\rho}.
$$

Each donor contributes at most $1/\rho$ independent cells however many are sequenced, because
past that point the cells are just re-measuring the same donor-level value. The parallel with a
case-control study's control ceiling is exact: one input saturates and the other does not.

(b) The ceiling is $n/\rho = 20n$, so 400 effective cells need $n \geq 20$ donors. **Twenty
donors**, and no amount of sequencing substitutes for the twentieth.

(c) With $n = 4$ the ceiling is $4/0.05 = 80$. At 1,000 cells per donor,
$\mathrm{DE} = 1 + 999(0.05) = 50.95$ and

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4{,}000}{50.95} = 78.51,
$$

which is 98.1% of the ceiling. The remaining 1.9% is unreachable: sequencing a million cells
per donor would add less than two effective cells. A four-donor study is a four-donor study.

</div>

</Exercise>
