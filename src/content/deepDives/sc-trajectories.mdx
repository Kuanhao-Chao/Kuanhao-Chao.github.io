## The question this lesson answers

A dissociated sample is a snapshot: every cell is destroyed at the moment it is measured, and
nothing is ever observed twice. Yet if a process is asynchronous — cells entering it
continuously and progressing at their own pace — a single snapshot contains cells at every
stage, and ordering them by similarity recovers the sequence
<Citation id="trapnell2014monocle" />
<Citation id="haghverdi2016dpt" />
<Citation id="street2018slingshot" />.

That ordering is the closest this data comes to watching something happen, and the axis it
produces gets read as time. This lesson is about what that axis actually is, and where the time
information really lives.

## The axis is defined only up to its ordering

A pseudotime method assigns each cell a number, but the numbers are not identified. Apply any
strictly increasing function $g$ to every value — square them, take logs, exponentiate — and
every cell keeps its position relative to every other. Nothing downstream that uses only the
ordering can distinguish the two assignments, and nothing in the data prefers one.

So the pseudotime *value* is a convention. Different methods produce different conventions, and
a benchmark comparing them finds their orderings agree far better than their values do
<Citation id="saelens2019trajectory" />. That is not a flaw; it is what the methods claim.

The trouble is what gets read off the axis anyway: that a gene turns on "halfway through", that
one stage is "longer" than another, that a change is "gradual". Each of those is a statement
about the parameterisation, and each survives being made false by reparameterising.

## Equal spans, unequal durations

Make it concrete. A process runs through three stretches: a fast opening, a bottleneck where
progress is ten times slower, and a fast finish. In arc length they are 30%, 20% and 50% of the
trajectory.

In elapsed time they are nothing like that. Time is length over speed, so the bottleneck —
short on the axis — takes far the longest:

$$
\frac{0.2}{0.1} = 2.0
\quad\text{against}\quad
\frac{0.3}{1.0} = 0.3
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{0.5}{1.0} = 0.5.
$$

As shares of the total, that is **10.71%**, **71.43%** and **17.86%**. The bottleneck occupies a
fifth of the axis and nearly three quarters of the duration, a discrepancy of **3.57-fold**;
the fast opening occupies 30% of the axis and 10.7% of the time, a discrepancy of 0.357 the
other way.

## Where the durations actually are

Here is the part worth carrying, and it is a positive result rather than a caveat.

Suppose cells enter the process at a constant rate $\lambda$ and move along it at speed $v(s)$.
At steady state, the flux through every point is the same — $\lambda$ cells per unit time cross
each position — and flux is density times speed. So

$$
\rho(s)\,v(s) = \lambda
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\rho(s) = \frac{\lambda}{v(s)}.
$$

Integrating over a stretch, the number of cells it holds is $\lambda$ times the time taken to
cross it. Normalising away the unknown $\lambda$:

$$
\frac{\text{cells in }[s_1, s_2]}{\text{cells in total}}
= \frac{\text{time to cross }[s_1,s_2]}{\text{total time}}.
$$

**The share of cells in a pseudotime interval is exactly the share of real time spent there.**
Not approximately, and with no unknown constants left over. Where cells pile up, the process is
slow; where they are sparse, it is fast. A bottleneck is short on the axis and crowded, and it
is the crowding that measures it.

The information was never missing. It is in the density along the trajectory — which is
routinely smoothed away, or discarded as an artefact of how many cells happened to be captured
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="What the steady-state assumption is doing">

The result rests on the process being in steady state: cells entering at a roughly constant
rate over a window long compared with the time to traverse the trajectory. That is a real
assumption and it is worth stating whenever the result is used.

It holds well for continuously renewing systems — haematopoiesis, gut epithelium, spermatogenesis
— where production has been running for far longer than a cell takes to mature. It fails for a
synchronised process: an experiment where a stimulus is applied at $t = 0$ and everything moves
together has no cells at the early stages an hour later, so density reflects the wave's
position rather than the local speed.

The tell is whether the sample is a cross-section of an ongoing process or a snapshot of a
cohort moving in step. For the second, the cells cannot tell you the durations, and nothing
else in the snapshot can either — you need the time course.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Reading the durations off the cell counts"
  given={[
    'A trajectory in three stretches: arc lengths 0.3, 0.2, 0.5',
    'Speeds 1.0, 0.1, 1.0 — the middle stretch is a ten-fold bottleneck',
    'Cells entering at a constant rate, sampled at steady state',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — what the axis says.** Arc length shares are 0.3, 0.2, 0.5 — **30%, 20%, 50%**. This
is what a pseudotime plot displays, and it is the only thing most analyses use.

**Step 2 — what the times are.** Length over speed gives 0.3, 2.0 and 0.5 hours, totalling 2.8.
As shares:

$$
\frac{0.3}{2.8} = \frac{3}{28} = 10.71\%,
\qquad
\frac{2.0}{2.8} = \frac{5}{7} = 71.43\%,
\qquad
\frac{0.5}{2.8} = \frac{5}{28} = 17.86\%.
$$

**Step 3 — what the cell counts are.** By $\rho = \lambda/v$, the same three numbers:
**10.71%, 71.43%, 17.86%**. Counting cells in each stretch and dividing by the total recovers
the duration shares with no further modelling.

**Step 4 — the size of the error avoided.** Reading durations off the axis instead would put the
bottleneck at 20% rather than 71.43% — understating it **3.57-fold** — and the opening at 30%
rather than 10.71%, overstating it 2.8-fold. Those errors run in opposite directions, so the
ordering of stage durations comes out wrong as well as their sizes: on the axis the opening
looks longer than the bottleneck, and in reality it is nearly seven times shorter.

**Step 5 — what is still not available.** The absolute durations. $\lambda$ is unknown, so
everything here is a share. Turning 71.43% into hours needs one external measurement — a
labelling experiment, a known cell-cycle length, anything that fixes the scale — and then all
three follow.

</WorkedExample>

## Two inherited problems

A trajectory is built on the neighbour graph of lesson 8 and is usually displayed on the
embedding of lesson 10, so it inherits both of their limits. An arm in a UMAP is not evidence
of a continuum — the embedding preserves neighbourhoods and is free to invent the shape that
joins them <Citation id="mcinnes2018umap" /> — and a trajectory fitted to a graph built in two
thousand noisy dimensions is fitted to a graph that was 41.5% correct. The pseudotime
implementations in the standard toolkits take the graph as given
<Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />.

And testing genes along pseudotime inherits lesson 12 exactly. Cells from one donor sit at
adjacent pseudotimes because they share that donor, so a regression of expression on pseudotime
across pooled cells has the same clustered structure and the same inflated false-positive rate.
The fix is the same one: fit per donor and combine, rather than pooling cells
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />.

## Summary

- Pseudotime is **arc length**, identified only up to any strictly increasing
  reparameterisation, so only the ordering is data. Claims about *where* on the axis something
  happens are claims about a convention.
- Equal spans are not equal durations. A ten-fold bottleneck occupying **20%** of the axis is
  **71.43%** of the elapsed time; the fast opening at 30% of the axis is **10.71%** of the time.
- At steady state the flux is constant, so $\rho(s) = \lambda/v(s)$, and therefore **the share
  of cells in a pseudotime interval is exactly the share of real time spent there**.
- Cell density along a trajectory is a duration measurement, not a sampling artefact. Smoothing
  it away discards the only timing information the snapshot contains.
- **The steady-state assumption is load-bearing.** It holds for continuously renewing systems
  and fails for a synchronised cohort, where the density reflects the wave's position instead.
- Absolute durations still need one external measurement to fix $\lambda$; without it every
  statement is a share.

<Exercise n={1} title="Two stretches, one bottleneck" difficulty="warm-up">

A trajectory has two equal-length halves. Cells cross the first at speed 1 and the second at
speed 0.25.

(a) What share of the pseudotime axis does each occupy?

(b) What share of the elapsed time?

(c) If 4,000 cells are on the trajectory, how many are in each half?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Equal arc lengths, so **50% each**. The axis cannot see the difference.

(b) Times are $0.5/1 = 0.5$ and $0.5/0.25 = 2.0$, totalling 2.5, so **20%** and **80%**.

(c) Cell shares equal time shares, so **800** and **3,200** — a four-fold pile-up in the slow
half, which is exactly the speed ratio. Counting them recovers the 20/80 split with no
modelling at all.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Inverting the counts" difficulty="core">

A trajectory is divided into four equal-length stretches, and the cells in them are counted:
1,200, 400, 2,000 and 400.

(a) What are the relative durations of the four stretches?

(b) What are the relative speeds?

(c) An author writes that the third stretch is "a brief transitional state, occupying a quarter
of the trajectory". Assess.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Duration shares are cell shares: total 4,000, so **30%, 10%, 50%, 10%**.

(b) Equal arc lengths mean speed is inversely proportional to duration, so relative speeds are
$1/30 : 1/10 : 1/50 : 1/10$, or normalising to the fastest, **1 : 3 : 0.6 : 3**. The second and
fourth stretches are crossed three times faster than the first and five times faster than the
third.

(c) It is wrong twice over. "A quarter of the trajectory" is true of the axis and irrelevant —
all four stretches are a quarter by construction. And "brief" is exactly backwards: the third
stretch holds half of all the cells, so it takes half of the total time and is the *longest* of
the four. The author has read a duration off the axis, and the crowding that contradicts it is
visible in their own data.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="When the cells cannot tell you" difficulty="stretch">

(a) A stimulus is applied to a synchronised culture at $t = 0$ and cells are collected six
hours later. Why does the density argument fail?

(b) What does the density measure in that experiment?

(c) Give a design that restores the argument, and one that answers the question directly.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Because the flux is not constant in time. The derivation sets $\rho v = \lambda$ with
$\lambda$ the steady entry rate, and a synchronised culture has $\lambda = 0$ after the single
pulse at $t = 0$. There are no cells at the early stages six hours later, not because those
stages are fast but because nothing is entering behind the wave.

(b) The distribution of the cohort at six hours — that is, the spread accumulated by cells
moving at slightly different rates. It is informative about *variability* between cells, which
is a different and also interesting quantity, but it says nothing about the local speed.

(c) To restore the argument: stimulate continuously rather than as a pulse, so entry becomes
steady and the population reaches a stationary distribution. To answer directly: collect at
several times and follow the wave, which measures the durations without needing the steady-state
assumption at all — and lets you check the assumption for any future cross-sectional study of
the same system. Note the second design also fixes the missing $\lambda$, so it converts every
share in this lesson into hours.

</div>

</Exercise>
