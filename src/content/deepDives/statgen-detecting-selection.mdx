## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson gave a number for how far apart two populations have drifted. This one
asks a different question of the same data: not how far apart they are, but whether anything
*pushed* them.

The difficulty is that selection leaves no direct trace. Nobody observes a selection
coefficient; what is observed is a pattern of variation, and drift produces patterns too.
Every test in this lesson works the same way — it finds a quantity that neutrality pins down,
and asks whether the data respect the pin.

The quantity is **age**. Under neutrality an allele's frequency and its age are not
independent: drift is slow, so a variant that has reached high frequency has had to spend a
long time getting there. A variant that is common *and* young is the signature, and every
method below is a way of noticing one.

## Frequency pins age

<Citation id="kimura1973age" /> solved the diffusion for the age of a neutral allele
conditional on its present frequency:

$$
\mathbb{E}[t \mid p] = -4N\,\frac{p}{1-p}\,\ln p
$$

The two limits are the sanity check. As $p \to 1$ the expression tends to $4N$, the coalescent
depth of the whole population — an allele on its way to fixation is as old as the genealogy
gets. As $p \to 0$ it tends to zero, because a rare allele is a young one. In between it rises
smoothly, and at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ it takes the clean value

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[t \mid p = \tfrac{1}{2}\right] = 4N\ln 2
$$

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why drift is so slow that age becomes a measurement">

Drift moves an allele's frequency by about $\sqrt{p(1-p)/2N}$ per generation. At $N = 10{,}000$
and $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ that is a step of $\sqrt{0.25/20{,}000} = 0.003536$ in frequency — about
a third of one percent — per generation, a random walk with a very short stride. Covering half
the frequency range by accumulating steps that small takes
tens of thousands of generations, and the exact answer is $4N\ln 2 = 27{,}725.89$ of them,
about 693,000 years at 25 years per generation.

That slowness is what makes the test possible. If drift were fast, every frequency would be
compatible with every age and there would be nothing to check.

</Callout>

The simulation behind those points is worth stating, because the closed form comes from a
diffusion approximation and it is reasonable to want it checked. A Wright–Fisher population of
2N = 1,000 chromosomes was run for 60,000 generations with recurrent mutation at
$\theta = 4N\mu = 10$, every mutation's birth generation recorded, and the mean age taken over
all segregating alleles falling within ±0.01 of each target frequency. Sampling that way — from
a stationary population rather than at an allele's first arrival — matters: taking the first
time an allele *reaches* a frequency selects the youngest ones and biases the answer downward
by more than a factor of two.

<WorkedExample
  title="How old is a variant at half frequency?"
  given={[
    { label: 'Effective size N', value: '10,000' },
    { label: 'Derived frequency p', value: '0.50' },
    { label: 'Generation time', value: '25 years' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**The age.** With $p/(1-p) = 1$ and $\ln \tfrac{1}{2} = -\ln 2$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[t] = -4 \times 10^4 \times 1 \times (-\ln 2) = 4N\ln 2 = 27{,}725.89 \text{ generations}
$$

which is about 693,000 years — older than the split between modern humans and Neanderthals.
An allele at half frequency is, under neutrality, expected to predate the species carrying it
in anything like its present form.

**The same calculation at other frequencies.** At $p = 0.10$ it is 10,233.71 generations; at
$p = 0.90$, 37,929.79. The curve is steep at the bottom and flat at the top, which is worth
noting: **most of the information about age is in low-frequency variants**, because that is
where age changes fastest with frequency.

**What a sweep looks like.** A variant driven to $p = 0.50$ by selection in 500 generations
has an age anomaly of $27{,}725.89 / 500 = 55.45$. Nothing about the frequency is unusual;
everything about the pairing of that frequency with that age is.

</WorkedExample>

## From age to haplotype, which is what you can actually see

Nobody observes an allele's age directly. What is observable is the haplotype it sits on, and
the link is recombination.

Take two chromosomes carrying the allele, whose lineages coalesced $t$ generations ago. Each
lineage has had $t$ generations in which recombination could break the ancestral haplotype, so
$2t$ in total. Over a genetic distance $d$ the chance that neither lineage recombined is
$e^{-2dt}$ — which is exactly extended haplotype homozygosity <Citation id="sabeti2002ehh" />:

$$
\mathrm{EHH}(d) = e^{-2dt}, \qquad d_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{2t}
$$

Length and depth are inversely proportional. A young genealogy carries a long haplotype
because there has been little time to break it, which is the whole hitchhiking argument of
<Citation id="smith1974hitchhiking" />: a favoured allele drags its neighbourhood to high
frequency faster than recombination can separate them.

<Callout variant="key" title="At half frequency the bound is exactly 1/(8N) Morgans">

Substitute the neutral age at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ into the half-length and the $\ln 2$ cancels:

$$
d_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{2 \times 4N\ln 2} = \frac{1}{8N}
$$

At $N = 10{,}000$ that is $1.25\times10^{-5}$ Morgans, or 1.25 kb where the map runs at
1 cM/Mb. The mutation rate does not appear, the logarithm does not appear, and neither does
the recombination rate except through the units.

This is a **floor**, not an estimate. Two copies of an allele must coalesce at or before the
allele arose, so they have had *at most* the allele's full age in which to recombine, and the
real neutral haplotype is longer than 1.25 kb.

</Callout>

## The admission that explains the method

Everything above compared a sweep against a *floor*. The obvious next step is to compare it
against the neutral expectation itself — and that is where the derivation stops working.

Two copies of a neutral allele at frequency $p$ sit in a class of about $2Np$ chromosomes, so
a first guess is that they coalesce in about $2Np$ generations. But the class was not always
that size: going backwards the allele was rarer, the class was smaller, and coalescence was
correspondingly faster. The answer depends on the whole trajectory that brought the allele to
$p$, and there is no tidy closed form for it.

A Wright–Fisher simulation with unbiased sampling of segregating alleles puts the mean
pairwise coalescence time at **0.786, 0.681 and 0.598 of $2Np$** at frequencies of 0.2, 0.5
and 0.8 — a fraction that falls as the allele gets commoner, not a constant. And that is at
one constant population size; real demography moves it further.

<Callout variant="warning" title="This is why iHS is a within-locus contrast">

There is no absolute haplotype length that means "selected". The expectation depends on the
local recombination rate, on the population's size history, and on the allele's own trajectory
— none of which is known well enough to calibrate a threshold.

<Citation id="voight2006ihs" /> solves this by not needing one. iHS compares the integrated
haplotype homozygosity of the **derived** allele against the **ancestral** allele *at the same
locus*. The local recombination rate is shared by both and divides out; so does most of the
demographic history, because both alleles experienced it. What survives is the asymmetry, and
an asymmetry is exactly what a sweep produces and drift does not.

<Citation id="sabeti2007xpehh" /> applies the same trick across populations instead of across
alleles, which extends the reach to sweeps that have gone to fixation locally — where there is
no ancestral allele left to compare against.

**A derivation that will not close is not always a failure.** Here it is the reason the method
has the shape it has, and a reader who has felt the derivation fail understands the design in
a way that reading the method's definition would not supply.

</Callout>

## Three signatures, three timescales

The mismatch between frequency and age can be read in three places, and they are sensitive at
different depths.

| Signature | What it reads | Sensitive window | Owned by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frequency spectrum, Tajima's D | Excess of rare variants after a sweep purges diversity | Roughly $0.1N$ to $N$ generations | Lesson 3 |
| Haplotype length, EHH and iHS | Genealogy too shallow for the frequency | Under $\sim 0.1N$ generations | This lesson |
| Between-population divergence, F_ST outliers | One population pushed and the other not | Since the populations split | Lesson 5 |

The ordering is not arbitrary. Haplotypes are the most recent signal because recombination
destroys them fastest: at $N = 10{,}000$ a sweep older than about 1,000 generations has
already had its haplotype broken down to a few tens of kilobases. The frequency spectrum
recovers more slowly and so remembers longer — that is the contrast
<Citation id="tajima1989" /> turned into a test, derived in lesson 3. F_ST outliers can in
principle reach back to the population split, but only for selection that acted on one side
of it.

The best-attested human example sits squarely in the haplotype window.
<Citation id="bersaglieri2004lactase" /> found the lactase-persistence allele at around 0.77
in northern Europe on a haplotype far longer than that frequency permits. Neutrality expects
an allele at 0.77 to be roughly 35,000 generations old; the sweep is dated to a few hundred.

## What all three miss

Every method above detects a *sweep* — one locus, one allele, a large enough selection
coefficient to move it against drift. Most selection is not like that.

If a trait is polygenic and the environment shifts, the response is spread over thousands of
loci and each allele moves by a fraction of a percent. No single locus becomes young for its
frequency by any detectable margin, no haplotype is unusually long, and no F_ST is an outlier.
The genome-wide response can be substantial while every individual signature is invisible
<Citation id="pritchard2010polygenic" />.

Detecting that requires aggregating across loci that a GWAS has already identified — asking
whether trait-increasing alleles have systematically shifted in frequency, rather than whether
any one of them has. <Citation id="field2016sds" /> does this with a statistic built from the
singleton density around each variant, which is a haplotype-age measure by another route:
recent frequency change leaves a local deficit of singletons.

That approach inherits every weakness of the GWAS it starts from, and in particular the
ancestry stratification of the previous lesson — which has produced at least one prominent
retraction in this literature. A polygenic selection signal and uncorrected population
structure produce the same pattern, and telling them apart needs the machinery of lesson 5.

<Exercise n={1} title="Age from frequency" difficulty="warm-up">

At an effective size of 10,000, how old is a neutral allele at frequency 0.20 expected to be,
in generations and in years at 25 years per generation? Compare it with the value at 0.50, and
say what the comparison implies about which variants carry the most information.

<div slot="solution">

$\mathbb{E}[t] = -4 \times 10^4 \times (0.2/0.8) \times \ln 0.2 = -40{,}000 \times 0.25 \times (-1.609438) = 16{,}094.38$
generations, or about 402,000 years.

At $p = 0.50$ the value is 27,725.89 generations. So raising the frequency by a factor of 2.5,
from 0.20 to 0.50, raises the expected age by a factor of only 1.72 — while halving the
frequency from 0.20 to 0.10 cuts the age to 10,233.71, a factor of 0.636.

The curve is steep at low frequency and flat at high frequency, so **frequency pins age most
tightly where the allele is rare** — a small change in frequency implies a large change in
expected age there, and almost none at the top. That cuts both ways for detection: at high
frequency the expected age barely moves, so an anomalously young common allele stands out
against a well-determined expectation, which is exactly the regime sweeps are found in and
where the allele is common enough to carry a haplotype worth measuring.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Two readings of one anomaly" difficulty="core">

A variant sits at frequency 0.75 in a population of effective size 10,000, and independent
dating puts its age at 1,200 generations. (a) What is the age anomaly? (b) What haplotype
half-length does the observed age imply, and what does the neutral floor allow? (c) Verify
that the two anomalies agree.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Neutral expectation is $-40{,}000 \times (0.75/0.25) \times \ln 0.75 = -40{,}000 \times 3 \times (-0.287682) = 34{,}521.85$
generations. The anomaly is $34{,}521.85/1200 = 28.77$.

**(b)** Observed: $d_{1/2} = \ln 2 / 2400 = 2.888\times10^{-4}$ Morgans, or 28.88 kb at
1 cM/Mb. Floor: $\ln 2 / (2 \times 34{,}521.85) = 1.004\times10^{-5}$ Morgans, 1.004 kb.

**(c)** $28.88/1.004 = 28.77$, the same as the age anomaly, because both are
$\mathbb{E}[t]/t_{\text{obs}}$ — the $\ln 2$ and the factor of 2 cancel in the ratio. Note
this is the anomaly measured against the *floor*, so it is an upper bound on the length excess
you would actually observe, not a prediction of it.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Why a fixed threshold cannot work" difficulty="core">

A colleague proposes flagging any variant whose haplotype extends past 50 kb at half
homozygosity as a sweep candidate. (a) What genealogy depth does 50 kb correspond to at
1 cM/Mb? (b) Give two reasons the threshold will misfire, one demographic and one genomic.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $d = 50 \times 10^{-5} = 5\times10^{-4}$ Morgans, so
$t = \ln 2 / (2 \times 5\times10^{-4}) = 693.15$ generations.

**(b)** *Demographic.* The neutral expectation scales with $N$: the floor is $1/(8N)$ Morgans
at half frequency, so it is 2.5 kb at $N = 5{,}000$ and 0.625 kb at $N = 20{,}000$ — a
four-fold range across plausible effective sizes, before any bottleneck is considered. A
bottleneck shortens coalescence times everywhere and lengthens every haplotype in the genome,
so the same threshold flags a different fraction of the genome in each population.

*Genomic.* The map is not uniform. A variant in a recombination cold spot carries a long
haplotype for entirely neutral reasons, and one inside a hotspot cannot carry a long haplotype
however hard it was selected. Since the threshold is stated in physical distance, it is really
a statement about the local map, not about selection.

Both failures are removed by the same move: compare within a locus, where the map and the
demography are shared, rather than against an absolute number.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="The sweep you cannot see" difficulty="stretch">

A trait under stabilising selection shifts its optimum. Two hundred loci each move by 0.4% in
frequency in response, from a starting frequency of 0.50. (a) Estimate the age anomaly at a
single one of those loci. (b) What does this say about the relationship between the strength
of selection on a *trait* and the detectability of selection at a *locus*?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Essentially none. A 0.4% frequency change is well within what drift produces at
$N = 10{,}000$ over a few hundred generations: the per-generation drift step at $p = 0.5$ is
about $\sqrt{0.25/20{,}000} = 0.0035$, so 0.004 is roughly one generation's worth of noise.
The allele's genealogy is unchanged to any measurable degree, its age is unchanged, and its
haplotype is exactly as long as neutrality predicts. The age anomaly is indistinguishable
from 1.

**(b)** The two are almost unrelated. Detectability at a locus depends on the *per-locus*
selection coefficient, which for a polygenic trait is the total response divided over many
loci. A trait can be under strong selection — a large, fast, biologically important shift in
the mean — while no individual locus experiences enough of it to leave any of the three
signatures.

This is why the polygenic case needs a different instrument entirely: not a scan for outlier

## Summary

- **Neutrality ties frequency to age**: $\mathbb{E}[t \mid p] = -4N\,p/(1-p)\,\ln p$, tending
  to $4N$ at fixation and to zero at the bottom. Simulation reproduces it to within 3–4%
  across the frequency range, a flat offset rather than a trend.
- **At half frequency the age is exactly $4N\ln 2$** — 27,725.89 generations at
  $N = 10{,}000$, about 693,000 years.
- **Haplotype homozygosity decays as $e^{-2dt}$**, so length is the reciprocal of genealogy
  depth and the age anomaly and the length anomaly are the same number.
- **The neutral bound at half frequency is exactly $1/(8N)$ Morgans**, 1.25 kb at
  $N = 10{,}000$ — with the $\ln 2$ cancelling. A variant swept there in 500 generations
  carries 69.31 kb, 55.45 times more.
- **There is no absolute length threshold**, because the neutral expectation depends on the
  trajectory, the demography and the local map. Simulation puts the within-class coalescence
  at 0.786, 0.681 and 0.598 of $2Np$ at frequencies 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 — no constant to
  calibrate against. That is why iHS contrasts derived against ancestral at one locus.
- **Three signatures, three windows**: haplotypes for the most recent sweeps, the frequency
  spectrum for older ones, F_ST outliers for selection that acted on one population.
- **All three miss polygenic adaptation**, where the response is spread thin enough that no
  locus is individually anomalous — and the aggregate tests that catch it inherit the ancestry
  confounding of lesson 5.
