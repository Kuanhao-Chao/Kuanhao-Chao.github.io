## The question this lesson answers

Module 4 has so far assumed one study. Almost nothing in this field is one study.

The sample sizes the power calculations demand — hundreds of thousands for effects
explaining a hundredth of a percent — are beyond any single cohort, so results are pooled
across consortia whose members recruited differently, genotyped on different arrays, and
measured the trait by different protocols. <Citation id="evangelou2013meta" /> surveys the
resulting methodology; <Citation id="willer2010metal" /> is the tool almost everyone uses.

Two questions follow, and they are the lesson:

1. **How should studies be combined?** There is a right answer, and the common alternative
   is a proxy for it that fails in a specific, predictable way.
2. **What does the pooled estimate mean if the studies disagree?** Disagreement is measured
   by Q and I², and I² in particular is routinely misread.

Then a third question, about what happens *after* discovery: an effect size that earned its
way past a threshold is, on average, too large.

## Inverse-variance weighting, and its proxy

Pool $k$ estimates by a weighted average. The variance of that average is minimised — over
all possible choices of weights — by taking

$$
w_i = \frac{1}{\mathrm{SE}_i^2},
\qquad
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \hat\beta_i}{\sum_i w_i},
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{IVW}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i}}
$$

This is not one option among several. It is *the* optimal linear combination, and every
other weighting is a compromise made for a reason.

The usual reason is that the studies do not report effects on a common scale — different
transformations of the trait, different units, or a mix of quantitative and binary outcomes.
Then <Citation id="willer2010metal" />'s sample-size scheme pools $z$-scores with weights
$\sqrt{N_i}$, which produces a $p$-value but **no effect size at all**, because nothing about
the units of $\beta$ survives the transformation.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Sample size is a proxy for precision, and proxies come apart">

Why does $\sqrt{N}$ work at all? Because $\mathrm{SE} \approx \sigma/\sqrt{N q^2 \cdot N}$ —
loosely, precision grows with the square root of the sample — so weighting by $\sqrt{N}$ is
weighting by something monotone in precision.

The approximation fails whenever precision depends on anything besides sample size, and in
genetics it usually does:

- **Allele frequency.** A variant at 1% in one cohort and 8% in another has very different
  standard errors at the same $N$, because $q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$ carries the frequency.
- **Case-control ratio.** 5,000 cases among 100,000 people carries far less information than
  50,000 cases among the same 100,000, and $\sqrt{N}$ cannot see the difference.
- **Imputation quality.** A poorly imputed variant has an inflated standard error at
  unchanged $N$.

Each of these makes a study's *reported precision* diverge from what its headcount suggests.
Inverse-variance weighting uses the precision the study actually achieved; sample-size
weighting uses the precision its size would predict. When they disagree, the second one is
wrong.

</Callout>

**Step 1 — the inverse-variance weights.** $w_i = 1/\mathrm{SE}_i^2$:

$$
(2500.0,\; 4444.4,\; 625.0,\; 3086.4)
$$

**Step 2 — the pooled estimate.**

$$
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}} = 0.079870,
\qquad
\mathrm{SE} = 0.009687,
\qquad
z = 8.2447
$$

**Step 3 — now weight by $\sqrt{N}$ instead.** The weights become
$(141.4,\; 173.2,\; 158.1,\; 148.3)$, and as shares of the total:

| | IVW share | √N share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| study 1 | 23.5% | 22.8% |
| study 2 | 41.7% | 27.9% |
| study 3 | **5.9%** | **25.5%** |
| study 4 | 29.0% | 23.9% |

**Step 4 — look at study 3.** It has 25,000 people, the second-largest sample, and the
*largest* effect estimate in the set at 0.14. Inverse-variance weighting gives it 5.9%.
Sample-size weighting gives it 25.5% — more than four times as much.

Study 3's standard error is 0.040, double anyone else's. Whatever the reason — a rarer
variant in that cohort, worse imputation, a more unbalanced design — the study is telling
you directly that its estimate is imprecise, and $\sqrt{N}$ weighting ignores that in favour
of a headcount.

**Step 5 — the consequence.** Sample-size weighting pulls the pooled estimate toward the
least reliable study, precisely because that study's unreliability is invisible to it. Here
it happens to be the study with the most extreme effect, which is not a coincidence: noisy
estimates are extreme more often.

</WorkedExample>

## Heterogeneity: Q and I²

If the studies are estimating the same thing, their spread should be no more than sampling
error accounts for. Cochran's Q tests that:

$$
Q = \sum_i w_i \left(\hat\beta_i - \hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}}\right)^2
$$

which is $\chi^2$ on $k-1$ degrees of freedom under homogeneity — one degree of freedom
spent on estimating the pooled value. For the four studies above, $Q = 5.0276$ on 3 df, a
$p$-value of $0.0249$.

<Citation id="higgins2002heterogeneity" /> and <Citation id="higgins2003inconsistency" />
introduced $I^2$ to put that on an interpretable scale:

$$
I^2 = \max\left(0,\; \frac{Q - (k-1)}{Q}\right) \times 100\%
$$

Here $I^2 = (5.0276 - 3)/5.0276 = 40.3\%$.

<Callout variant="warning" title="I² is not a measure of how much studies disagree">

This is the most consistently misread statistic in meta-analysis, and the misreading is
easy to state.

$I^2$ is the **share of total variability that is not sampling error**. It is a ratio, and
the denominator shrinks as studies get more precise. So:

> **Take the same studies, disagreeing by exactly the same absolute amount, and quadruple
> everyone's sample size. The between-study differences have not changed. $I^2$ goes up.**

A large $I^2$ in a well-powered consortium may reflect genuinely trivial disagreement that
has simply become visible. A small $I^2$ in four tiny studies may hide disagreement that is
enormous but drowned in noise.

Two consequences. Report $\tau^2$ — the between-study variance, in the units of the effect —
alongside $I^2$, because $\tau^2$ is an absolute measure and $I^2$ is not. And do not apply
the conventional 25/50/75% "low/moderate/high" bands across studies of very different size,
because they are not comparable.

For the worked example $\tau^2 = 0.000278$, so the between-study standard deviation is
$0.0167$ — about a fifth of the pooled effect of 0.0799. That is a statement one can act on
in a way that "40.3%" is not.

</Callout>

### Fixed effect or random effects

The two models answer different questions.

**Fixed effect** assumes every study estimates the same underlying $\beta$ and that all
spread is sampling error. Its confidence interval answers: *what is that common effect?*

**Random effects** — <Citation id="dersimonian1986random" /> is the standard estimator —
assumes each study has its own $\beta_i$ drawn from a distribution with variance $\tau^2$,
and widens the interval accordingly. It answers: *what is the mean of the distribution of
study-specific effects?*

In genetics the fixed-effect model is used far more than the logic strictly justifies, and
the reason is defensible: for a genuinely causal variant with a biological mechanism, the
per-allele effect really should be close to constant across cohorts, and observed
heterogeneity is more often a sign of a problem — different phenotype definitions,
population structure, imputation differences — than of genuinely varying biology. Random
effects would absorb that problem into a wider interval rather than flagging it.

## The winner's curse

A variant enters the literature because some study's estimate crossed a threshold.
Conditioning on that fact selects, among all studies of that variant, the ones whose
sampling error happened to point away from zero.

<Citation id="zollner2007winners" /> set this out for genetic association. The expected
reported effect, given discovery, is

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat z \;\middle|\; |\hat z| > c\right]
= z + \frac{\varphi(z - c) - \varphi(z + c)}{\Phi(z - c) + \Phi(-z - c)}
$$

which exceeds $z$ always, and by an amount that depends sharply on how close $z$ is to $c$.

<WorkedExample
  title="How much too large is a discovery estimate?"
  given={[
    { label: 'Threshold', value: '|z| > 5.4513 (genome-wide)' },
    { label: 'Question', value: 'expected reported z, given discovery' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — a variant whose true z is 5.0.** It sits just *below* the threshold, so it is
discovered only when noise pushes it up:

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat z \mid \text{discovered}\right] = 6.105655
$$

an overestimate of **22.1%**.

**Step 2 — a variant whose true z is 6.0.** Comfortably past the threshold, so most studies
of it are significant and the selection is mild:

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat z \mid \text{discovered}\right] = 6.484465
\qquad\text{— an 8.1\% overestimate}
$$

**Step 3 — a variant whose true z is 9.0.** Essentially always discovered, so conditioning
on discovery selects nothing:

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat z \mid \text{discovered}\right] = 9.000735
\qquad\text{— 0.0\%}
$$

**Step 4 — see where the damage is.** The curse is negligible for large effects and severe
near the threshold. And **most discoveries are near the threshold**, because the distribution
of true effects rises steeply as effects get smaller — there are far more variants just
strong enough to be found than variants far past the line.

So the typical newly discovered variant is meaningfully overestimated, and the effect
reported in the discovery paper is the wrong number to carry forward.

**Step 5 — what this means for replication.** A replication study estimating the same variant
without conditioning on significance gets an unbiased estimate, which is *smaller*. That is
the expected outcome of a successful replication, not evidence against the finding.

</WorkedExample>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Pooling by hand" difficulty="warm-up">

Three studies report β = 0.20 (SE 0.05), β = 0.12 (SE 0.04), and β = 0.16 (SE 0.10).
(a) Compute the inverse-variance weights and the pooled estimate. (b) Compute Q and I².
(c) Is the heterogeneity concerning?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $w_i = 1/\mathrm{SE}_i^2 = (400,\; 625,\; 100)$, summing to 1,125.

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{400(0.20) + 625(0.12) + 100(0.16)}{1125} = \frac{171}{1125} = 0.152000
$$

$$
\mathrm{SE} = 1/\sqrt{1125} = 0.029814, \qquad z = 5.0983
$$

**(b)** $Q = 400(0.048)^2 + 625(-0.032)^2 + 100(0.008)^2 = 0.9216 + 0.64 + 0.0064 = 1.568000$
on 2 df. Since $Q < k-1 = 2$, $I^2$ is truncated at $0\%$ and $\tau^2 = 0$.

**(c)** No. $Q$ is *below* its degrees of freedom, meaning the studies agree slightly more
closely than sampling error alone would predict — which is what homogeneous studies look
like. The three estimates span 0.12 to 0.20, which sounds like disagreement until you notice
the standard errors are 0.04 to 0.10; that spread is entirely ordinary noise.

This is the case $I^2$ handles correctly, and it is worth seeing alongside the warning
above: the statistic is not useless, it is just a ratio whose denominator moves.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="The same disagreement, four times the data" difficulty="core">

Take two studies with $\hat\beta_1 = 0.10$ and $\hat\beta_2 = 0.14$, both with SE 0.030.
(a) Compute Q and I². (b) Now quadruple both sample sizes, which halves both standard
errors to 0.015, and suppose the point estimates are unchanged. Recompute. (c) What
changed, and what did not?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Weights are $1/0.03^2 = 1111.1$ each; the pooled estimate is 0.12, and

$$
Q = 1111.1(0.02)^2 + 1111.1(-0.02)^2 = 0.888889 \text{ on 1 df}
$$

$Q < 1$, so $I^2 = 0\%$.

**(b)** Weights become $1/0.015^2 = 4444.4$, the pooled estimate is still 0.12, and

$$
Q = 4444.4(0.02)^2 \times 2 = 3.555556 \text{ on 1 df}
$$

$$
I^2 = \frac{3.5556 - 1}{3.5556} = 71.9\%
$$

**(c)** **What did not change:** the studies' estimates, their difference of 0.04, and the
pooled value of 0.12. In every absolute sense the disagreement is identical.

**What changed:** $I^2$ went from 0% to 71.9% — from "no heterogeneity" to "high
heterogeneity" on the conventional bands — purely because the studies got more precise.
$Q$ scales with the weights, and $I^2$ is a function of $Q$.

This is the warning made concrete. Reporting $I^2$ without $\tau^2$ or the raw spread invites
exactly the wrong conclusion, and in a field where sample sizes grow by an order of magnitude
per decade, the same finding will acquire a worse-looking $I^2$ over time while being
unchanged.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A replication that looks like a failure" difficulty="stretch">

A discovery study reports a variant at $z = 6.2$. An independent replication of similar size
estimates the same variant at $z = 4.9$, which does not reach genome-wide significance. A
reviewer calls the finding unreplicated. (a) Is that reading right? (b) What would you
compute? (c) What would genuinely indicate a false positive?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** No, and the reviewer has made two errors. First, a replication does not need to clear
$5\times10^{-8}$ — that threshold corrects for a genome-wide search, and a replication tests
*one* pre-specified variant, so the appropriate threshold is roughly 0.05. At $z = 4.9$ the
replication $p$-value is around $10^{-6}$, which is overwhelming for a single test.

Second, the replication estimate being smaller is the *expected* outcome. The discovery
estimate is conditioned on having crossed the threshold; the replication is not.

**(b)** The winner's-curse-corrected expectation. If the true $z$ were around 5.3, a
discovery estimate of 6.2 and an unbiased replication near 4.9 are both entirely consistent —
the discovery was inflated by roughly the amount the curse predicts at that effect size, and
the replication is estimating the truth. Comparing the replication against the *corrected*
discovery estimate rather than the raw one is the right test.

**(c)** Three things would. A replication estimate whose confidence interval **excludes** the
corrected discovery estimate, rather than merely being smaller. An effect in the **opposite
direction**, which the curse cannot produce. Or heterogeneity concentrated by ancestry or by
cohort in a way that tracks a known technical difference — different arrays, different
imputation panels, different phenotype definitions — which points at an artefact rather than
at a variable biological effect. Heterogeneity that lines up with ancestry is worth
separating from the rest, because it can equally mean the discovery cohort was
inadequately corrected for structure in the sense of <Citation id="price2006eigenstrat" />
rather than that the effect genuinely differs between populations.

None of those is "the replication p-value did not clear a genome-wide threshold it was never
required to clear."

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Inverse-variance weighting is the unique optimal pooling.** $w_i = 1/\mathrm{SE}_i^2$
  minimises the variance of the pooled estimate; everything else is a compromise.
- **Sample-size weighting is a proxy that fails predictably** — when allele frequency,
  case-control ratio or imputation quality make a study's achieved precision differ from what
  its headcount predicts. In the worked example study 3 gets 5.9% of the weight by precision
  and 25.5% by headcount, and it has both the largest effect estimate and the worst standard
  error.
- **It also produces no effect size**, only a $p$-value, which is why it is reserved for
  studies whose scales cannot be pooled.
- **$Q = \sum_i w_i(\hat\beta_i - \hat\beta)^2$ is χ² on $k-1$ df**; for the worked example
  5.0276 on 3 df, $p = 0.0249$.
- **$I^2$ is a ratio, not an amount.** The same studies disagreeing by the same absolute
  amount give $I^2 = 0\%$ at SE 0.030 and 71.9% at SE 0.015. Report $\tau^2$ alongside it.
- **The winner's curse inflates discovery estimates**, by 22.1% at a true $z$ of 5.0, 8.1% at
  6.0 and essentially nothing at 9.0 — worst near the threshold, which is where most
  discoveries are. A smaller replication estimate is the expected result of a successful
  replication, and discovery effect sizes must never weight a score built from the same data.
