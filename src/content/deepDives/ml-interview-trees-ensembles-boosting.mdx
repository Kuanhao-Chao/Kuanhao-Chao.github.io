## Three ideas, not one family name

Trees, forests, and boosted trees share a base learner, but solve different statistical
problems. A single tree creates interpretable rectangular regions. Bagging averages noisy
versions to suppress variance. Boosting constructs an additive function, asking each new
tree to repair the current model.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Instability becomes useful when errors disagree">
A deep tree is a brittle expert: change a few observations and its first split may change,
rewriting everything below it. Averaging many identical brittle experts would not help.
Bootstrap samples and random feature subsets make their mistakes disagree; averaging then
cancels the disagreements while preserving shared signal.
</Callout>

## How one tree is grown

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-split-criterion">
At a node containing set $S$, enumerate candidate feature/threshold splits into children
$S_L,S_R$. Choose the candidate with greatest impurity decrease

$$
\Delta I=I(S)-\frac{|S_L|}{|S|}I(S_L)-\frac{|S_R|}{|S|}I(S_R).
$$

For regression, $I$ is commonly mean squared deviation from the node mean. For
classification it is Gini or entropy. With a sorted continuous feature, candidate
thresholds only need to fall between distinct adjacent values; efficient implementations
scan sufficient statistics rather than copying datasets. Quinlan's induction work made
information-gain tree construction systematic <Citation id="quinlan1986trees" />.

The search is greedy: the best immediate split is accepted without exploring every future
subtree. Finding a globally optimal tree under common objectives is combinatorial, so the
heuristic is the practical bargain. **Trap:** a large raw gain on a tiny child may simply
isolate noise; leaf-size and complexity controls belong in the answer.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-gini-entropy">
For node proportions $p_k$,

$$I_G=1-\sum_kp_k^2,\qquad I_H=-\sum_kp_k\log p_k.$$

Gini is the probability of mislabeling when labels are sampled from the node distribution;
entropy is expected information content. Both are zero at purity, concave, and sensitive
enough to rank incremental improvements. Entropy rises more sharply near a rare class;
Gini is marginally cheaper and often chooses the same split.

Classification error $1-\max_kp_k$ is flatter. In a binary node it remains unchanged as
$p$ moves from .51 to .99, even though purity changes dramatically, so it is poor for
growing. It can still summarize a finished node or participate in pruning. Compare the
**weighted child decrease**, not just child impurity.
</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="Score a binary split with Gini impurity"
  given={[
    { label: 'Parent', value: '10 positive, 10 negative' },
    { label: 'Left child', value: '8 positive, 2 negative' },
    { label: 'Right child', value: '2 positive, 8 negative' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>
The parent has $p=(.5,.5)$, so $I_G(parent)=1-.5^2-.5^2=.5$. Each child has proportions
$(.8,.2)$ in opposite order and impurity

$$I_G(child)=1-.8^2-.2^2=.32.$$

Both children contain ten of twenty observations, so their weighted impurity is
$.5(.32)+.5(.32)=.32$. The gain is $.5-.32=.18$. A candidate with one perfectly pure
one-observation child and a nearly unchanged 19-observation child may have a smaller
weighted gain despite advertising a “pure leaf.”
</WorkedExample>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-depth-pruning">
An unrestricted tree can keep splitting until leaves are pure or unique, yielding low
training bias and high variance. **Pre-pruning** restricts maximum depth, minimum samples
per split/leaf, maximum leaves, or minimum gain. These controls are computationally cheap
but can block a split whose value appears only two levels later.

**Post-pruning** first grows a large tree, then removes branches. Cost-complexity pruning
chooses a subtree $T$ minimizing
$R(T)+\alpha|T_{leaves}|$: empirical leaf error plus a price per leaf. As $\alpha$ rises,
the optimal subtree becomes smaller. Select $\alpha$ by cross-validation, respecting
groups and time.

**Bias–variance.** Shallower or larger-leaf trees average more observations in each region,
raising approximation bias but reducing sensitivity to samples. Do not tune until test
performance peaks; the test set gets one final use.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-instability">
Tree induction makes discrete, hierarchical choices. If two early split gains are close,
replacing a few rows can reverse them. Every later candidate then sees a different subset,
so two visually unrelated trees may result. This is algorithmic instability—high variance
under perturbing the training sample.

Measure it with bootstrap refits: compare predictions, split frequencies, or feature ranks
across resamples. A stable aggregate prediction can coexist with unstable individual
explanations. That distinction matters when someone presents one tree from an ensemble as
“the model's reasoning.”

This is also the opportunity behind bagging. If perturbed trees remain reasonably accurate
but make different errors, averaging can reduce variance without forcing any one tree to
become stable.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Bagging and random forests

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-bagging">
Bagging draws bootstrap training sets, fits one learner per draw, and averages regression
predictions or votes/probabilities. Breiman proposed it specifically for unstable
procedures <Citation id="breiman1996bagging" />.

If each estimator has variance $\sigma^2$ and every pair has correlation $\rho$, then

$$
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\hat f_b\right)
=\sigma^2\left[\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\right].
$$

$B$ removes only the uncorrelated part. As $B\to\infty$, variance approaches
$\rho\sigma^2$, not zero. This is why both base-learner strength and error correlation
matter. Bagging a stable high-bias learner changes little; averaging deep noisy trees is
the canonical win.
</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="How much variance remains after averaging correlated trees?"
  given={[
    { label: 'Variance of one tree', value: 'σ² = 1' },
    { label: 'Pairwise correlation', value: 'ρ = 0.20' },
    { label: 'Number of trees', value: 'B = 25' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>
Insert the values:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar f)=1\left[.20+\frac{.80}{25}\right]=.232.
$$

The ensemble removes $76.8\%$ of one tree's variance. But even infinitely many trees leave
$.20$: shared error cannot average away. If random feature selection lowers correlation to
$.10$ while raising individual variance to $1.10$, the 25-tree variance becomes
$1.10[.10+.90/25]=.1496$—weaker trees, stronger forest.
</WorkedExample>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-random-forest">
Bootstrap trees can still resemble one another: a dominant predictor wins the root split
in nearly every sample. Random forests additionally expose each split to a random subset
of features <Citation id="breiman2001randomforests" />. This raises the bias or variance of
an individual tree slightly but reduces cross-tree correlation; the ensemble can improve.

The main controls are number of trees, features considered per split, leaf size/depth, and
sample scheme. More trees generally stabilize rather than overfit, though they consume
memory and latency. Fewer candidate features decorrelate more but can hide signal.

For regression, average outputs. For classification, average class probabilities or votes;
be explicit because they need not yield identical decisions. Leaf frequencies are not
automatically calibrated probabilities.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-oob">
A size-$n$ bootstrap sample draws $n$ times with replacement. The probability an observation
is never drawn is $(1-1/n)^n\to e^{-1}\approx.368$. For each training row, aggregate only
trees for which it was out of bag; compare this prediction with its label. OOB error uses
training data efficiently and can tune many forest choices without a separate validation
slice.

But “omitted from this tree” does not guarantee independence. A global imputer, target
encoding, feature selection, or calibration fitted before bootstrapping leaks information.
Repeated patients, households, or future time points violate row-level exchangeability.
Group/bootstrap structure must follow deployment. With few trees, some rows receive too
few OOB votes, increasing noise.

Use the untouched test set once after OOB-driven choices, and use ordinary structured
cross-validation when the bootstrap assumptions do not match the data-generating process.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-importance">
Mean decrease in impurity sums each feature's credited training split gains. It favors
continuous/high-cardinality features because they offer more candidate thresholds, and it
can assign arbitrary or divided credit among correlated substitutes. Strobl and colleagues
demonstrated this selection bias <Citation id="strobl2007importance" />.

Permutation importance shuffles a feature on held-out data and measures performance loss.
That is closer to predictive reliance, but marginal shuffling can create impossible
feature combinations; correlated alternatives mask one another because the model can use
the unshuffled substitute. Conditional or grouped permutation may better match the question.

**Interview standard.** State the estimand: global predictive reliance, local explanation,
or causal effect. Report validation data, metric, uncertainty across resamples, and how
correlation is handled. Neither impurity importance nor SHAP turns association into cause.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Sequential additive models

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-boosting">
Bagging fits learners independently to perturbed versions of the same task, then averages;
its main target is variance. Boosting fits a sequence where stage $m$ depends on the errors
of $F_{m-1}$, then sums; it progressively expands the function and commonly reduces bias.

The distinction yields operational consequences. Bagging is naturally parallel and
robust to adding trees. Boosting is serial across stages, more sensitive to noisy labels
and outliers depending on loss, and needs shrinkage, weak-tree constraints, subsampling,
and early stopping. Both can reduce variance; “bagging equals variance and boosting equals
bias” is a useful orientation, not a theorem for every setting.

Use out-of-fold validation to compare them under the same preprocessing and budget. Their
probabilities may both require calibration.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-adaboost">
For labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, AdaBoost begins with equal example weights. At step $m$, fit
$h_m$, compute its weighted error $e_m$, assign
$\alpha_m=\tfrac12\log((1-e_m)/e_m)$, and multiply example weights by
$\exp[-\alpha_m y_i h_m(x_i)]$. Misclassified examples receive more weight. The final sign
is $\operatorname{sign}\sum_m\alpha_mh_m(x)$.

Freund and Schapire established the adaptive boosting construction
<Citation id="freundschapire1997adaboost" />. The same update is forward stagewise
optimization of exponential loss $\sum_i\exp[-y_iF(x_i)]$. That functional view explains
both the focus on negative margins and sensitivity to mislabeled outliers: exponential
loss keeps amplifying them.

**Trap.** A base learner with error at or above one half has no positive edge in the binary
setup. It should be repaired/reversed, not given an ordinary positive stage weight.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-gradient-boosting">
Choose any differentiable loss $L(y,F(x))$. At stage $m$, compute pseudo-residuals

$$r_{im}=-\left.\frac{\partial L(y_i,F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right|_{F=F_{m-1}},$$

fit $h_m(x)$ to $r_{im}$, choose a step, and update
$F_m=F_{m-1}+\eta\gamma_mh_m$. For squared error, the negative gradient is the ordinary
residual $y_i-F(x_i)$. For logistic loss it is probability-related, not a raw class error.

Friedman's gradient-boosting formulation turned boosting into optimization over functions
<Citation id="friedman2001gbm" />. Small $\eta$ usually needs more trees but regularizes
stagewise fitting. Shallow trees control interaction order; row/column subsampling adds
stochastic regularization; early stopping chooses effective ensemble size on validation
data.

**Trap.** Fitting a tree to residuals is exact language only for squared loss. Say
“negative loss gradients” for the general algorithm.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-xgboost">
XGBoost uses a regularized second-order approximation. For proposed tree leaf $j$ with
examples $I_j$, let $G_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}g_i$ and $H_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}h_i$. With L2 leaf
penalty $\lambda$, the optimal leaf value is

$$w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda},$$

and its score contribution is proportional to $-G_j^2/(H_j+\lambda)$. Split gain compares
children with the parent and subtracts a leaf-complexity cost. This makes curvature,
regularization, and tree structure explicit.

The system adds sparse-aware default directions, weighted quantile sketches for approximate
splits, column/row subsampling, cache-conscious blocks, parallel split search, and out-of-core
execution <Citation id="chenguestrin2016xgboost" />. Those engineering choices explain the
name's practical importance. The learning principle remains gradient-boosted trees.

Mention library semantics when discussing missing values or categorical support: behavior
differs by implementation and version, so it belongs in an evaluated pipeline contract.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Choosing and operating tree ensembles

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-rf-vs-gbdt">
Prefer a random forest when you need a sturdy baseline with little tuning, parallel training,
natural OOB diagnostics, and resistance to small hyperparameter mistakes. It excels when
individual deep trees have adequate signal and variance is the main enemy. Prefer gradient
boosting when residual structure remains and careful sequential correction can buy tabular
accuracy.

For boosting, validate learning rate, tree depth/leaves, minimum leaf support, subsampling,
regularization, and early stopping jointly. For forests, validate feature subsampling and
leaf structure; increase tree count until metrics and uncertainty stabilize. Compare
training and inference throughput, model size, recalibration need, and retraining cadence.

**Good system answer.** Launch the simpler candidate as a baseline, specify the expected
gain from the other, and design a latency/quality experiment. “Boosting always wins on
tables” ignores small data, label noise, tuning budget, and operational constraints.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-trees-real-data">
**Missing values.** Some boosted implementations learn a default branch from training
gain; other estimators require imputation. Missingness itself may be predictive, so add an
indicator when justified and test changes in the missingness mechanism.

**Imbalance.** Weights or sampling alter the optimized error; thresholding converts scores
to workload. Validate on natural prevalence and calibrate separately. **Leakage.** A tree
will exploit timestamps, post-outcome aggregates, IDs, and target encodings with exceptional
efficiency. Time-aware feature availability checks matter more than regularization.

**Constraints.** Monotonic constraints can encode a defensible rule such as “holding all
modeled variables fixed, greater risk score must not lower predicted risk.” They reduce the
hypothesis space and can improve trust, but correlated proxies and confounding mean the
model is not thereby causal. Interaction constraints similarly encode allowed combinations.

Version the preprocessing/model library because native missing and categorical semantics
are part of the learned function, not an invisible implementation detail.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="Information gain by hand" difficulty="warm-up">
A binary parent has 8 positives and 8 negatives. A split produces a left child with 6
positives and 2 negatives and a right child with 2 positives and 6 negatives. Compute the
Gini gain.

<div slot="solution">
The parent Gini is $.5$. Each child has $p=(.75,.25)$, so Gini is
$1-.75^2-.25^2=.375$. Equal child sizes make the weighted value $.375$, hence gain
$.5-.375=.125$.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="More trees cannot fix shared bias" difficulty="core">
Base predictions have variance 4 and pairwise correlation .30. What is ensemble variance
for 100 trees, and its infinite-tree limit?

<div slot="solution">
$4[.30+.70/100]=1.228$. The limit is $4(.30)=1.2$. Adding trees from 100 onward can remove
at most .028 variance; improving diversity or the representation now matters more than
simply increasing $B$.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A suspiciously perfect feature" difficulty="stretch">
A boosted churn model gets almost perfect validation performance, and `days_since_close`
dominates importance. What should you investigate before tuning anything?

<div slot="solution">
Determine exactly when `days_since_close` becomes known relative to the prediction timestamp.
It likely encodes the outcome or a downstream event. Reconstruct every feature “as of” the
decision time, split temporally or by entity, fit all encoders within training folds, and
rerun the baseline. Importance explains reliance, not legitimacy; no depth, regularizer, or
monotonic constraint repairs target leakage.
</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Tree growth greedily maximizes weighted impurity reduction; depth and leaves set the bias–variance tradeoff.
- Bagging succeeds when accurate base learners have errors that are not perfectly correlated.
- Random feature subsets trade individual-tree strength for lower forest correlation.
- OOB evaluation is convenient, but it inherits leakage and dependence if the resampling unit is wrong.
- Boosting is sequential functional optimization; squared-loss residuals are one special case.
- XGBoost combines regularized second-order tree scoring with consequential systems engineering.
- Feature importance measures model reliance, not causal effect, and correlated features complicate credit.
