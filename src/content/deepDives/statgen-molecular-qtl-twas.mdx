## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson took an association and resolved it into a credible set of variants. That
is progress and it is not an answer, because a set of variants in an intergenic region names
nothing a biologist can work on. The question everyone actually wants answered is *which gene*.

The most-used bridge is to put expression in between. Genotype affects expression; expression
affects the trait; so find the gene whose predicted expression associates with the trait.
That is a transcriptome-wide association study, and it works well enough to be standard.

It also has a failure mode that is not a caveat but a theorem. This lesson derives it. The
practical machinery — running the scan, reading the output, choosing a reference panel — is
the GWAS track's; the colocalisation framework that TWAS is often compared against belongs to
the previous lesson. What is here is the statistic and what it is entitled to claim.

## The statistic

An expression reference — <Citation id="gtex2020" /> is the usual one — gives, for each gene, a
set of weights $\mathbf{w}_g$ predicting its expression from nearby genotypes. Predicted
expression in the GWAS cohort is then $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_g$, and its association with the
trait can be formed from summary statistics alone:

$$
z_g = \frac{\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_g}}
$$

This is PrediXcan <Citation id="gamazon2015predixcan" /> and FUSION
<Citation id="gusev2016fusion" />, which differ in how the weights are fitted and not in the
statistic. Under the null $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R})$, so
$\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_g$
and $z_g$ is standard normal. That is the whole appeal: no individual-level data, one number
per gene, tested like any other association.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Two genes at a locus are predicted from overlapping evidence">

The weights come from eQTLs, and eQTLs are variants. Two genes a hundred kilobases apart are
predicted from variants that sit in the same LD block — sometimes literally the same variants,
often different ones that tag each other well.

So the two predicted expressions are two weighted sums of largely the same numbers. They have
to correlate, and the correlation has nothing to do with whether the genes are biologically
related, co-regulated or even transcribed in the same direction. It is a property of the map.

That is the whole difficulty in one sentence: **a locus is, by definition, a place where the
evidence about neighbouring genes is shared.** Anything that predicts genes from local
genotype inherits that sharing, and no amount of care in fitting the weights removes it.

</Callout>

## One correlation, two roles

Take two genes at the same locus. Their statistics are not independent, and the covariance is

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(z_g, z_h) = \frac{\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_h}
{\sqrt{(\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_g)(\mathbf{w}_h^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_h)}}
$$

Now write down the correlation between the two genes' **predicted expression**. With
standardised genotypes, $\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_g, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_h) =
\mathbf{w}_g^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_h$, normalised the same way. It is the same
expression.

<Callout variant="key" title="The correlation between two TWAS statistics is the correlation between two predicted expressions">

Not approximately, and not under an assumption — identically, because both are the same
quadratic form over the same normalisation. That single fact does all the work below.

It also explains why the problem cannot be filtered away. The correlation is a property of the
eQTL weights and the LD between them, both of which are fixed before the GWAS is run. Nothing
about the trait, the sample size or the significance threshold changes it.

</Callout>

## What a null gene inherits

Suppose the trait acts through gene A's expression and no other. Then the mean of the GWAS
z-vector is proportional to $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_A$, and for any other gene B

$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}[z_B]}{\mathbb{E}[z_A]}
= \frac{\mathbf{w}_B^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_A}
{\sqrt{(\mathbf{w}_B^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_B)(\mathbf{w}_A^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_A)}} = r
$$

exactly. **A gene with no causal role has expected statistic $r$ times the causal one.** Its
own weights, its own expression level, its own biology do not enter.

Simulating an LD block confirms both halves: across genes whose eQTLs sit 0, 2, 4, 6, 10 and
20 variants from the causal gene's, the correlation of predicted expression is 1.000, 0.906,
0.767, 0.649, 0.465 and 0.202, the empirical correlation of the TWAS statistics matches to
within Monte-Carlo error, and $\mathbb{E}[z_B]/\mathbb{E}[z_A]$ equals it to machine precision.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The critical correlation falls as the signal strengthens">

$r^{*} = 4.7081/z_{\mathrm{causal}}$ is a *decreasing* function of the causal statistic. A
locus twice as significant drags in every gene down to half the correlation.

This is the opposite of how a significance threshold usually behaves, and it is worth sitting
with. Strengthening a study — more samples, a better-powered GWAS — does not clean up the gene
list. It lengthens it, and the genes it adds are the ones with weaker claims.

</Callout>

**The threshold.** $0.05/20{,}000$ two-sided gives $|z| > 4.7081$.

**The critical correlation.** $r^{*} = 4.7081/8 = 0.5885$. Any gene whose predicted expression
correlates above 0.5885 with the causal gene's has expected statistic past the threshold.

**Is that a lot?** No. In the simulated LD block above, a gene whose eQTLs sit four variants
away already has $r = 0.767$, and one six variants away has $0.649$. Both clear 0.5885
comfortably. Genes at a locus share a neighbourhood by construction — that is what makes them
a locus.

**As a probability.** The inherited statistic is normal with mean $r z$ and unit variance, so
the chance a null gene actually reaches significance is an ordinary tail:

| r | E[z] at the null gene | chance it reaches significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 4.00 | 0.2394 |
| 0.6 | 4.80 | 0.5366 |
| 0.7 | 5.60 | 0.8138 |
| 0.8 | 6.40 | 0.9547 |
| 0.9 | 7.20 | 0.9936 |

At $r = 0.8$ a gene with **no causal role whatsoever** is reported as transcriptome-wide
significant 95.47% of the time. This is a power calculation for an effect nobody wants to
detect.

**A check.** Each curve passes through exactly one half at its own $r^{*}$, because there the
mean equals the threshold.

</WorkedExample>

## What this does and does not say

It does **not** say TWAS is uninformative. A TWAS hit is strong evidence that *something at
this locus* affects the trait through expression, which is more than a credible set of
intergenic variants supplies.

What it says is that the ranking within a locus is not trustworthy. The gene with the largest
TWAS statistic need not be the causal one; it need only have the largest $r$ with whatever is
causal, and the weights that determine $r$ come from an expression reference of a few hundred
people in a tissue that may not be the relevant one <Citation id="wainberg2019twas" />.

Two responses exist, and both are honest about the problem rather than solving it.
<Citation id="mancuso2019focus" /> fine-maps *within* the TWAS signal, returning a credible set
of genes with posterior probabilities instead of a ranked list — the same move the previous
lesson made for variants, and it inherits the same dependence on getting the correlation
structure right. <Citation id="zhu2016smr" /> tests whether the expression and trait signals
are consistent with one causal variant and adds a heterogeneity test to detect when they are
not.

<Callout variant="assumption" title="TWAS and colocalisation ask different questions">

Colocalisation, derived in the previous lesson, asks whether the trait signal and the
expression signal share a causal *variant* <Citation id="giambartolomei2014coloc" />. TWAS asks
whether predicted expression associates with the trait. These are not the same question and
they fail differently.

Colocalisation's H₃ — two distinct causal variants — is exactly the case TWAS cannot see,
because a weighted sum does not care whether the weight it loaded came from the trait's causal
variant or a neighbour. Conversely a gene can colocalise and still not be causal, if the shared
variant acts on the trait through a different gene.

Running both and requiring agreement is the standard defence. It is a filter, not a proof, and
neither method can distinguish a causal gene from a gene whose expression merely correlates
with the causal one — because at a locus, that correlation is the normal state.

</Callout>

<Exercise n={1} title="A weaker locus, and a stronger one" difficulty="warm-up">

Twenty thousand genes are tested. (a) What is the critical correlation at a causal statistic of
5.5? (b) At 12? (c) State in one sentence what the comparison implies for a study that doubles
its sample size.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $4.7081/5.5 = 0.8560$.

**(b)** $4.7081/12 = 0.3923$.

**(c)** Doubling the sample size multiplies a genuine $z$ by about $\sqrt{2}$, which *lowers*
the critical correlation by the same factor — so the better-powered study reports every gene
the weaker one did, plus every gene between the two critical values. The extra genes are the
ones whose claim to the locus is weakest, and nothing in the output distinguishes them from
the original hit.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Reading a locus with four genes" difficulty="core">

A locus contains four genes. Their predicted expressions correlate with the causal gene's at
$r = 1.00$ (itself), 0.88, 0.71 and 0.34. The causal gene's TWAS statistic is 9.
(a) Which reach significance in expectation? (b) What is the chance the $r = 0.34$ gene does?
(c) A collaborator proposes reporting only the top-ranked gene. What does that assume?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $r^{*} = 4.7081/9 = 0.5231$. The genes at 1.00, 0.88 and 0.71 all exceed it, with
expected statistics 9.00, 7.92 and 6.39. The gene at 0.34 has expected statistic 3.06 and does
not.

**(b)** Its statistic is normal with mean 3.06 and unit variance, so the chance of exceeding
4.7081 is $1 - \Phi(1.648) = 0.0497$ — about one in twenty, which across a genome of loci is
not rare.

**(c)** It assumes the largest statistic belongs to the causal gene. Since
$\mathbb{E}[z_B] = r\,\mathbb{E}[z_A]$ with $r \le 1$, the causal gene does have the largest
*expected* statistic, so the assumption is right on average.

How often it fails is worth computing carefully, because the obvious calculation is wrong. The
two statistics are **not independent** — they are correlated at exactly the same $r = 0.88$
that put the neighbour over the threshold. So
$\mathrm{Var}(z_A - z_B) = 2 - 2(0.88) = 0.24$, a standard deviation of 0.4899 rather than the
$\sqrt{2}$ independence would give, and

$$
\Pr(z_B > z_A) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{-1.08}{0.4899}\right) = \Phi(-2.2045) = 0.0137
$$

about one time in seventy-three. Treating them as independent gives 0.2225 — sixteen times too
pessimistic.

**The correlation cuts both ways, and that is the useful thing to take away.** It is what drags
the innocent gene over the threshold, and it is also what keeps the innocent gene from
outranking the causal one, because it makes the two statistics move together. So the failure
mode of TWAS is *too many genes reported at a locus*, not *the wrong gene ranked first* — which
is why the sensible response is a credible set of genes rather than a better ranking.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Why a bigger reference panel does not fix it" difficulty="stretch">

A colleague argues that the problem comes from noisy eQTL weights, and that a reference panel
ten times larger would resolve it. (a) What does a larger panel improve? (b) Why does it leave
$r$ essentially unchanged? (c) What would actually reduce $r$?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** It improves the *accuracy* of each gene's weights — the predicted expression becomes a
better estimate of true expression, so each gene's own TWAS statistic gains power.

**(b)** Because $r$ is a property of the LD between the variants the weights load on, not of
how precisely they are estimated. Two genes whose eQTLs sit in the same LD block will have
correlated predicted expression however well each is measured; in the limit of perfect
weights, $r$ is the correlation of their *true* genetically-determined expression, which is
still high. A larger panel makes both statistics larger and leaves their ratio alone.

**(c)** Only something that breaks the LD: a population with shorter haplotypes, or a
multi-ancestry analysis where the variants tag differently in each group, or conditioning on
the causal variant directly if it is known. That is the same list as for fine-mapping, and for
the same reason — the limit is the correlation structure of the genome, not the size of the
study.

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A TWAS statistic is $\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}/\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}$**,
  computable from summary statistics and an expression reference.
- **The correlation between two genes' statistics is the correlation between their predicted
  expression** — identically, because both are the same quadratic form over the same
  normalisation.
- **A non-causal gene inherits $\mathbb{E}[z_B] = r\,\mathbb{E}[z_A]$ exactly.** Its own
  biology does not enter.
- **The critical correlation is $r^{*} = 4.7081/z_{\mathrm{causal}}$ and it falls as the signal
  strengthens** — 0.7847 at $z = 6$, 0.5885 at 8, 0.3139 at 15. A stronger locus implicates
  more innocent genes.
- **At $r = 0.8$ and a causal $z$ of 8 a null gene is reported significant 95.47% of the
  time**, and four variants of separation in a realistic LD block already gives $r = 0.767$.
- **A TWAS hit is evidence about a locus, not an identification of a gene.** Fine-mapping the
  TWAS signal or requiring agreement with colocalisation narrows it; neither separates a causal
  gene from one whose expression merely correlates with it.
