## The question this lesson answers

Almost every single-cell paper opens with the same image: a two-dimensional scatter, coloured
by cluster, produced by t-SNE <Citation id="vandermaaten2008tsne" /> or UMAP
<Citation id="mcinnes2018umap" />
<Citation id="becht2019umapviz" />. Readers take a great deal from it — that these two
populations are similar because they sit adjacent, that this one is rare because its blob is
small, that this trajectory is continuous because the points form an arm.

Very little of that is supported. These algorithms optimise a neighbourhood-preservation
objective: for each point, keep the same points nearby. That is the whole contract. It says
nothing about *how* near, nothing about the distance between two groups, and nothing about the
area a group occupies.

This lesson makes the gap between the contract and the reading quantitative.

## The measurable version of "faithful"

Trustworthiness formalises the contract these methods actually make
<Citation id="venna2006localmds" />. For each point it finds the intruders — points that are
in its embedded neighbourhood but were not in its true one — and penalises each by how far
away it really was:

$$
T(k) = 1 - \frac{2}{nk(2n - 3k - 1)}\sum_i \sum_{j \in U_k(i)} \left(r(i,j) - k\right).
$$

It is 1 when no false neighbours are introduced at all, and it falls as the embedding puts
things together that were apart.

Read the definition carefully and its blind spot is already visible: every quantity in it is a
**rank**. Nothing in the formula refers to a distance in the embedding. An embedding can
therefore place a neighbourhood anywhere, at any scale, and score identically — as long as the
*ordering* of neighbours is preserved.

## What that blindness costs

Take six points in one dimension: a triplet at 0, 0.1, 0.2 and another at 10, 10.1, 10.2. Their
centres sit ten apart and each triplet spreads about 0.067 from its own centre, so the true
separation — the between-cluster gap in units of within-cluster spread — is exactly 150.

Now embed them three ways: stretch the gap fifty-fold, crush it tenfold, or leave it alone. In
all three, no point ever changes which point is its nearest, second-nearest, or third. So all
three score

$$
T(2) = 1.000000
$$

while the apparent separation reads 7,500, 15 and 150 respectively — a **five-hundred-fold**
range in the thing a reader would actually conclude from the picture, at a constant, perfect
score.

The contrast with what *does* move the score is stark. Take one point out of the first triplet
and put it in the second — a single mislocated cell out of six — and trustworthiness falls to
**0.466667** immediately. The metric is not insensitive. It is precisely sensitive to
neighbourhood membership and completely blind to scale, which is exactly what its definition
says it should be.

<WorkedExample
  title="Four embeddings, one score"
  given={[
    'Six points: triplets at 0, 0.1, 0.2 and at 10, 10.1, 10.2',
    'Trustworthiness evaluated at k = 2',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the true separation.** Each triplet's centroid is its middle point, and the mean
distance from it is $(0.1 + 0 + 0.1)/3 = 0.066667$. The centres are 10 apart, so

$$
\frac{10}{0.066667} = 150.
$$

**Step 2 — stretch the gap fifty-fold.** Move the second triplet to 500, 500.1, 500.2. Apparent
separation becomes **7,500**. No point's neighbour ordering changed — the second-nearest point
to 0 is still 0.2, whatever happened five hundred units away — so $T(2) = 1.000000$.

**Step 3 — crush it tenfold.** Move the second triplet to 1, 1.1, 1.2. Apparent separation is
now **15**. Still no ordering changed, since 0.2 is nearer to 0.1 than 1 is. Again
$T(2) = 1.000000$.

**Step 4 — the span.** Across three embeddings all scoring a perfect 1.000000, apparent
separation runs from 15 to 7,500:

$$
\frac{7{,}500}{15} = 500.
$$

**Step 5 — where the score finally moves.** Crush the gap fifty-fold, to 0.2001, 0.3001,
0.4001. Now 0.2 and 0.2001 are adjacent, orderings genuinely change, and
$T(2) = 0.933333$ — a drop of under seven points for an embedding in which the two clusters
have effectively merged.

**What this rules out.** Any claim of the form "these two populations are more similar than
those two, because they sit closer in the UMAP". The embedding's distances were never fitted to
mean that, and the standard check for whether an embedding is faithful cannot detect it if they
do not.

</WorkedExample>

## What is safe to read

Given the contract, the features of an embedding sort cleanly.

**Carries information:** which points are adjacent to which. If two cells sit inside the same
dense region, they were neighbours in the graph — and that is exactly what the previous two
lessons built and partitioned. An embedding is a picture of the kNN graph, so it inherits the
graph's quality and nothing more.

**Carries none:** the distance between two clusters, the area or diameter of a cluster, the
density of points within it, and the position of anything relative to the axes. t-SNE and UMAP
both actively normalise local density, so a large sparse blob and a small tight one can be the
same number of equally-related cells <Citation id="kobak2019tsneart" />.

**Depends entirely on parameters:** whether a group appears as one blob or several, and whether
a continuum appears connected or broken. Perplexity and `n_neighbors` change these, and the same
data at two settings supports two different narratives <Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The picture is a hypothesis display, not evidence">

The useful discipline is to treat the embedding as a way of *showing* a result obtained
elsewhere, never as the result.

Cluster identity comes from the graph partition, not from what looks separate. Similarity
between populations comes from a distance computed in PC space, or from a correlation of
pseudobulk profiles — a number you can report and someone can check. Rarity comes from counting
cells, not from blob area. A trajectory needs the analysis of lesson 15, not an arm in a
scatter plot.

Every one of those is a quantity, and every one can go in the text next to the figure. The
figure then does what it is good at: showing the reader where things are, so they can follow
the argument. The stronger claim — that the arrangement itself is evidence — is the one the
algorithm never promised and the one a growing literature keeps finding does not hold
<Citation id="chari2023specious" />
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

</Callout>

## Summary

- t-SNE and UMAP optimise **neighbourhood preservation** and nothing else: which points are
  near, not how near, and not how far two groups are.
- **Trustworthiness** measures exactly that contract, and every term in it is a *rank* — so it
  is scale-free by construction.
- Consequently the apparent separation of two clusters can span **500-fold** — 15 to 7,500 in
  units of within-cluster spread — at trustworthiness **exactly 1.000000**.
- The score is not weak: moving one point of six between clusters drops it to **0.466667**. It
  is sharply sensitive to neighbourhood membership and blind to scale.
- **Adjacency carries information; distance, area and density do not.** Whether a group appears
  as one blob or several depends on parameters, not only on data.
- Report the quantity, show the picture. Similarity is a number in PC space, rarity is a cell
  count, and a trajectory is an analysis.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading a figure legend" difficulty="warm-up">

A paper's figure legend states: "Cluster 3 lies adjacent to cluster 5 and far from cluster 9,
indicating that clusters 3 and 5 represent closely related states."

(a) Which part of that sentence is supported by what UMAP optimises?

(b) What would need to be reported instead?

(c) The authors add that they verified the embedding with a trustworthiness of 0.98. Does that
change your answer?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Essentially none of it. Adjacency of individual *points* is what the algorithm preserves;
the *distance between two clusters* is not something it fits, so "far from cluster 9" carries
no information. Even "adjacent" is weaker than it sounds when applied to whole clusters rather
than to cells.

(b) A distance computed where distances mean something — for example the correlation or
Euclidean distance between cluster centroids in PC space, or between pseudobulk profiles.
That is one number per pair, reportable and checkable.

(c) No, and it is the interesting part. A trustworthiness of 0.98 says the embedding introduced
few false neighbours, which is a genuine and useful fact. It says nothing about inter-cluster
distance, because the statistic is built entirely from ranks — this lesson's embeddings score a
perfect 1.000000 across a five-hundred-fold range of apparent separation. Quoting it in support
of a distance claim is using a correct number as evidence for something it cannot measure.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Computing the blind spot" difficulty="core">

The lesson's six points score $T(2) = 1.000000$ when the gap is stretched fifty-fold and when
it is crushed tenfold.

(a) Why does stretching never change the score, however far you go?

(b) Crushing eventually does. Estimate the gap at which it must, and check it against the
lesson's fifty-fold case.

(c) What does this imply about the direction of the metric's blindness?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Because moving the second triplet further away only increases every cross-cluster distance.
No point's nearest neighbours can change, since they were already all within its own triplet
and remain so. There is no upper limit — the score is 1 at any stretch factor whatsoever.

(b) The orderings change once a point of the other triplet becomes nearer to some point than
that point's own triplet-mates are. Within a triplet the largest internal distance is 0.2, so
the gap must fall to roughly 0.2 before cross-cluster points intrude. A fifty-fold crush of a
gap of 10 gives 0.2, which is exactly that boundary — and indeed the lesson's fifty-fold case
is the first one to move the score, to 0.933333.

(c) The blindness is **asymmetric and unbounded in one direction**. Exaggerating separation is
completely invisible to the metric no matter how extreme; understating it is invisible only
until clusters actually overlap. Since exaggerated separation is what makes a figure
persuasive, the failure mode the metric cannot see is precisely the one that gets published.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Designing a check that would work" difficulty="stretch">

You want a check that would catch an embedding that misrepresents the relationship between two
clusters.

(a) Explain why any purely rank-based measure will fail, however sophisticated.

(b) Propose a check that would succeed, and state what it costs.

(c) Your collaborator objects that if the check is done in PC space, the embedding is
unnecessary. Respond.

<div slot="solution">

(a) A rank-based measure is invariant to any monotone transformation of the distances, and
scaling the gap between two clusters is such a transformation as long as no ordering changes.
So the entire family — trustworthiness, continuity, rank correlations of neighbourhoods —
is blind to it by construction, not by oversight. Sophistication within the family cannot help;
the invariance is the point of the family.

(b) Compare the embedding's inter-centroid distances against the same distances computed in PC
space, as a correlation across all cluster pairs, or simply report the PC-space distances
alongside the figure. The cost is that it needs a distance in PC space to be meaningful — which
lesson 8 showed requires the reduction to have been done well — and that a single number per
pair is less compelling than a picture.

(c) The embedding is not doing the inferential work and was never able to. It is doing
communication: letting a reader see which of forty clusters the argument is about, and follow a
comparison across a figure. That is a real job and worth the panel. The objection is right that
it is not evidence, which is why the numbers belong in the text — and it is the strongest
argument for reporting them there rather than leaving readers to measure the picture with their
eyes.

</div>

</Exercise>
