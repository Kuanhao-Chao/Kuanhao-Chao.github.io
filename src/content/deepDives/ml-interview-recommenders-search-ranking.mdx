## Ranking is allocation under constraints

A recommender does more than predict affinity. It decides which items receive exposure,
under inventory, latency, policy, diversity, and business constraints. Those exposures then
become tomorrow's training labels.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The model sees only what the old system chose to show">
A missing interaction is not a clean dislike: the user may never have seen the item. A
click is not pure relevance: position, thumbnail, price, and curiosity affect it. Treat the
log as outcomes under a serving policy. If exposure and propensities are not logged, many
offline questions are unidentifiable after the fact.
</Callout>

## Retrieval and ranking optimize different bottlenecks

Large systems often combine multiple candidate generators, then apply a more expensive
ranker. YouTube's published architecture is a canonical two-stage example
<Citation id="covington2016youtube" />; its particulars are not a universal recipe.

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-two-stage">
Candidate retrieval must search millions or billions of items in milliseconds, so it uses
compact representations, inverted indexes, graph search, rules, or precomputed lists and
targets high recall. Ranking sees perhaps hundreds or thousands of candidates and can use
cross-features, sequence context, calibrated outcomes, and richer models. Reranking can
enforce eligibility, diversity, quotas, and slate constraints.

Write the key inequality: if the only relevant item enters the candidate set with
probability $r$, no downstream ranker can exceed $r$ top-$K$ recall for that query family.
Measure source-level and union recall, ranker quality conditional on retrieval, and
end-to-end outcomes. Reserve fallbacks for index or feature-store failure.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Collaborative signal and missing labels

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-matrix-factorization">
Matrix factorization predicts

$$
\hat r_{ui}=\mu+b_u+b_i+p_u^Tq_i,
$$

learning embeddings by regularized loss on observed ratings or weighted implicit events.
Koren, Bell, and Volinsky explain why biases and temporal effects matter beside low-rank
interaction structure <Citation id="koren2009matrixfactorization" />. The dot product means
users and items are close when their latent preferences align; dimensions need not have a
stable human meaning because rotations preserve dot products.

Regularize sparse users/items more strongly, keep time-aware splits, and avoid random
interaction splits that place one user's future in training. Cold entities have no learned
vector, so use metadata encoders, priors, or a hybrid model.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-implicit-feedback">
Hu, Koren, and Volinsky model implicit observations with a preference indicator and
observation-dependent confidence <Citation id="hu2008implicit" />. That distinction is
fundamental: play count can increase confidence that an observed positive matters, but zero
plays do not establish dislike.

Define the event and window precisely—impression, click, dwell, purchase, skip—and account
for delayed or censored outcomes. Log the candidate set and display position. Weighting
every non-event as negative overwhelms positives and teaches the exposure policy; sampling
or confidence weighting changes the estimand and must be documented.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-negative-sampling">
For a positive pair $(u,i)$, a sampled-softmax or contrastive loss compares $s(u,i)$ with
sampled items. Uniform negatives teach broad separation; popularity sampling produces hard,
frequent comparisons but can reinforce popularity; in-batch negatives are computationally
efficient but may include items that are actually relevant to another user or duplicate
entities.

If the goal is an unbiased estimate of a full-corpus objective, correct for the sampling
probability in logits or weights. If the sampler deliberately defines a different ranking
task, say so. Track false-negative rate, sampler drift, duplicate handling, and performance
against both easy and hard candidate sets.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-two-tower">
A query/user tower produces $f(u)$ and an item tower produces $g(i)$; retrieval uses a dot
product or distance. Item vectors can be computed offline and inserted in an index, making
online retrieval fast. The factorized form cannot directly attend over every query-item
feature interaction, so a cross model often reranks the resulting set.

Keep online and offline feature transformations identical. Version tower checkpoints and
indexes together, handle embedding staleness, and monitor vector norms. When filtering by
inventory or permission, choose whether filtering occurs inside the index or after
over-retrieval and quantify its effect on recall.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-ann">
Exact nearest-neighbor search scans every vector. Approximate indexes trade some recall for
latency. HNSW builds a multilayer proximity graph and searches through neighbors
<Citation id="malkov2020hnsw" />; graph degree and construction/search breadth affect
memory, build time, recall, and query time.

Benchmark end-to-end on live-like query vectors with required filters. Report candidate
recall versus exact search, p95/p99 latency, memory, update and deletion lag, and shard
imbalance. Quantization can lower memory but perturb neighbors. A 99% ANN recall claim does
not say whether the missed 1% contains the product's rare valuable items.
</InterviewQuestion>

## The training loss is not the product metric

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-pointwise-pairwise-listwise">
Pointwise learning treats $(u,i)$ independently with regression or classification; it is
simple and can estimate calibrated outcomes if exposure is handled. Pairwise loss uses a
preference $i\succ j$, aligning with local swaps but discarding absolute utility. Listwise
loss scores a slate or surrogate of a ranking measure, capturing context at greater cost.

None automatically optimizes user welfare. Choose labels, weights, and loss from the served
decision. If multiple objectives matter—click, satisfaction, safety, revenue—model or
constrain them explicitly and validate the trade-off online rather than hiding them in one
uninterpretable label.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-bpr">
Bayesian Personalized Ranking samples $(u,i,j)$ where $i$ is observed and $j$ is unobserved,
then maximizes $\log\sigma(s_{ui}-s_{uj})$ plus a parameter prior
<Citation id="rendle2009bpr" />. For matrix factorization,
$s_{ui}-s_{uj}=p_u^T(q_i-q_j)$ and gradients move $i$ toward $u$ and $j$ away.

The score is an ordering statistic, not a calibrated click probability. The assumption that
observed $i$ should outrank unobserved $j$ is violated by unseen relevant items, and the
negative sampler determines which comparisons dominate. Time and exposure-aware sampling
can be more faithful than drawing uniformly from the whole catalog.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-ndcg-map-mrr">
Use recall@$k$ for candidate coverage and precision@$k$ when top-set purity matters. MRR is
$1/r$ for the first relevant result and fits navigation tasks. Average precision averages
precision at relevant ranks and MAP averages over queries. NDCG supports graded judgments:

$$
DCG@k=\sum_{r=1}^k\frac{2^{rel_r}-1}{\log_2(r+1)},\qquad
NDCG@k=DCG@k/IDCG@k.
$$

Järvelin and Kekäläinen introduced cumulative-gain measures for graded relevance
<Citation id="jarvelin2002ndcg" />. Define treatment of no-relevant-item queries, ties,
cutoff, gains, and incomplete judgments; otherwise identical metric names can hide different
calculations.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Logged behavior is policy-dependent

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-offline-online">
Offline data was produced by an older retrieval/ranking/UI policy. It under-labels items not
shown, overrepresents popular positions, and cannot capture novelty, latency, or ecosystem
response to the new policy. Offline metric gains are useful iteration gates, not causal
product evidence.

Launch a powered randomized experiment with a declared primary outcome, user-level or other
appropriate randomization, sample-ratio and instrumentation checks, guardrails, and enough
time for novelty and delayed effects. Examine retrieval/ranking slices and latency. An
online lift can still be harmful if it sacrifices retention, creator health, safety, or
catalog diversity.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-position-bias">
If examination probability at rank $r$ is $e_r$, a click roughly combines examination and
relevance. Training directly on clicks makes the current top rank look relevant because it
was visible. Joachims and colleagues developed propensity-weighted learning-to-rank from
biased feedback <Citation id="joachims2017unbiasedltr" />.

For a logged action, inverse propensity weighting uses outcome divided by its probability
under the logging policy. It requires overlap: the old policy must sometimes expose actions
the new policy would choose. Tiny propensities create huge variance; clip only with an
explicit bias trade-off, report effective sample size, and prefer randomized interventions
or doubly robust estimation when feasible.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-cold-start">
New users lack history; use context, onboarding choices, session behavior, population priors,
and safe exploration. New items lack exposure; use content/metadata encoders, creator or
catalog priors, editorial candidates, and an exploration budget. A returning user with a
new intent is a third case: stale history can be worse than none.

Report cohorts by interaction count and item age, not one average. Ensure a new item can be
retrieved before judging its ranker performance, and preserve fallback quality when feature
stores or personalized state are unavailable.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-feedback-loops">
Exposure creates interactions; interactions train the next policy; the policy may then
amplify popularity, narrow consumption, or reproduce historical exclusion. Monitor exposure
share, catalog coverage, concentration, novelty, diversity, cohort utility, and supplier
outcomes beside engagement. Log propensities and keep controlled exploration.

Counterfactual estimators can compare nearby policies under assumptions, but large changes
need experiments. Contextual-bandit deployment on news recommendation demonstrated both
the value of logged randomized exploration and the importance of policy evaluation
<Citation id="li2010contextualbandit" />. Long-term effects may require longer experiments
and explicit ecosystem objectives.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-rank-system-design">
Begin with the decision: who receives which slate, when, under what constraints, and what
long-term utility defines success. Generate complementary candidates—collaborative, content,
search, popular/fresh, and exploration—then deduplicate and apply hard eligibility. Rank
with point-in-time-correct features, rerank for slate diversity and policy, and provide a
safe fallback.

Specify feature and embedding stores, index update/delete SLOs, cache invalidation, sharding,
latency budgets, graceful degradation, and privacy. Log request context, candidate sources,
scores, filters, exposure positions, propensities, versions, and delayed outcomes. Gate
offline releases, experiment online with guardrails, and monitor drift and feedback loops.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Upper-bound the ranker" difficulty="warm-up">
Relevant-item recall is 92% after candidate union. The ranker places a relevant retrieved
item in the top ten for 80% of those queries. What is end-to-end top-ten recall?
  <div slot="solution">
    $0.92\times0.80=0.736$, or **73.6%**, assuming the conditional 80% is measured among
    queries with a relevant candidate. Improving only the ranker cannot recover the missing
    8%.
  </div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Unbiased or impossible?" difficulty="core">
A deterministic logging policy never showed category Z. Can inverse propensity scoring
estimate the value of a target policy that gives Z 10% exposure?
  <div slot="solution">
    No. The logging propensity for Z is zero, violating overlap, so the importance ratio is
    undefined and no logged reward exists for that action. Run bounded randomized
    exploration or another intervention before claiming counterfactual value.
  </div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Debug an online reversal" difficulty="stretch">
NDCG rises offline, but click-through and p95 latency worsen online. Give a diagnosis plan.
  <div slot="solution">
    Verify experiment integrity and instrumentation, then decompose latency across retrieval,
    features, ranking, and reranking. Check whether richer scoring causes timeouts or stale
    fallbacks; compare exposed-item relevance, positions, and candidate recall; slice new
    users/items and devices; and test whether the offline labels reflect the old exposure
    policy. Roll back if guardrails fail before iterating.
  </div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Retrieval maximizes coverage under corpus scale; ranking optimizes richer ordering on a
  smaller set; reranking enforces slate and policy constraints.
- Implicit logs record outcomes after exposure, not clean positive and negative labels.
- Losses and offline ranking metrics are proxies. Randomized online evidence and long-term
  guardrails complete the evaluation.
- A production design includes freshness, fallbacks, propensity and exposure logging, cold
  start, counterfactual assumptions, and feedback-loop monitoring from the start.
