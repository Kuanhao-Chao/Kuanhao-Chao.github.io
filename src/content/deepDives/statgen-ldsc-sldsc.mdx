## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson left a diagnostic that does not work. $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ measures
inflation but cannot say what caused it, and the correction
<Citation id="devlin1999gc" /> proposed — divide every statistic by it — actively destroys
real findings when the inflation is genuine signal.

<Citation id="bulik2015ldsc" /> resolved this with a single observation, and it is worth
stating before any algebra:

> **Confounding shifts every test statistic by about the same amount. Polygenic signal
> shifts a variant's statistic in proportion to how much of the genome that variant tags.**

Those are different functional forms, so if you can measure "how much of the genome a variant
tags", you can regress on it and read the two contributions off separately. That quantity is
the **LD score**, and the entire method is one line fitted to ten million points.

## The LD score, and the expectation

The LD score of variant $j$ is how much linkage disequilibrium it sits in:

$$
\ell_j = \sum_k r^2_{jk}
$$

summed over every variant $k$ in a window, including $j$ itself, so $\ell_j \ge 1$. A variant
in a recombination desert tags hundreds of neighbours and has a large LD score; one in a
hotspot tags almost nothing and has a small one.

Under a polygenic model where causal effects are spread across the genome, a variant's
expected test statistic is

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\chi^2_j\right] = 1 + Na + \frac{N h^2}{M}\,\ell_j
$$

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why the LD score is the variable that tells them apart">

A variant is tested marginally, so its statistic picks up not only its own effect but the
effect of everything it is correlated with. If effects are spread across the genome, a
variant that tags 200 neighbours is standing in for 200 chances to have caught a causal
site, and a variant that tags 5 is standing in for 5. **Real signal therefore scales with
the LD score.**

Now consider stratification. A variant appears associated because its frequency differs
between ancestry groups and ancestry predicts the trait. That mechanism has nothing to do
with how many neighbours the variant tags — a variant in a recombination hotspot is exactly
as capable of differing in frequency between populations as one in a desert. **Confounding
therefore does not scale with the LD score.** It adds a constant.

One effect is a slope; the other is an intercept. Fit a line and you have both.

This is why the method needs no individual-level data, no genotypes and no permission: LD
scores come from a reference panel and the χ² values come from a published summary-statistics
file. It turned a computation that required the cohort into one that requires a download.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Recovering heritability and confounding from six points"
  given={[

    { label: 'LD scores', value: '10, 30, 50, 80, 120, 200' },

**Step 1 — fit the line.** Regressing $\chi^2$ on $\ell$ gives

$$
\text{intercept} = 1.050000, \qquad \text{slope} = 0.025000
$$

**Step 2 — read the slope as heritability.** The slope is $Nh^2/M$, so

$$
h^2 = \text{slope} \times \frac{M}{N} = 0.025 \times \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.2500
$$

**Step 3 — read the intercept as everything else.** A clean analysis has intercept 1. This
one is 1.05, so there is a small amount of confounding or uncontrolled relatedness.

**Step 4 — the attenuation ratio.** The mean $\chi^2$ across these variants is $3.091667$,
so the fraction of the excess attributable to the intercept rather than to polygenicity is

$$
\frac{\text{intercept} - 1}{\text{mean }\chi^2 - 1} = \frac{0.05}{2.091667} = 0.023904
$$

**Only 2.4% of the inflation is confounding.** The other 97.6% is real polygenic signal.

**Step 5 — now compute what the previous lesson would have reported.** The median of these
six statistics is $2.675$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 2.675/0.4549364\ldots = 5.8799$.

A $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ of 5.88 reads as a catastrophically broken analysis, and the
genomic-control prescription would divide every statistic by 5.88 — annihilating every
discovery in the study. The intercept of 1.05 says the analysis is very nearly clean and the
trait is simply extremely polygenic. **The two diagnostics disagree completely on the same
data, and the regression is the one that is right.**

</WorkedExample>

## What replaced genomic control

Three numbers come out of one fit, and modern practice reports all three.

- **The intercept** estimates confounding. Near 1 means clean; substantially above means
  structure, relatedness or sample overlap that the analysis did not handle.
- **The slope** estimates SNP heritability, from summary statistics alone — no genotypes,
  and none of the computational cost of the methods in Module 3.
- **The attenuation ratio** puts the intercept on an interpretable scale by asking what
  fraction of the mean excess it accounts for. Values below about 0.1 are routinely treated
  as acceptable; a high ratio says the inflation is mostly not signal.

The practical rule that follows is short: **report $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ if convention
demands it, act on the intercept.** And never correct test statistics by
$\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$, which after this lesson should look like dividing by a number that
measures the wrong thing.

## Two traits at once: genetic correlation

The same regression run on the *product* of two studies' statistics estimates the genetic
covariance rather than the variance. <Citation id="bulik2015rg" /> works this out and it
gives $r_g$ between any two traits with published summary statistics — no shared samples
required, and sample overlap, if it exists, lands in the intercept where it can be seen
rather than biasing the slope.

That last point is what made the method spread. Estimating a genetic correlation previously
needed individual genotypes for both traits in the same people; afterwards it needed two
downloads. The result was an atlas of correlations across hundreds of traits, built from
studies whose authors never coordinated.

## Stratified LDSC: where in the genome the heritability is

The regression generalises by splitting the LD score into per-annotation LD scores.
<Citation id="finucane2015sldsc" /> partitions the genome into functional categories and
fits

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\chi^2_j\right] = 1 + Na + N\sum_C \tau_C\,\ell_{j,C}
$$

where $\ell_{j,C}$ is variant $j$'s LD score computed only against variants in category $C$,
and $\tau_C$ is that category's per-variant contribution **conditional on every other
category in the model**. Summing gives each category's heritability, and the headline
quantity is the enrichment:

$$
\text{enrichment}_C = \frac{h^2_C/h^2}{M_C/M}
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="Enrichment across a functional partition"
  given={[
    { label: 'Conserved, non-coding', value: '2.5% of variants, 20% of h²' },
    { label: 'Coding', value: '1.1% of variants, 8% of h²' },
    { label: 'Intergenic', value: '48.0% of variants, 15% of h²' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — divide the shares.**

$$
\text{conserved: } \frac{0.20}{0.025} = 8.0000, \qquad
\text{coding: } \frac{0.08}{0.011} = 7.2727, \qquad
\text{intergenic: } \frac{0.15}{0.480} = 0.3125
$$

**Step 2 — read them.** Conserved non-coding sequence carries eight times the heritability
its size would predict. Coding sequence carries seven times. Intergenic sequence carries
about a third of what its size would predict — it is **depleted**, and it is nearly half the
genome.

**Step 3 — notice what this says about GWAS.** The great majority of genome-wide significant
variants are non-coding, which was initially read as a puzzle. It is not: non-coding
regulatory and conserved sequence is where the heritability is. The enrichment in conserved
non-coding sequence is comparable to that in coding sequence, and there is far more of it.

**Step 4 — the caveat that matters.** Enrichment is a marginal quantity. If two annotations
overlap — and in the real baseline model many do — an annotation can look enriched purely by
containing another one that is. That is what $\tau_C$ is for: it is the contribution of a
category *conditional on the others in the model*, and an annotation with high enrichment but
$\tau_C$ indistinguishable from zero is explaining nothing the model did not already know.

</WorkedExample>

disease acts in.

<Callout variant="warning" title="What the regression assumes, and where it breaks">

The expectation $\mathbb{E}[\chi^2] = 1 + Na + (Nh^2/M)\ell$ rests on assumptions, and they
are not always met.

**Effect sizes are assumed independent of LD score.** If variants in low-LD regions have
systematically larger effects — which <Citation id="gazal2017baselineld" /> showed they do —
the basic model is misspecified and heritability is biased. The baseline-LD model exists to
absorb this.

**The variance model is assumed uniform across frequency.** <Citation id="speed2019sumher" />
argues the standard assumption that each variant contributes variance in proportion to
$2p(1-p)$ is wrong for many traits, and that heritability estimates shift materially under a
different assumption. This is a live disagreement, not a settled point, and estimates from
LDSC and from LDAK-style models can differ by a factor that matters.

**The reference panel must match the study population.** LD scores are computed from a
reference panel; if the study population's LD structure differs, the regressor is
mismeasured and the slope is wrong. This is why LDSC results are reported per ancestry and
why applying European LD scores to a non-European study is an error rather than an
approximation.

**It is not a substitute for a well-specified association model.** A clean intercept means
the *summary statistics* show no evidence of confounding at the level LDSC can detect. It
does not mean the mixed model was fitted correctly, and <Citation id="yang2014mlm" />'s
pitfalls remain in force upstream.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Heritability from a slope" difficulty="warm-up">

A study of 250,000 people with 1,100,000 variants reports an LDSC slope of 0.012 and an
intercept of 1.04. (a) What is the SNP heritability? (b) What does the intercept say?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $h^2 = \text{slope} \times M/N = 0.012 \times 1{,}100{,}000/250{,}000 = 0.052800$,
so about 5.3% of the variance is explained by common variants.

**(b)** An intercept of 1.04 is a small excess over 1, consistent with mild residual
structure, some cryptic relatedness, or a modest amount of sample overlap if the study was a
meta-analysis. On its own it is not alarming — but it should be read alongside the mean
$\chi^2$, because an intercept of 1.04 means something very different in a study with mean
$\chi^2 = 1.05$ than in one with mean $\chi^2 = 2.0$. That comparison is exactly the
attenuation ratio, and it is the subject of the next exercise.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Same inflation, opposite conclusions" difficulty="core">

Two studies each report a mean $\chi^2$ of 1.30. Study A has an LDSC intercept of 1.02;
study B has 1.25. (a) Compute each attenuation ratio. (b) What do you conclude about each?
(c) What would $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ have told you?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The ratio is $(\text{intercept}-1)/(\text{mean }\chi^2 - 1)$:

$$
\text{A: } \frac{0.02}{0.30} = 0.066667, \qquad
\text{B: } \frac{0.25}{0.30} = 0.833333
$$

**(b)** Study A's inflation is 6.7% confounding and 93.3% signal — a normal, healthy
polygenic result. Study B's is 83.3% confounding: almost all of its apparent inflation is
artefact, and its association results should not be trusted until the source is found.

**(c)** Nothing useful, and that is the point. Both studies have the same mean $\chi^2$, so
their $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ values are similar and neither is far from 1.3 — a value that
would be read as "slightly inflated, apply genomic control" in both cases. The prescription
would shrink study A's genuine discoveries and would fail to convey that study B is broken.

One number is not enough to describe inflation, because inflation has two sources. That is
the whole content of this lesson.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="An enrichment that means less than it looks" difficulty="stretch">

An annotation covering 1.0% of variants is reported as carrying 10% of the heritability.
(a) What is its enrichment? (b) A colleague concludes the annotation marks causal biology.
What would you check first? (c) How would you tell the difference?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $0.10/0.010 = 10.0000$ — a tenfold enrichment, which sounds decisive.

**(b)** Whether the annotation **overlaps other enriched annotations**. Suppose it is largely
contained within conserved non-coding sequence, which is itself enriched around eightfold.
Then most of the tenfold could be inherited from the containing annotation rather than being
a property of this one, and the number would look almost as large for any annotation drawn
inside conserved sequence — including annotations with no biological meaning at all.

**(c)** By reading $\tau_C$ rather than the enrichment. $\tau_C$ is the per-variant
contribution *conditional on every other annotation in the model*, so an annotation whose
apparent enrichment is entirely inherited from an overlapping one has $\tau_C$ near zero
even while its enrichment is large. The test that matters is whether $\tau_C$ is
significantly non-zero when the baseline model is included, which is exactly why the
baseline model exists and why enrichment is never reported without it.

A second thing to check: the standard error. Enrichment for a 1% annotation is estimated
from a small slice of the genome, and a tenfold point estimate with a confidence interval
running from two to eighteen is a different claim from one running from eight to twelve.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **LD score regression separates confounding from polygenicity** by using the one variable
  that distinguishes them: real signal scales with how much of the genome a variant tags,
  and confounding does not.
- **$\mathbb{E}[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + (Nh^2/M)\ell_j$.** The slope is heritability; the
  intercept is everything else. On the worked example a slope of 0.025 returns
  $h^2 = 0.2500$ exactly, and the intercept of 1.05 accounts for only 2.4% of the inflation.
- **On that same data $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ reads 5.8799.** The two diagnostics disagree
  completely, and the regression is right. Report $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ if convention
  demands it; act on the intercept.
- **It needs only summary statistics**, which is why it turned heritability and genetic
  correlation from computations requiring a cohort into computations requiring a download.
  Sample overlap between two studies lands in the intercept rather than biasing $r_g$.
- **Stratified LDSC partitions heritability across functional annotations.** Conserved
  non-coding sequence and coding sequence each carry seven to eight times their share;
  intergenic sequence, nearly half the genome, carries a third of its share.
- **Enrichment is marginal; $\tau_C$ is conditional.** An annotation can look tenfold
  enriched purely by sitting inside another enriched annotation, which is why the baseline
  model is not optional.
- **The assumptions are real.** Effects are taken to be independent of LD score, the
  frequency-variance relationship is assumed, and the reference panel must match the study
  population. Each has been shown to bite.
