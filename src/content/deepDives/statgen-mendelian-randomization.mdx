## The question this lesson answers

Observational epidemiology is full of associations that are not causes. People with higher
circulating levels of some biomarker get more of some disease — but they also differ in diet,
income, smoking, and a hundred things nobody measured, and the biomarker may be a consequence
of early disease rather than a cause of it.

<Citation id="daveysmith2003mr" /> pointed out that genetics offers a partial escape. Alleles
are assigned at conception, before any of those confounders exist, and are not chosen. A
variant that raises the biomarker assigns its carriers to a lifetime of slightly higher
exposure, in a way that is uncorrelated with income or diet. That is close enough to a
randomised trial to be worth exploiting.

The whole subject is about how close "close enough" is.

## The three assumptions, and the one genetics does not give you

| | Assumption | What it requires | How it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Relevance** | $\mathrm{Cov}(Z, X) \ne 0$ — the variant really moves the exposure | Weak instruments, diagnosed by $F$ |
| 2 | **Independence** | $Z \perp U$ — the variant is unrelated to the confounders | Population structure, assortative mating, dynastic effects |
| 3 | **Exclusion restriction** | $Z$ affects $Y$ **only** through $X$ | Horizontal pleiotropy — and it is not directly testable |

Assumption 1 is checkable from the data. Assumption 2 is what Mendel's randomisation is
supposed to buy, and mostly does — subject to the corrections from Module 4, since ancestry
correlates with both genotype and outcome exactly as it does in any association study.

**Assumption 3 is the problem.** A variant that affects the exposure has no obligation to
affect nothing else, and genes are pleiotropic as a rule rather than as an exception.
<Citation id="lawlor2008mr" /> sets out the framework and is careful about this; most of the
methodology since has been about what to do when assumption 3 fails.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why the ratio estimator is a ratio">

The estimator is easier to remember if you see where the division comes from.

The instrument moves the exposure by $\gamma_X$. If the exposure causes the outcome with
effect $\beta$, then the instrument must move the outcome by $\gamma_X \times \beta$ — the
effect travels *through* the exposure and picks up both factors on the way.

So $\gamma_Y = \beta\,\gamma_X$, and rearranging gives

$$
\beta = \frac{\gamma_Y}{\gamma_X}
$$

That is the whole idea. The instrument's effect on the outcome, divided by its effect on the
exposure, is the exposure's effect on the outcome — **provided the only route from $Z$ to
$Y$ is through $X$**, which is assumption 3 restated as arithmetic.

Two things follow immediately. If $\gamma_X$ is small the ratio divides by something near
zero, which is weak-instrument bias. And if the instrument reaches $Y$ by any other route,
that extra effect lands in the numerator and is attributed to the exposure — which is
pleiotropy, and nothing in the ratio can tell the difference.

</Callout>

## Weak instruments

Dividing by a noisy $\hat\gamma_X$ does not merely inflate the variance; it *biases* the
ratio — and the direction depends on where the two estimates came from. If the same
individuals supply both $\hat\gamma_X$ and $\hat\gamma_Y$ (**one-sample** MR), the two
errors are correlated and the bias runs toward the confounded observational association
the study was run to escape. If they come from independent samples (**two-sample** MR,
which is what the summary-statistic estimators below assume), the errors are independent
and the bias runs toward the **null** instead. The
first-stage F-statistic measures it:

$$
F = \frac{\hat\gamma_X^2}{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\gamma_X)},
\qquad
\text{bias} \approx \frac{1}{F}
$$

The conventional threshold is $F > 10$. It is worth being precise about what that buys:
$F = 10$ leaves roughly **10% of the bias** in place — a tenth of the way toward the
observational estimate in one sample, a tenth of the effect attenuated away in two — not that
the instrument is fine. It is a bias tolerance dressed as a significance threshold.

## Many instruments: IVW

With several instruments, each gives its own Wald ratio and they are pooled exactly as
Module 4 pooled studies — inverse-variance weighted. <Citation id="burgess2013ivw" /> gives
the form:

$$
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}}
= \frac{\sum_j \hat\gamma_{Xj}\hat\gamma_{Yj}/\sigma_{Yj}^2}{\sum_j \hat\gamma_{Xj}^2/\sigma_{Yj}^2}
$$

which is identical to a weighted regression of $\hat\gamma_Y$ on $\hat\gamma_X$ **through the
origin**. That constraint is not a modelling convenience — it *is* assumption 3. Forcing the
intercept to zero states that an instrument with no effect on the exposure has no effect on
the outcome.

<WorkedExample
  title="Eight instruments, three of them pleiotropic"
  given={[
    { label: 'True causal effect', value: 'β = 0.30' },
    { label: 'Instruments', value: '8, all strong (F from 49 to 225)' },
    { label: 'Pleiotropy', value: 'three instruments add +0.02 to the outcome directly' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the per-instrument ratios.** For a valid instrument with
$\hat\gamma_X = 0.15$ and $\hat\gamma_Y = 0.045$:

$$
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Wald}} = \frac{0.045}{0.15} = 0.3000
$$

All five valid instruments give exactly 0.3000. The three pleiotropic ones give
$0.4818$, $0.4538$ and $0.5857$ — inflated, and inflated *most* for the weakest instrument,
because the same $+0.02$ is divided by a smaller $\hat\gamma_X$.

**Step 2 — instrument strength.** Every $F$ runs from 49 to 225, so weak-instrument bias is
not in play here. This matters: the failure about to appear is not a strength problem, and
checking $F$ would not have caught it.

**Step 3 — IVW.**

$$
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}} = 0.365058, \qquad \mathrm{SE} = 0.019436
$$

The truth is 0.30. IVW is **21.7% too high**, with a 95% interval of $0.3270$ to $0.4032$
that **excludes the true value**. It is precise and it is wrong, which is the worst
combination a point estimate can have.

**Step 4 — where the bias comes from.** The three pleiotropic instruments sit above the line
that passes through the origin. Since the line is forced through zero, the only way it can
accommodate them is by tilting upward — so their $+0.02$ is absorbed into the *slope*, which
is precisely the quantity being reported as a causal effect.

</WorkedExample>

## When the exclusion restriction fails

Two methods handle pleiotropy by substituting a weaker assumption for assumption 3. Neither
removes the need for an assumption; they move it.

**MR-Egger** frees the intercept. <Citation id="bowden2015egger" /> shows that the slope of
the unconstrained regression is consistent under **InSIDE** — that the pleiotropic effects
are independent of the instrument strengths. A non-zero intercept is directional pleiotropy:
instruments pushing the outcome one way on average.

**The weighted median** takes the median of the Wald ratios, weighted by precision.
<Citation id="bowden2016median" /> shows it is consistent if **more than half the weight**
sits on valid instruments — which is a different assumption, not a weaker one, and it is
robust to a few badly behaved instruments in a way Egger is not.

<WorkedExample
  title="Three estimators on the same eight instruments"
  given={[
    { label: 'Truth', value: 'β = 0.30' },
    { label: 'Valid instruments', value: '5 of 8, carrying most of the weight' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

| estimator | estimate | SE | 95% interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| IVW | 0.365058 | 0.019436 | 0.3270 to 0.4032 |
| MR-Egger slope | 0.264912 | 0.157663 | −0.0441 to 0.5739 |
| weighted median | 0.300000 | — | — |

**Step 1 — IVW is precise and excludes the truth.** Its interval is narrow and wrong.

**Step 2 — Egger is close and enormously imprecise.** The slope, 0.264912, is nearer the
truth than IVW's. But its standard error is $0.157663$ — **8.1 times wider** than IVW's — so
the interval runs from below zero to 0.57. Egger has traded almost all of its power for a
weaker assumption, and on this data it cannot distinguish a causal effect from none at all.

**Step 3 — the weighted median is exact.** $0.300000$, because five of eight instruments are
valid and they carry a majority of the weight. This is the case the median was built for.

**Step 4 — now read Egger's intercept.** It is $0.011228$ with a standard error of
$0.017208$, so

$$
t = \frac{0.011228}{0.017208} = 0.6525, \qquad p = 0.5141
$$

**Not significant.** And yet the pleiotropy is real: three of eight instruments were
constructed with a direct effect on the outcome, and IVW is biased by 21.7% because of it.

This is the diagnostic's central limitation, and it is routinely misread. **A non-significant
Egger intercept is not evidence that there is no pleiotropy.** The test is underpowered with
a realistic number of instruments, and reporting "Egger intercept p = 0.51, so no evidence of
pleiotropy" — as papers do — states almost nothing.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Agreement between methods is weak evidence, and disagreement is strong">

The standard practice is to report IVW alongside Egger, the weighted median and
<Citation id="verbanck2018presso" />'s MR-PRESSO, and to treat agreement as reassurance.
Half of that is sound.

**Disagreement is informative.** If IVW says 0.37 and the median says 0.30, something is
violating assumption 3, and the IVW estimate should not be the headline.

**Agreement is much weaker than it looks.** The methods are computed from the *same* eight
numbers and fail together under the failure modes that matter most. If pleiotropy is
*balanced* — as likely to push down as up — then Egger's intercept is near zero, IVW is
unbiased, and all three agree. But if pleiotropy is correlated with instrument strength,
InSIDE fails, and Egger is biased in the same direction as IVW while agreeing with it.
Concordance across methods that share an assumption is not independent confirmation.

<Citation id="davies2018reading" /> is the practical guide to reading these studies, and its
recommendations are mostly about what to look at besides the point estimate.

</Callout>

## Three things the two-variable picture leaves out

Everything so far has one exposure, one outcome and instruments taken as given. Each of those
is an assumption, and each has a standard repair.

### The exposure is not the only thing the instrument moves

Assumption 3 says the instrument affects the outcome only through the exposure. When a variant
plausibly acts through a *second* measured trait — body-mass index and lipids share instruments
constantly — the honest response is not to hope, but to condition.

**Multivariable MR** <Citation id="sanderson2019mvmr" /> extends the weighted regression to
several exposures at once, fitting

$$
\hat\gamma_Y = \beta_1 \hat\gamma_{X_1} + \beta_2 \hat\gamma_{X_2} + \varepsilon
$$

so each coefficient is the effect of one exposure holding the other fixed. This does not remove
assumption 3; it *narrows* it, from "no path except through the exposure" to "no path except
through the exposures in the model". That is a weaker claim and therefore a better one, and it
comes at the usual price — the instruments must now be strong for each exposure *conditionally*,
which is a stricter requirement than being strong for either alone.

### The arrow might point the other way

Nothing in the algebra distinguishes "X causes Y" from "Y causes X". Both give a variant that
associates with both traits, and the ratio $\hat\gamma_Y/\hat\gamma_X$ is computable either way.

**Steiger filtering** <Citation id="hemani2017steiger" /> resolves this by asking which trait
the variant explains *better*. If the instrument is a genuine cause of X and X causes Y, the variant's correlation
with X should exceed its correlation with Y, because the effect on Y is transmitted through X and
attenuated by everything else that affects Y. Comparing the two correlations gives a directional
test, and instruments failing it are dropped.

The caveat is in the paper's title: *imprecisely measured* traits. If Y is measured much more
accurately than X, the correlation comparison can favour Y even when X is the cause, so the
test's answer depends on measurement error in both traits.

### The instruments were chosen by looking at the data

This one is quantifiable. Genome-wide significance is a selection rule, and a variant selected
at $5\times10^{-8}$ has an inflated $\hat\gamma_X$ — the winner's curse of the meta-analysis
lesson, applied to the denominator of a ratio.

<Callout variant="key" title="Selection biases the causal estimate toward the null">

$\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Wald}} = \hat\gamma_Y/\hat\gamma_X$, so an inflated denominator makes
the ratio too **small**. Conditioning on $|z| > 5.4513$, the expected inflation of an
instrument whose true $z$ is 5.6 — one that just cleared the bar — is a factor of 1.1260,
which biases the causal estimate 11.2% low. At a true $z$ of 6 it is 7.5%, at 7 it is 1.8%,
and by 8 it has effectively vanished.

Two things follow. The bias is worst for the marginal instruments, which are the ones a larger
scan keeps adding. And it runs **toward the null**, which is the opposite direction from the
pleiotropy above — so a study with both may report something close to the truth for entirely
the wrong reasons.

The fix is the same as everywhere else: select instruments in one sample and estimate
$\hat\gamma_X$ in another. Two-sample MR with non-overlapping cohorts does this by
construction, which is one of its real advantages over the one-sample design and not merely a
convenience.

</Callout>

## What genetic randomisation does not randomise

Assumption 2 needs more care than "alleles are randomly assigned" suggests, because the
randomisation is *within families*, not across a population.

<Citation id="brumpton2020withinfamily" /> shows three mechanisms that break it in
population samples. **Dynastic effects**: a parent's genotype affects the child's environment,
so the child's genotype correlates with an environmental exposure. **Assortative mating**:
partners resemble each other on a trait, which induces correlations between variants that
Mendel's law would have kept independent. **Residual population structure**: the same
confounding Module 4 spent a lesson on.

The fix is a within-sibship design, comparing siblings who differ in genotype — where the
randomisation genuinely is random, because which alleles a sibling received is determined at
meiosis. The estimates are substantially attenuated for some traits, which is the size of the
problem in population MR rather than a curiosity.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="One instrument, two checks" difficulty="warm-up">

An instrument has $\hat\gamma_X = 0.15$ with $\mathrm{SE} = 0.010$, and
$\hat\gamma_Y = 0.045$. (a) Compute the Wald ratio. (b) Compute $F$ and say whether the
instrument is strong. (c) A second instrument has $\hat\gamma_X = 0.02$ with the same
standard error. What is its $F$, and what does that imply?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\hat\beta = 0.045/0.15 = 0.300000$.

**(b)** $F = (0.15/0.010)^2 = 225$. Comfortably strong — the bias from weak-instrument
effects is around $1/225$, well under a percent.

**(c)** $F = (0.02/0.010)^2 = 4$. The bias is roughly $1/4 = 25\%$, **toward the
observational association**. An instrument this weak does not merely add noise; it drags the
estimate back toward the confounded association MR was supposed to escape, which is the
opposite of what the method is for.

Note what $F$ does *not* check. It is entirely about assumption 1. An instrument with
$F = 225$ can still violate the exclusion restriction completely, and in the worked example
every instrument is strong while three are invalid.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Removing the bad instruments" difficulty="core">

Suppose you knew which three instruments were pleiotropic and dropped them. (a) What does
IVW return on the remaining five? (b) What does this tell you about IVW? (c) Why is this not
a usable strategy?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IVW}} = 0.300000$ with $\mathrm{SE} = 0.024214$ — exact.

**(b)** That IVW is not broken. It is the efficient estimator *when its assumption holds*,
and its 21.7% bias in the worked example is entirely attributable to three invalid
instruments. The problem is never the estimator; it is the instrument set.

**(c)** Because you do not know which instruments are pleiotropic. That is the entire
difficulty: assumption 3 is not directly testable, and the pleiotropic instruments in the
worked example are indistinguishable from the valid ones by any within-study check — they
are strong, they are individually significant, and their Wald ratios are not extreme enough
to look like outliers without knowing the answer.

Methods like MR-PRESSO do attempt exactly this — detect and remove outlying instruments —
and they work when pleiotropy affects a minority of instruments and is large enough to see.
They fail quietly when it is widespread and modest, which is the case the whole field
worries about.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Reading a published MR result" difficulty="stretch">

A paper reports: IVW $\hat\beta = 0.37$ (95% CI 0.33 to 0.40); weighted median 0.30;
MR-Egger slope 0.26 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.57), intercept $p = 0.51$. The authors conclude a
causal effect of 0.37 and note "no evidence of directional pleiotropy". Evaluate.

<div slot="solution">

**The conclusion is not supported, and the pleiotropy sentence is the clearest error.**

*On the intercept.* $p = 0.51$ means the test did not detect pleiotropy, which given its
power says very little. These are the worked example's numbers, where pleiotropy is present
by construction in three of eight instruments and biases IVW by 21.7%. "No evidence of
directional pleiotropy" should read "this test is not powered to detect the pleiotropy that
may be present" — and the honest version is that the intercept is uninformative.

*On the discrepancy.* IVW at 0.37 and the weighted median at 0.30 disagree by more than IVW's
entire confidence interval. That is the finding. When a robust estimator moves the answer by
more than the primary estimator's stated uncertainty, the primary estimator's uncertainty is
not describing the real uncertainty.

*On Egger.* Its interval includes zero, so it neither supports nor refutes a causal effect.
Quoting it as agreement is wrong in both directions: it does not corroborate 0.37, and it
does not establish 0.

**What should be reported:** that the estimates range from 0.26 to 0.37 depending on which
assumption is made, that they are not independent checks, and that the data cannot narrow it
further. If a single number is required, the weighted median is the better default here
because its assumption — a majority of weight on valid instruments — is more plausible than
IVW's requirement that *every* instrument be valid.

And separately: none of these methods addresses whether the population sample carries dynastic
or assortative-mating confounding, which a within-sibship analysis would test and none of the
three sensitivity analyses touches.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="Which estimator, and on what evidence?" difficulty="stretch">

Using Figure 3: (a) at four pleiotropic instruments of eight, which estimator is closest to
the truth and which is furthest? (b) At eight of eight the answer reverses completely — explain
why. (c) A referee asks you to "use the most robust method". What is wrong with the request?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The weighted median is exact at 0.3000; MR-Egger is furthest at 0.1596, wrong by 47%
and in the *opposite* direction from IVW's 0.3818.

**(b)** At eight of eight every instrument carries the same $+0.02$, so the pleiotropy is
perfectly directional and constant. That is exactly what Egger's intercept is built to absorb:
the intercept takes the whole $0.02$ and the slope returns 0.3000. The weighted median, which
assumes a *majority* of instruments are valid, now has none and returns 0.4679. IVW, forced
through the origin, is at its worst at 0.4784.

**(c)** There is no most robust method, only methods robust to different violations. Egger
needs pleiotropy to be directional and uncorrelated with instrument strength; the median needs
the invalid instruments to be a minority; IVW needs no pleiotropy at all. Each is exactly right
where its own assumption holds and can be the worst of the three where it does not. Choosing
between them requires a belief about the pleiotropy, which is unobserved — which is why the
useful output of an MR analysis is the *pattern* across estimators, not one number from the one
that sounds safest.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} title="Two biases pointing in opposite directions" difficulty="stretch">

A two-sample MR uses instruments selected at $5\times10^{-8}$ in the same cohort that estimated
$\hat\gamma_X$. Several instruments sit just past the threshold, and there is also modest
positive directional pleiotropy. (a) Which way does each bias push? (b) The reported estimate
comes out very close to the observational association. What should you conclude? (c) What
single design change removes one of the two?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The winner's curse inflates $\hat\gamma_X$, so the ratio $\hat\gamma_Y/\hat\gamma_X$
is biased **toward the null** — 11.2% low for an instrument whose true $z$ is 5.6. Directional
pleiotropy adds a constant to $\hat\gamma_Y$, which IVW can only absorb by tilting the line, so
it biases the estimate **away from the null**.

**(b)** Nothing reassuring. Two biases of opposite sign can cancel to any degree, so an estimate
that looks sensible is not evidence that either is absent — and the cancellation is a
coincidence of magnitudes that will not survive a change of instrument set. Agreement with the
observational association is particularly weak evidence here, since confounding is what MR was
brought in to escape.

**(c)** Split the samples: select the instruments in one cohort and estimate $\hat\gamma_X$ in
another. That removes the winner's curse entirely and leaves the pleiotropy, which the estimator

## Summary

- **MR exploits that alleles are assigned at conception**, giving a variant the character of
  a randomised assignment to lifelong exposure — which buys assumption 2 largely, assumption
  1 checkably, and assumption 3 not at all.
- **The Wald ratio is $\hat\gamma_Y/\hat\gamma_X$** because the effect travels through the
  exposure and picks up both factors. Pleiotropy lands in the numerator and is attributed to
  the exposure; nothing in the ratio can separate them.
- **$F > 10$ is a bias tolerance, not a significance threshold.** At $F = 10$ about 10% of
  the confounding remains; at $F = 4$, 25%.
- **IVW is a weighted regression through the origin**, and that constraint *is* assumption 3.
  With three of eight instruments pleiotropic it returns 0.365058 for a truth of 0.30 — 21.7%
  high, with an interval that excludes the true value.
- **MR-Egger frees the intercept** and lands at 0.264912, but with a standard error 8.1 times
  wider; its interval spans below zero to 0.57. **The weighted median returns 0.300000
  exactly**, needing only that a majority of weight be valid.
- **A non-significant Egger intercept is not evidence of no pleiotropy.** On data where
  pleiotropy is present by construction, the intercept test gives $p = 0.5141$.
- **Disagreement between methods is informative; agreement is weak**, because they are
  computed from the same numbers and fail together under correlated pleiotropy.
- **Population MR does not randomise everything.** Dynastic effects, assortative mating and
  residual structure survive it, and only a within-sibship design tests for them.
