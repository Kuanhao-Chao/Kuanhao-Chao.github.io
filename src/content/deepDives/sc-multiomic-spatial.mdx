## The question this lesson answers

Sixteen lessons have worked with one modality, from dissociated cells, with the coordinates
thrown away. Each of those is a choice, and each can be undone: measure protein alongside RNA,
measure chromatin alongside both, or keep the tissue intact and record where every measurement
came from.

Every one of those additions is worth having. Every one also removes an assumption that
something earlier in this track was standing on, and the useful way to approach a new assay is
to ask which one.

## A second modality, and what it does not give you

CITE-seq tags antibodies with sequencable barcodes, so protein abundance and mRNA are read from
the same cell in the same reaction <Citation id="stoeckius2017citeseq" />. Multiome does the
same for chromatin accessibility. The gain is real and specific: lesson 11 showed that a
marker's contrast against the ambient soup is capped at $1 + (1-\alpha)/(\alpha\varphi)$, and
that ceiling is a property of *that gene in that soup*. A protein measurement has its own
ambient background, with its own composition and its own $\alpha$, so it is not bound by the
RNA ceiling — which is why surface markers so often separate populations that transcript
counts cannot.

What it does not give you is a second opinion. The two modalities come from one barcode, so
they share every artefact that acts on barcodes: a doublet is a doublet in both, ambient
contamination arrives in both from the same lysed cells, and a stressed cell is stressed in
both. When protein and RNA agree, that agreement is evidence about the biology only to the
extent that the artefacts do not already force it — and for doublets, which lesson 3 showed
produce coherent intermediate profiles, they do force it. A doublet of a T cell and a monocyte
is CD3-positive and CD14-positive in protein exactly as it is in RNA.

The corollary is that hashing remains the thing that actually catches doublets
<Citation id="stoeckius2018hashing" />, and that a multi-modal dataset needs the same doublet
handling as a single-modal one rather than less <Citation id="hao2021seurat" />.

## Keeping the coordinates breaks something bigger

Spatial methods — sequencing on a patterned array, on a bead surface
<Citation id="rodriques2019slideseq" />, or by imaging with multiplexed probes
<Citation id="lewis2021spatialomics" /> — record where each measurement came from. The gain is
enormous and needs no arguing: tissue architecture is what most of the biology is *about*, and
dissociation destroys it.

The cost is that the observations are no longer independent, and independence is what every
test in this track has assumed after the clustering was accounted for. Two spots a hundred
microns apart share a niche, a vessel, an oxygen gradient and usually a clone. They are
correlated, and correlated observations carry less information than independent ones — which is
exactly the argument of lesson 12, arriving from geometry instead of from donors.

Put a field of $n$ spots on a line with correlation $\rho$ between neighbours, decaying
geometrically with distance. The variance of the field mean is inflated by

$$
\mathrm{DE} = 1 + \frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\,\rho^{k},
$$

which converges, as the field grows, to

$$
\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}.
$$

At $\rho = 0.2$ that is 1.50; at 0.5, **3.00**; at 0.8, 9.00; at 0.9, **19.00**.

The consequence is the same as it was for cells within a donor: **more spots do not buy
proportionally more information.** A field of two thousand spots at $\rho = 0.5$ carries the
information of **667** independent ones, and enlarging the field does not change $\rho$.

<WorkedExample
  title="What a spatial field is worth"
  given={[
    'A field of 2,000 spots',
    'Correlation ρ = 0.5 between neighbouring spots, decaying geometrically',
    'A mean expression level compared between two conditions',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the design effect.** Summing the series for $n = 2{,}000$ gives
$\mathrm{DE} = 2.998$, essentially the limit $(1+0.5)/(1-0.5) = 3$.

**Step 2 — the effective field size.**

$$
n_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{2{,}000}{2.998} = 667.1,
$$

so the field is worth **667** independent spots, and a test that treats all 2,000 as
independent reports a standard error $\sqrt{3} = 1.732$ times too small.

**Step 3 — what enlarging the field buys.** At 20,000 spots the design effect is 2.9998 and
the effective size is 6,667 — ten times the spots, ten times the information, because $\rho$
governs the *ratio* rather than the total. Enlarging works. What does not work is treating the
enlargement as though it removed the problem.

**Step 4 — where it stops working.** Raise the correlation instead. At $\rho = 0.9$ a field of
20,000 spots has $\mathrm{DE} = 18.99$ and is worth **1,053** independent spots — from ten
times the data, a sixth of the effective size at $\rho = 0.5$. Strong spatial structure is
scientifically the interesting case and statistically the expensive one.

**Step 5 — measuring ρ rather than assuming it.** Moran's I estimates it from the data. Its
null expectation is **not zero** but $-1/(n-1)$, which for 50 spots is $-0.0204$ — so a small
negative I is what no autocorrelation looks like, not evidence against it. A smooth gradient
across 50 spots gives $I = 0.9592$; a perfect checkerboard gives $-1$.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The same quantity, reached three ways">

This is where the track ends, and it ends on the number it opened with.

The hub derived $1 + (m-1)\rho$ from cells sharing a donor: they are not replicates, so a
per-cell test's false-positive rate rises with the number of cells. Lesson 13 reached the same
structure from composition, where a Dirichlet-multinomial has $\rho = 1/(1+\alpha_0)$ and the
design effect follows unchanged. And this lesson reaches it from geometry, where neighbouring
spots are correlated and the inflation converges to $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$.

Three unrelated routes — biology, composition, space — to one quantity, because all three are
the same situation: observations that look independent and are not. Whenever a single-cell
analysis produces a p-value that seems too good, this is the first thing to check, and the
check is always the same question. **How many independent things were actually measured?**

</Callout>

## What each addition costs, in one table

| Assay | What it buys | Which lesson's assumption it breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CITE-seq, multiome | A modality with its own ambient ceiling | Lesson 3 — the modalities share every barcode-level artefact |
| Hashing, genotype demultiplexing | Doublets caught regardless of type | None; it strengthens lesson 3 |
| Spatial arrays and imaging | Tissue architecture | Lesson 12 — observations are no longer independent |
| Lineage barcoding | True ancestry rather than inferred | Lesson 15 — pseudotime is no longer the only ordering available |
| Metabolic labelling | Observed direction rather than fitted | Lesson 16 — γ no longer has to be estimated |

The last two rows are worth noticing: they are the assays that *remove* a limitation this track
spent a lesson explaining, rather than adding one. Where a question genuinely turns on
direction or on ancestry, they are the answer, and no amount of care applied to a snapshot
substitutes for them.

## Summary

- Every extension buys something real and breaks an assumption. Asking *which* is the fastest
  way to evaluate a new assay.
- A **second modality** escapes lesson 11's ambient ceiling, because its background has its own
  composition — but it shares every barcode-level artefact, so agreement between modalities is
  not independent corroboration. Doublets are doublets in both.
- **Spatial coordinates** break independence, which every test after lesson 12 relied on. The
  variance of a field mean is inflated by $1 + (2/n)\sum(n-k)\rho^k$, converging to
  $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$: **3.00** at $\rho = 0.5$, **19.00** at $\rho = 0.9$.
- Two thousand spots at $\rho = 0.5$ are worth **667** independent ones. Enlarging the field
  helps proportionally; raising the correlation does not.
- **Moran's I has a null of $-1/(n-1)$**, not zero. A small negative value is the absence of
  autocorrelation, not evidence against it.
- The design effect has now been reached three ways — from donors, from composition, and from
  space. The question behind all three is how many independent things were measured.

<Exercise n={2} title="Reading Moran's I" difficulty="core">

A 50-spot field returns $I = -0.02$ for gene A, $I = 0.96$ for gene B, and $I = -0.98$ for
gene C.

(a) What does each value indicate?

(b) A colleague reports gene A as "spatially anti-correlated". Respond.

(c) Which of the three genes is most affected by the design effect, and which least?

<div slot="solution">

(a) The null expectation is $-1/(n-1) = -0.0204$, so gene A is **indistinguishable from no
spatial structure**; gene B has a strong smooth gradient; and gene C alternates almost perfectly
between neighbours, like a checkerboard.

(b) Gene A's value is essentially exactly the null. Reading a small negative I as
anti-correlation is the standard trap — the expectation under no autocorrelation is negative,
not zero, because each spot is excluded from its own mean. Anti-correlation would look like
gene C.

(c) Gene B most: positive autocorrelation is what inflates the variance of a mean, and at
$I \approx 0.96$ the design effect is very large. Gene A least, since at $\rho \approx 0$ the
design effect is 1. Gene C is the interesting case — strong *negative* autocorrelation makes
neighbouring spots more different than random ones, which *deflates* the variance of a mean
rather than inflating it, so a naive test on gene C is conservative rather than anti-conservative.

</div>

</Exercise>
