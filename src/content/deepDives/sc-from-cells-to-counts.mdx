## The question this lesson answers

You are handed a matrix. Twenty thousand rows called genes, ten thousand columns called
cells, and every entry a small non-negative integer, mostly zero. Everything the rest of
this track does — normalising, clustering, annotating, testing — is done to that matrix.

So it is worth being exact about what it is. It is not a measurement of how much of each
transcript was present in each cell. It is a record of which molecules survived a specific
physical process, tagged with which labels, and observed how many times. Nearly every
analysis decision in the following sixteen lessons exists to undo one step of that process,
and you cannot undo a step you cannot name.

This lesson walks the path forward, from tissue to integer. Two of the steps are governed by
the same distribution and are worth deriving rather than describing, because both produce
numbers you will be asked to defend: the rate at which two cells land in one partition, and
the rate at which two molecules receive the same barcode.

## The path a transcript takes

Seven steps, each of which leaves a signature in the matrix.

**Dissociation.** A tissue is cut, digested and triturated until it is a suspension of single
cells. This is the least reversible step in the protocol and the one least often reported.
Cell types differ enormously in how well they survive it — neurons and adipocytes are fragile,
lymphocytes are not — so the composition of the suspension is already not the composition of
the tissue. Worse, dissociation is itself a stress: it induces immediate-early genes such as
*FOS*, *JUN* and the heat-shock family within minutes, and those genes will show up later as a
"cluster" with a coherent expression programme that exists nowhere in the animal.

**Partitioning.** The suspension is divided so that each cell ends up alone in a reaction
volume with a bead carrying a barcode. Droplet methods do this in oil
<Citation id="macosko2015dropseq" /><Citation id="klein2015indrop" />, and their commercial
descendant made the approach routine <Citation id="zheng2017chromium" />; combinatorial-index
methods achieve the same isolation without physical partitions, by splitting and pooling cells
through successive rounds of barcoding <Citation id="cao2017sciRNA" />. Whichever is used,
cells arrive at random, which is the subject of the next section.

**Barcoding and reverse transcription.** Inside the partition, mRNA is captured — usually by
its poly(A) tail, which means most protocols see the 3′ end of a transcript and nothing else —
and reverse-transcribed onto a primer carrying two labels: a **cell barcode**, shared by every
molecule from that partition, and a **unique molecular identifier**, drawn at random and
different for each molecule <Citation id="kivioja2011umi" />. The cell barcode is what makes
the columns; the UMI is what makes the entries counts rather than intensities.

**Amplification.** There is roughly a picogram of RNA in a cell, and a sequencer needs
nanograms, so the material is amplified by several orders of magnitude. Amplification is
exponential and therefore stochastic: two molecules that started equal do not finish equal. If
you counted reads, you would be counting amplification luck. You count distinct UMIs instead,
which is the entire reason the UMI exists <Citation id="islam2013umi" />.

**Sequencing.** The library is sequenced to some depth, and each read is assigned to a cell by
its cell barcode and to a gene by alignment. Depth is a budget decision, not a property of the
biology, and it varies several-fold between cells in the same run for reasons that have nothing
to do with those cells.

**Counting.** For each (gene, cell) pair, count the distinct UMIs. That integer is $c_{gj}$.

**What you are left with.** A matrix whose entry $c_{gj}$ is the number of distinct molecules
of gene *g* recovered from partition *j*. Not the number present. Recovery is on the order of
10–30% for droplet protocols, it differs by gene, and it differs by cell.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The count is a sample, not a measurement">

If you take one cell, split its lysate in two, and process each half separately, you get two
different count vectors. Not slightly different — substantially different, with many genes at
zero in one half and non-zero in the other. Nothing biological distinguishes the halves.

This is the single most useful thing to hold onto in the whole track. A count is a draw from a
distribution, and the analysis pipeline is a long argument about what that distribution is. The
next lesson makes the argument precisely; here it is enough to know that the variability is
there before any biology has been allowed to contribute.

</Callout>

## Partitioning is Poisson, and that fixes the doublet rate

Cells are dilute, they do not interact, and the instrument makes a great many partitions. Those
are exactly the conditions for a Poisson process. If $\lambda$ is the mean number of cells per
partition, the number in any given partition is

$$
\Pr(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}.
$$

Only occupied partitions produce a barcode, so the quantities that matter are conditional on
$X \geq 1$. The fraction of partitions that yield anything is $1 - e^{-\lambda}$, and the
fraction holding more than one cell is $1 - e^{-\lambda} - \lambda e^{-\lambda}$. Dividing gives
the **multiplet rate** — the fraction of the barcodes in your matrix that are not single cells:

$$
r(\lambda) = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda} - \lambda e^{-\lambda}}{1 - e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

Expanding both parts for small $\lambda$, the numerator goes as $\lambda^2/2$ and the
denominator as $\lambda$, so

$$
r(\lambda) \approx \frac{\lambda}{2}.
$$

At $\lambda = 0.01$ the exact rate is 0.4992% against the approximation's 0.5000%, an error of
0.167%. The approximation is excellent in the regime everyone works in, and it says something
worth saying out loud: **the doublet rate is half the loading concentration, and has nothing to
do with the quality of the instrument.**

### Why it is quoted per thousand cells recovered

Nobody sets $\lambda$ directly. You load a suspension and get back some number of cells, so the
natural control variable is $R$, the cells recovered. Since $R = D(1 - e^{-\lambda})$,

$$
\lambda = -\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{R}{D}\right) \approx \frac{R}{D},
$$

and substituting into $r \approx \lambda/2$ gives $r \approx R/(2D)$. The doublet rate is
**linear in cells recovered**, with a slope of $1/(2D)$ set by the number of partitions.

That is where the field's rule of thumb comes from. Manufacturers quote roughly 0.8% doublets
per 1,000 cells recovered, which is $500/D$ per thousand and therefore implies $D = 62{,}500$
effective partitions. Everything below uses that figure, and it reproduces the quoted rule by
construction: 0.80% at 1,000 cells.

The rule is a linearisation, though, and $r(\lambda)$ is convex. At 10,000 cells the exact rate
is 8.46% rather than 8.00%; at 20,000 it is 18.05% rather than 16.00%. The gap grows precisely
where people push hardest.

**Step 1 — the loading concentration.** Recovering 10,000 of 62,500 partitions requires

$$
\lambda = -\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{10{,}000}{62{,}500}\right) = -\ln(0.84) = 0.17435.
$$

**Step 2 — the multiplet rate.**

$$
r = \frac{1 - e^{-0.17435} - 0.17435\,e^{-0.17435}}{1 - e^{-0.17435}} = 0.084645,
$$

so **8.46%**, against the 8.00% the linear rule promises. In absolute terms, 846 of the 10,000
barcodes contain more than one cell.

**Step 3 — how many of those can be caught.** Pooling four samples and labelling each with a
distinct hashtag makes a doublet detectable whenever its two cells came from different samples,
which happens with probability $(S-1)/S = 3/4$. So 635 of the 846 are detectable and are thrown
out; **212 remain**, invisible.

**Step 4 — the rate that actually enters the analysis.** After removing 635 barcodes, 9,365
remain, of which 212 are undetected doublets:

$$
\frac{212}{9{,}365} = 2.26\%.
$$

**What this buys.** Hashing four samples cut the contamination from 8.46% to **2.26%**, and no
amount of computational doublet detection changes the arithmetic of step 3 — a doublet of two
cells from the same sample with the same cell type is not distinguishable from a single cell by
any method, because there is nothing to distinguish. Pooling eight samples instead of four
would leave 1.14%. The lever is the number of samples pooled, not the software.

</WorkedExample>

## Counting molecules with random labels

The UMI solves amplification bias by replacing "how many reads" with "how many distinct
labels". That substitution is exact only if every molecule got a different label, and labels
are drawn at random, so it is not exact.

With $U = 4^k$ possible UMIs and $m$ molecules of one gene in one cell, each label is occupied
with probability $1 - (1 - 1/U)^m$, and the expected number of distinct labels observed is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{distinct}] = U\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{U}\right)^{m}\right)
\approx U\left(1 - e^{-m/U}\right).
$$

This is the coupon-collector count, and it is always less than $m$. Expanding the exponential
for $m \ll U$, the fractional undercount is

$$
1 - \frac{U(1 - e^{-m/U})}{m} \approx \frac{m}{2U}.
$$

A power law in $m$, with a coefficient that falls by a factor of four for every base added —
and therefore by **sixteen for every two bases**. That single fact settles barcode length. For
a gene present at 1,000 molecules in a cell, an 8-base UMI loses 0.76% of the count, a 10-base
UMI loses 0.048%, and a 12-base UMI loses 0.0030%.

## What a count is not

Three claims the matrix does not support, each of which is made routinely.

**It is not a concentration.** $c_{gj}$ scales with how much material was recovered from cell
*j*, and depth $n_j$ varies several-fold across cells within one run. A gene with count 5 in a
cell of depth 2,000 and count 5 in a cell of depth 20,000 is not equally expressed in the two.
Undoing this is the subject of the normalisation lesson, and it is not as simple as dividing.

**It is not comparable across genes.** Capture efficiency depends on transcript length, GC
content, secondary structure and how close the poly(A) tail is to the sequenced end. Two genes
with equal true abundance in the same cell do not give equal counts. Comparisons of one gene
across cells are meaningful; comparisons of two genes within a cell are not, without a model.

**A zero is not an absence.** This is the one that causes the most trouble, and the next lesson
is devoted to it. A gene expressed at a mean of 0.1 molecules recovered per cell is zero in over
90% of cells for no reason other than counting statistics. Calling those zeros "dropout" and
imputing them is, in the great majority of cases, inventing data to fill a hole that sampling
dug — and doing so before a differential-expression test is a well-documented way to
manufacture significance <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

## Summary

- A count matrix records molecules that **survived a physical process**, tagged and observed —
  not abundances. Dissociation, partitioning, capture, amplification, sequencing and counting
  each leave a signature, and later lessons undo them one at a time.
- Cells enter partitions at random, so occupancy is Poisson and the multiplet rate is
  $r(\lambda) = (1 - e^{-\lambda} - \lambda e^{-\lambda})/(1 - e^{-\lambda}) \approx \lambda/2$.
- Because $\lambda \approx R/D$, the doublet rate is **linear in cells recovered** with slope
  $1/(2D)$. The 0.8%-per-1,000 rule implies $D = 62{,}500$ effective partitions; it is exact at
  1,000 cells and optimistic beyond, giving 8.00% where the truth is 8.46% at 10,000 cells and
  16.00% where the truth is 18.05% at 20,000.
- Pooling $S$ hashed samples makes a fraction $(S-1)/S$ of doublets detectable. At 10,000 cells
  and four samples, 8.46% becomes 2.26% — a design lever, not a software one.
- Counting distinct random labels recovers $U(1 - e^{-m/U})$, not $m$, so the undercount is
  $m/(2U)$: divided by sixteen for every two bases added. At 1,000 molecules an 8-base UMI loses
  0.76% and a 10-base UMI loses 0.048%.
- A count is not a concentration, is not comparable across genes, and a zero is not an absence.

A run on the same device recovers 5,000 cells.

(a) What is the exact multiplet rate?

(b) $0.041111 \times 5{,}000 = 205.6$, so about **206 barcodes**.

(c) $4.1111\%/5 = 0.8222\%$ per 1,000 — already above the quoted 0.8%, because the curve is
convex and the rule is calibrated at 1,000 cells. The excess is small here and reaches 0.85%
per thousand by 10,000 cells.

<Exercise n={2} title="Superloading against separate lanes" difficulty="core">

A lab needs 20,000 cells from eight samples and is choosing between two designs: pool all eight,
hash them, and superload one lane to recover 20,000 cells; or run eight separate lanes
recovering 2,500 cells each.

(a) For the superloaded lane, compute the multiplet rate, the number of doublets, how many
hashing detects, and the doublet rate among the cells that survive filtering.

(b) For the eight-lane design, compute the multiplet rate per lane and the overall doublet rate
in the pooled result.

(c) Which design gives the cleaner matrix, and what does the cleaner one cost?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\lambda = -\ln(1 - 20{,}000/62{,}500) = -\ln(0.68) = 0.385662$, giving $r = 0.180467$, so
**18.05%** — 3,609 doublets. Hashing eight samples detects $7/8$ of them, or 3,158, leaving
451 among the 16,842 surviving barcodes:

$$
\frac{451}{16{,}842} = 2.68\%.
$$

(b) $\lambda = -\ln(1 - 2{,}500/62{,}500) = -\ln(0.96) = 0.040822$, giving $r = 0.020272$, so
**2.03%** per lane. Nothing is hashed and nothing is removed, so the pooled 20,000 cells carry
2.03% doublets.

(c) The eight-lane design is cleaner — 2.03% against 2.68% — but it consumes eight lanes of
reagent instead of one. Superloading with hashing gets within a percentage point of the
cost-no-object design at one-eighth the price, which is why it is standard. Note also that the
comparison would reverse in the lab's favour with more samples: pooling sixteen hashed samples
into the superloaded lane leaves $3{,}609/16 = 226$ undetected doublets among 16,616 survivors,
or 1.36%, beating the separate lanes outright.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Choosing a barcode length" difficulty="stretch">

A highly expressed gene is present at 5,000 molecules in a single cell.

(a) Compute the fractional undercount from UMI collision for an 8-base and a 10-base UMI.

(b) What is the shortest UMI that keeps the undercount below 0.1% for this gene?

(c) The undercount is worst in the most highly expressed genes. Argue briefly why this is a
mild problem for clustering but a real one for absolute quantification.

<div slot="solution">

(a) With $U = 4^8 = 65{,}536$, the expected distinct count is
$65{,}536(1 - e^{-5{,}000/65{,}536}) = 4{,}814.0$, an undercount of **3.72%**. With
$U = 4^{10} = 1{,}048{,}576$, it is $4{,}988.1$, an undercount of **0.24%**.

(b) Using $m/(2U) < 0.001$ we need $U > 2.5 \times 10^6$, and $4^{10} = 1.05 \times 10^6$ is too
small while $4^{11} = 4.19 \times 10^6$ suffices. Checking exactly at $k = 11$ gives an
undercount of **0.0596%**, so **11 bases**. (Ten bases gives 0.24%, over the budget.)

(c) Clustering depends on relative differences between cells, and the collision loss is a
deterministic function of $m$ alone — the same gene at the same abundance loses the same
fraction in every cell. A monotone, cell-independent compression of the top of the expression
range leaves the ordering of cells by that gene intact, so structure survives. Absolute
quantification does not have that protection: a claim that a cell contains 5,000 molecules is
wrong by 3.7% at 8 bases, and the error is systematic rather than noise, so averaging over more
cells does not remove it.

</div>

</Exercise>
