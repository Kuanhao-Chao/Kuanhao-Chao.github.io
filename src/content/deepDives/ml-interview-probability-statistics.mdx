## The mental model: populations, observations, and uncertainty

Probability starts with a process and reasons about possible data. Statistics starts with
observed data and reasons about the process. An interview answer becomes much clearer when
it says which distribution is random, what is conditioned on, and which assumptions make
the calculation valid.

<Callout variant="intuition">
Probabilities always live inside a **reference population and information set**. Conditioning
changes that population; Bayes' rule changes the information set; a sampling distribution
imagines repeating the data collection. Most interpretation errors come from silently
switching one of those three.
</Callout>

## Conditioning and Bayesian updating

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-conditional">

Conditional probability is

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.
$$

It asks for the fraction satisfying $A$ *within the restricted population satisfying $B$*.
The product rule follows: $P(A,B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$. Because the denominator changes,
$P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ can differ dramatically.

In ML, selection on $B$ may make a training statistic irrelevant at deployment. Conditioning
on a collider—a variable influenced by both $A$ and another cause—can even create an
association that was absent in the population. State what has been conditioned on before
calling a feature independent, unbiased, or predictive.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-bayes">

Bayes' rule is

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid\neg A)P(\neg A)}.
$$

The prior $P(A)$ is the base rate, the likelihood measures compatibility of evidence with
each state, and the denominator normalizes. In odds form, posterior odds equal prior odds
times the likelihood ratio. This formulation makes sequential evidence updates transparent
and traces back to Bayes' original inverse-probability argument <Citation id="bayes1763" />.

For rare events, false positives from the large negative population can outnumber true
positives. Ask for prevalence, sensitivity, and specificity before accepting “95% accurate.”
The same update underlies spam filtering, diagnosis, fraud review, and Bayesian parameter
inference.

</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample

Among 10,000 cases, expect 100 positives and 9,900 negatives. The detector flags
$0.90(100)=90$ true positives and $0.05(9{,}900)=495$ false positives. Therefore

$$
P(Y=1\mid +)=\frac{0.90(0.01)}{0.90(0.01)+0.05(0.99)}
=\frac{0.009}{0.0585}=0.1538.
$$

Only **15.4%** of alerts are true events. The detector is not contradictory: sensitivity
and specificity condition on the true class, while precision conditions on the alert. To
raise precision, improve specificity, enrich the reviewed population, or require additional
independent evidence.

</WorkedExample>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-independence">

Marginal independence means $p(a,b)=p(a)p(b)$. Conditional independence means
$p(a,b\mid c)=p(a\mid c)p(b\mid c)$ for relevant $c$. Neither implies the other. Two
symptoms can be associated because both share a disease, yet become independent once the
disease is known. Conversely, two independent causes become dependent after conditioning
on their common effect—a collider.

This distinction enables graphical models and naive Bayes. Naive Bayes assumes features are
conditionally independent given the class, not marginally independent. Test the claim in
the population where the model will operate; grouping, selection, and unobserved variables
can invalidate it.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Moments, distributions, and sampling

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-expectation">

Expectation is $\mathbb E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p(x)$ or
$\int g(x)p(x)dx$. Linearity,
$\mathbb E[aX+bY]=a\mathbb E[X]+b\mathbb E[Y]$, needs no independence. By contrast,
$\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]$ generally does.

For convex $g$, Jensen's inequality gives
$g(\mathbb E[X])\le\mathbb E[g(X)]$. This explains why average loss and loss at an average
prediction differ. Expectations may not exist for sufficiently heavy tails, and a sample
mean can be dominated by rare extremes. Always state which distribution owns the
expectation—training, test, posterior, or model-generated.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-variance-covariance">

$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)^2]$ measures spread in squared units.
$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$ measures joint linear variation;
correlation divides by $\sigma_X\sigma_Y$ to remove units. Covariance matrices are
positive semidefinite because $a^\top\Sigma a=\operatorname{Var}(a^\top X)\ge0$.

Independence implies zero covariance when moments exist, but zero covariance need not imply
independence: if $X$ is symmetric and $Y=X^2$, then covariance is zero although $Y$ is
determined by $X$. Correlation is also sensitive to outliers and can hide nonlinear or
group-specific relations.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-distributions">

Match support and generation mechanism. Bernoulli models one binary outcome; binomial counts
successes in fixed independent trials. Categorical and multinomial extend them to multiple
classes. Gaussian models additive continuous variation and is characterized by mean and
covariance. Poisson models counts under a rate and independence approximation; exponential
models memoryless waiting times. Beta and Dirichlet distributions live on probabilities and
probability vectors.

Do not choose from the variable's appearance alone. Count variance exceeding its mean
contradicts a basic Poisson; bounded scores contradict an untruncated Gaussian; repeated
events contradict independence. In neural networks, the output activation and loss jointly
encode a distributional assumption.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-lln-clt">

Under standard conditions, the law of large numbers says
$\bar X_n\to\mu$ as $n$ grows. It justifies sample averages as estimators. The central limit
theorem says
$\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma$ approaches a standard Gaussian, explaining the familiar
$\sigma/\sqrt n$ standard error.

The CLT describes fluctuations, not convergence itself. It can be poor for small samples,
rare events, heavy tails, or strong dependence. More data reduce sampling error but do not
remove bias, leakage, distribution shift, or measurement error. For clustered observations,
effective sample size may be much smaller than row count.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Estimation and uncertainty

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-mle">

Given observed $D$, maximum likelihood selects
$\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta p(D\mid\theta)$. For independent examples, the likelihood is a
product; log turns it into a sum and avoids underflow. Because log is monotone, the optimizer
is unchanged. Minimizing categorical cross-entropy is therefore MLE for a conditional
categorical model <Citation id="fisher1922likelihood" />.

MLE is an estimator, not a guarantee that the model is correct. It can be biased in finite
samples, fail to exist—for separable unregularized logistic regression—or overfit flexible
models. Report assumptions, constraints, uncertainty, and out-of-sample checks along with
the point estimate.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-map">

MAP chooses
$\hat\theta_{MAP}=\arg\max_\theta[\log p(D\mid\theta)+\log p(\theta)]$. A Gaussian prior
on parameters produces an L2-shaped penalty; a Laplace prior produces L1. The equivalence
depends on scale and parameterization, so “regularization is a prior” is a modeling
interpretation, not a universal identity.

MAP retains one posterior mode, ignores multimodality and posterior width, and changes under
nonlinear reparameterization. Full Bayesian prediction integrates
$p(y_*\mid x_*,D)=\int p(y_*\mid x_*,\theta)p(\theta\mid D)d\theta$. Use it when parameter
uncertainty matters and approximation cost is justified.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-intervals">

A 95% frequentist confidence interval is produced by a procedure that covers the fixed true
parameter in 95% of repeated compatible samples. After observing one interval, the
parameter is not random in that framework. A 95% Bayesian credible interval contains 95% of
posterior mass conditional on prior, likelihood, and data.

The numerical intervals can coincide in special cases but answer different questions.
Neither automatically handles misspecification, selection after looking at data, multiple
comparisons, or deployment shift. State the procedure, estimand, unit of resampling, and
assumptions rather than merely attaching “95% confidence.”

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-bootstrap">

The nonparametric bootstrap samples $n$ observed units with replacement, recomputes the
statistic, and uses the resulting empirical distribution for standard errors, bias, or
intervals <Citation id="efron1979bootstrap" />. It is powerful when analytic sampling
distributions are difficult.

Resample the independent unit: rows for IID data, clusters for grouped data, or blocks for
time dependence. Ordinary bootstrap can fail at boundaries, with tiny samples, extreme
tails, rare classes, nonsmooth statistics, or dependent observations. It reproduces the
empirical distribution, including its blind spots; it does not repair a biased sample.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Testing and decision errors

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-pvalue">

A p-value is $P_{H_0}(T\text{ at least as incompatible as }T_{obs})$ for a prespecified
statistic and null model. It conditions on $H_0$; it is not $P(H_0\mid D)$, not an effect
size, and not a replication probability. Optional stopping or testing many hypotheses
without adjustment changes its error behavior.

Good reporting includes the effect estimate, uncertainty interval, sample size, design, and
multiplicity handling. “Statistically significant” can be practically negligible with huge
$n$; a nonsignificant small study can still allow important effects. The test is a decision
procedure with assumptions, formalized through long-run error control
<Citation id="neymanpearson1933" />.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-errors-power">

Type I error rejects a true null, controlled at $\alpha$. Type II error fails to reject at a
specified alternative, with rate $\beta$; power is $1-\beta$. At fixed $n$ and effect size,
reducing $\alpha$ usually raises $\beta$. More samples, less noise, balanced allocation, or a
more efficient design can improve both.

Power is not a property of a test alone: it depends on effect size, variance, prevalence,
design, and alternative. In product experiments, distinguish statistical error from the
decision cost of false launch or missed improvement. Predefine a minimum practically
important effect rather than powering for any nonzero change.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Information and probabilistic predictions

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-entropy">

Shannon entropy is $H(p)=-\sum_xp(x)\log p(x)$, the expected surprise or optimal code length
under $p$ <Citation id="shannon1948information" />. Cross-entropy
$H(p,q)=-\sum_xp(x)\log q(x)$ is the expected code length when data follow $p$ but we encode
with $q$. Since $H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{KL}(p\parallel q)$, it is minimized at $q=p$.

For one-hot labels, empirical cross-entropy is $-\log q_y$, exactly negative conditional
log-likelihood. It penalizes confident wrong answers without bound. Label noise, class
weighting, and label smoothing change the effective target and often the calibration, so
explain the objective rather than saying it “measures classification error.”

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-kl">

$D_{KL}(p\parallel q)=\mathbb E_p[\log(p(X)/q(X))]\ge0$, with equality only when the
distributions agree almost everywhere. It measures extra coding cost or information lost
when $q$ approximates $p$ <Citation id="kullback1951information" />.

Direction matters. Forward KL heavily penalizes $q=0$ where $p>0$ and tends to cover modes;
reverse KL, common in variational inference, can prefer one mode because it averages under
$q$. KL is asymmetric and violates the triangle inequality, so it is not a metric. It can
also be infinite when supports mismatch.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-mutual-information">

Mutual information is
$I(X;Y)=D_{KL}(p(x,y)\parallel p(x)p(y))=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)$. It is zero exactly at
independence and detects nonlinear dependence. It is invariant under invertible
reparameterizations, unlike raw covariance.

Applications include feature selection, representation learning, and information gain.
The hard part is estimation: discretization is sensitive, density estimation deteriorates
in high dimensions, and neural lower bounds can be loose or high-variance. A high estimate
does not establish causality and may reflect an identifier or leakage.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-calibration">

A probabilistic classifier is calibrated if, among cases assigned probability near $p$, the
event occurs about fraction $p$. Reliability diagrams and calibration error summarize this;
Brier score and log loss are proper scores that jointly reward honest probability forecasts
<Citation id="gneiting2007scoring" />.

Calibration alone is insufficient: always predicting prevalence is calibrated in a stable
population but has no discrimination. Conversely, a ranking can have high AUC and poor
probabilities. Evaluate ranking, calibration, sharpness, and decision utility. Fit Platt,
isotonic, or temperature calibration on held-out data and recheck after shift.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-prob-monte-carlo">

For $\mu=\mathbb E_p[f(X)]$, draw $X_i\sim p$ and estimate
$\hat\mu=n^{-1}\sum_i f(X_i)$. With independent finite-variance samples,
$SE(\hat\mu)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)/n}$: halving error takes four times the samples.
This rate is dimension-insensitive in form, though variance can explode with dimension.

Importance sampling draws from $q$ and weights by $p/q$; it fails when $q$ misses important
mass. Markov-chain samples are correlated, reducing effective sample size. Report uncertainty,
multiple chains or seeds, and convergence diagnostics. A larger sample cannot rescue a
biased estimator or wrong target distribution.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="Update the odds" difficulty="warm-up">

A prior event probability is 0.20. Evidence has likelihood ratio 3. What is the posterior
probability?

<div slot="solution">

Prior odds are $0.20/0.80=0.25$. Posterior odds are $0.25\times3=0.75$. Convert back:
$0.75/(1+0.75)=0.4286$. The evidence raises probability from 20% to **42.9%**, not to 60%;
likelihood ratios multiply odds, not probabilities.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="A confidence statement" difficulty="core">

Rewrite this claim correctly: “There is a 95% probability that the fixed conversion-rate
difference lies in this frequentist confidence interval.”

<div slot="solution">

“The interval was produced by a procedure that, under its sampling assumptions, covers the
true fixed conversion-rate difference in 95% of repeated experiments.” If a direct 95%
probability statement about the parameter is wanted, specify a Bayesian model and prior and
report a posterior credible interval.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="KL is directional" difficulty="stretch">

Let $p=(0.5,0.5)$ and $q=(1,0)$. Compare $D_{KL}(p\parallel q)$ with
$D_{KL}(q\parallel p)$ using natural logs.

<div slot="solution">

$D_{KL}(p\parallel q)$ is infinite because $p$ assigns positive mass to the second outcome
where $q$ assigns zero. In the other direction,
$D_{KL}(q\parallel p)=1\log(1/0.5)+0=\log2\approx0.6931$. This support mismatch is the
clearest demonstration that KL is asymmetric.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Conditioning changes the reference population; Bayes' rule combines a base rate with
  evidence.
- LLN justifies averages, while CLT approximates their sampling fluctuations under stated
  conditions.
- MLE, MAP, and posterior integration answer different estimation questions.
- Confidence intervals, credible intervals, p-values, and power require distinct readings.
- Cross-entropy is negative log-likelihood; KL and mutual information quantify distributional
  mismatch and dependence.
- Calibration, ranking, sharpness, and decision utility are complementary—not interchangeable—
  properties of probabilistic predictions.
