## The mental model: transformations and sensitivity

An interview rarely rewards reciting a determinant formula. It rewards noticing that
`X @ W` cannot work, recognizing that a covariance matrix has lost rank, or explaining why
backpropagation uses one reverse sweep instead of one derivative calculation per parameter.

<Callout variant="intuition">
Treat **shapes as types**, a matrix as a machine that transforms directions, and a
derivative as a map from a small input change to a small output change. Then most formulas
can be reconstructed rather than memorized. SVD reveals what directions a matrix amplifies;
the chain rule composes sensitivity maps; conditioning tells us whether tiny uncertainty can
become a large answer.
</Callout>

## Vectors, matrices, and rank

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-shapes">

State the convention first: a batch $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ contains $n$
examples and $d$ features; weights $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$ produce
$\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$. The touching dimensions contract and
the outside dimensions remain.

This check catches more than illegal products. If a bias intended to be
$1\times k$ is accidentally $n\times1$, broadcasting may produce an $n\times k$ result
that runs without error but means something different. Name every axis—batch, time,
channel, feature—and test boundary cases such as batch size one. In code, prefer explicit
reshaping and assertions at interfaces over a chain of silent `squeeze` calls.

**Follow-up:** for attention, if $Q$ and $K$ are $b\times h\times t\times d_h$, then
$QK^\top$ is $b\times h\times t\times t$; the last two axes index query and key positions.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-dot-product">

Algebraically, $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}=\sum_j x_jw_j$ is a weighted sum. Geometrically,

$$
\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}=\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2\cos\theta.
$$

It is positive for aligned directions, zero for orthogonal ones, and negative for opposing
ones. A linear classifier compares this projection with a threshold. Cosine similarity
divides out both magnitudes, which is useful when direction represents semantics but harmful
when magnitude contains confidence or frequency. A dot product is basis-dependent unless
the feature scales and inner product have been defined; standardizing unrelated units can
therefore change the geometry deliberately.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-matrix-product">

The entry formula $(AB)_{ij}=\sum_r A_{ir}B_{rj}$ matters less than the composition:
$B$ transforms first and $A$ transforms its result. That explains why $AB\ne BA$ in
general and why shapes must line up. In a dense layer, every example row receives the same
linear transformation; in a stack, $XW_1W_2$ composes two linear maps.

Associativity permits $(XW_1)W_2=X(W_1W_2)$, but the computational cost differs. With no
nonlinearity, two dense layers collapse to one matrix; nonlinear activations are what add
expressive power. In sparse or batched code, choose an order that avoids materializing a
large intermediate, while remembering floating-point arithmetic makes the two orders only
approximately equal.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-rank">

Rank is the dimension of the column space, equivalently the number of nonzero singular
values. If $\operatorname{rank}(X)<d$, there is a nonzero vector $v$ with $Xv=0$:
moving parameters along $v$ cannot change predictions. Least squares may therefore have
many minimizers and $X^\top X$ is singular.

Causes include duplicate features, one-hot encoding all categories plus an intercept,
$d>n$, and near-collinearity. Near-rank deficiency is numerically more dangerous than exact
rank deficiency because a solve may return enormous, unstable coefficients. Diagnose with
singular values or a rank-revealing factorization; address it with feature redesign,
regularization, or a pseudoinverse—not by adding an unexplained constant to the diagonal.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-solve-inverse">

Writing $x=A^{-1}b$ is algebraically clean but is usually the wrong implementation. An LU,
QR, or Cholesky solve performs less work, stores less, and avoids rounding error introduced
by constructing every entry of $A^{-1}$. For symmetric positive-definite $A$, Cholesky is
both efficient and structure-aware; for least squares, QR or SVD avoids forming
$X^\top X$, whose condition number is squared.

Use an inverse only when the inverse itself is the required output—and even then consider a
factorization or linear operator. Libraries expose `solve`, `lstsq`, and triangular solves
for this reason. Always inspect residual $\lVert Ax-b\rVert$ and conditioning; a tiny
residual does not imply accurate parameters when the problem is ill-conditioned
<Citation id="golub2013matrix" />.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Spectral views: eigenvectors, SVD, and PCA

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-eigendecomposition">

An eigenpair satisfies $Av=\lambda v$: the map preserves the direction $v$ and scales it
by $\lambda$. A square matrix is diagonalizable only if it has enough independent
eigenvectors. The spectral theorem gives the especially useful case: every real symmetric
matrix has $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal $Q$ and real $\Lambda$.

Covariance matrices are symmetric positive semidefinite, so their eigenvalues are
nonnegative variances. The largest eigenvalue of a transition or adjacency operator can
govern long-run dynamics. Traps: eigenvectors are defined only up to sign; repeated
eigenvalues define an eigenspace rather than unique vectors; and a general nonsymmetric
matrix can have complex eigenpairs or be defective.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-svd">

Every $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ has $A=U\Sigma V^\top$. Each right singular vector
$v_i$ is an orthogonal input direction, $A$ maps it to $\sigma_i u_i$, and singular values
are nonnegative gains in descending order. Unlike eigendecomposition, SVD works for
rectangular and rank-deficient matrices.

The rank is the count of nonzero $\sigma_i$; the spectral norm is $\sigma_1$; and the
pseudoinverse reciprocates only nonzero singular values. Keeping the first $k$ terms gives
the best rank-$k$ approximation in Frobenius and spectral norm
<Citation id="eckart1936lowrank" />. The practical caveat is scale: randomized or truncated
methods are preferable when only leading components of a huge sparse matrix are needed.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-pca">

Center $X$ first. For a unit direction $v$, projected variance is
$v^\top(X^\top X/n)v$. The Rayleigh quotient is maximized by the top eigenvector of the
covariance matrix; later components maximize variance subject to orthogonality. If
$X=U\Sigma V^\top$, the columns of $V$ are those directions and variance explained by
component $i$ is proportional to $\sigma_i^2$ <Citation id="pearson1901pca" />
<Citation id="hotelling1933pca" />.

Equivalently, $X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top$ minimizes $\lVert X-X_k\rVert_F^2$ among rank-$k$
matrices. Scale features when units should not dominate, fit centering/scaling on training
data only, and do not claim components are causal or intrinsically interpretable. PCA is
linear and variance-seeking; low-variance directions can still predict the target.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-norms">

For a vector, $\lVert x\rVert_1=\sum_i|x_i|$ and
$\lVert x\rVert_2=(\sum_i x_i^2)^{1/2}$. L1 balls have corners, so an optimum touching a
constraint boundary often has exact zeros; L2 shrinks smoothly and is rotation-invariant.
For matrices, Frobenius norm treats all entries as one vector, whereas spectral norm
$\lVert A\rVert_2=\sigma_{\max}$ is the maximum factor by which $A$ stretches any vector.

The penalty, error metric, and operator norm are different uses. Squaring L2 makes a smooth
penalty but removes the norm's linear scaling. Norm choice encodes geometry: it should match
which perturbations or parameter structures matter, not be selected because “L1 is sparse,
L2 is stable” without checking feature scale and correlation.

</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample

The covariance is

$$
C=\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}2/3&2/3\\2/3&2/3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its normalized eigenvectors are $v_1=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt2$ and
$v_2=(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalues $4/3$ and $0$. Thus the data have rank one,
the first component explains $100\%$ of variance, and each point's first score is
$[-\sqrt2,0,\sqrt2]$. The zero eigenvalue is not “small noise”; here it is exact evidence
that the second feature adds no independent direction.

</WorkedExample>

## Derivatives and optimization geometry

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-conditioning">

For an invertible matrix, $\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$. Roughly, relative
input error can be amplified by up to this factor in a linear solve. Scaling a loss cannot
repair information lost because two columns are nearly identical.

Conditioning describes the mathematical problem. Stability describes whether an algorithm
solves a nearby problem accurately. A stable algorithm can still return an uncertain answer
to an ill-conditioned problem; an unstable algorithm can corrupt a well-conditioned one.
Use scaling or preconditioning, better parameterization, regularization, and stable
factorizations. Monitor singular values, residuals, and solution sensitivity—not just NaNs.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-derivatives">

If $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$, its gradient $\nabla_x f\in\mathbb{R}^d$ collects first
partial derivatives and points in steepest ascent under Euclidean geometry. If
$g:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^k$, its Jacobian $J_g\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$ maps a small
$dx$ to $dg\approx J_gdx$. A scalar function's Hessian
$H_f\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ maps $dx$ to the gradient change.

Declare numerator- or denominator-layout convention in matrix calculus; many “transpose
errors” are convention mismatches. In implementation, vector-Jacobian and
Jacobian-vector products are usually preferable to materializing a huge Jacobian.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-chain-rule">

For $L=g(f(x))$, $\nabla_xL=J_f(x)^\top\nabla_fL$. A computation graph applies this
locally: each node receives an upstream derivative, multiplies by its local derivative,
and sums contributions along multiple outgoing paths. Reverse-mode automatic
differentiation is efficient for one scalar loss and millions of parameters because one
reverse traversal obtains all parameter gradients <Citation id="rumelhart1986backprop" />.

Keep saved forward values until backward, avoid accidental graph detachment or in-place
mutation, and check custom operations with finite differences at several step sizes. A
gradient is a local sensitivity, not a finite causal effect; saturation can make it tiny
even when a feature is globally important.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-convexity">

A function is convex when
$f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)$ for $\theta\in[0,1]$. For a
differentiable convex function, every tangent plane is a global lower bound; therefore any
stationary point is globally optimal. Strict convexity gives at most one minimizer, if one
exists. Strong convexity additionally supplies a quadratic lower bound and enables sharper
rates <Citation id="boyd2004convex" />.

Convexity does not ensure a minimizer exists, make constraints convex, or guarantee a
poorly conditioned problem is fast. Neural-network objectives are nonconvex, while logistic
regression with an L2 penalty is convex. State the objective *and feasible set* before
claiming a guarantee.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-taylor">

Near $x$, a smooth scalar function satisfies

$$
f(x+\Delta)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top\Delta+
\tfrac12\Delta^\top H_f(x)\Delta.
$$

The first-order term motivates descent opposite the gradient. The quadratic term explains
curvature, Newton's $H^{-1}\nabla f$ step, sharp versus flat directions, and local
uncertainty approximations. The approximation is local: large steps, nonsmooth activations,
indefinite Hessians, or badly estimated curvature can invalidate it. Damped or trust-region
methods explicitly restrict how far the local model is trusted.

</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-mla-numerical-stability">

Floating point has finite range and precision. Stable softmax subtracts
$m=\max_i z_i$ before exponentiating; stable log-sum-exp uses
$m+\log\sum_i e^{z_i-m}$. Use `log1p` for $\log(1+x)$ near zero, avoid normal equations for
ill-conditioned least squares, scale inputs, and accumulate reductions in adequate
precision.

Debug systematically: reproduce with a tiny batch, locate the first nonfinite tensor,
inspect ranges and dtypes, verify algebraic invariants, and compare against a higher-
precision reference. Gradient clipping contains an explosion but does not explain it.
Mixed precision needs loss scaling and selected full-precision reductions. Numerical
correctness includes a plausible error bound, not merely a finite result.

</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="Shape a two-head attention score" difficulty="warm-up">

$Q$ and $K$ each have shape $32\times2\times128\times64$ for batch, heads, tokens, and
head width. What is the shape of $QK^\top$, and what factor usually scales it?

<div slot="solution">

Transpose only the final two axes of $K$, giving
$32\times2\times64\times128$. Contract the width 64, so the score tensor is
$32\times2\times128\times128$. Scale logits by $1/\sqrt{64}=1/8$ to control their variance
before softmax.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Diagnose a singular design" difficulty="core">

A regression uses an intercept and one-hot columns for red, green, and blue. Why is
$X^\top X$ singular, and what are two principled fixes?

<div slot="solution">

For every row, `red + green + blue = intercept`, so the columns are linearly dependent and
parameters are not identifiable. Drop one reference category, or remove the intercept and
interpret all three category coefficients directly. A regularizer can select a numerical
solution but does not make the original unregularized coefficients uniquely identifiable.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Differentiate softplus stably" difficulty="stretch">

For $f(x)=\log(1+e^x)$, derive $f'(x)$ and explain stable evaluation for large positive and
negative $x$.

<div slot="solution">

$f'(x)=e^x/(1+e^x)=\sigma(x)$. Computing $e^x$ directly overflows for large positive $x$.
Use $\max(x,0)+\log(1+e^{-|x|})$ for the value and a library's stable sigmoid for the
derivative. For large positive $x$, $f(x)\approx x$ and $f'(x)\approx1$; for large negative
$x$, both approach zero.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Shapes are executable dimensional analysis: contract matching axes and name every axis.
- Rank tells how many directions survive; SVD exposes those directions and their gains.
- PCA is both maximum-variance projection and minimum-error linear reconstruction.
- Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians are sensitivity maps with checkable dimensions.
- Backpropagation is reverse-mode chain rule; convexity supplies global guarantees only
  when its assumptions hold.
- Conditioning belongs to a problem, stability to an algorithm, and both belong in an ML
  correctness argument.
