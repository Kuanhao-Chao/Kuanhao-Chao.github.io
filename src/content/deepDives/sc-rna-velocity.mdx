## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson recovered an *ordering* and, from the cell density, the *durations*. What
it could not supply is a direction: an ordering read backwards is the same ordering, and
nothing in a similarity graph says which end is the start.

RNA velocity claims to supply it, from a real and elegant observation. A transcript is made
unspliced and becomes spliced, so when a gene is switching on the unspliced pool runs ahead of
the spliced one, and when it is switching off the unspliced pool empties first
<Citation id="lamanno2018velocity" />. The ratio of the two is a derivative, and derivatives
have signs.

This lesson works out what that sign actually depends on.

## The model, and what the sign reduces to

Two compartments and three rates:

$$
\frac{du}{dt} = \alpha - \beta u,
\qquad
\frac{ds}{dt} = \beta u - \gamma s.
$$

The second equation is the velocity of the spliced count, and it is what gets estimated. With
$\beta$ scaled to 1 — the usual convention, since only ratios of rates are identifiable —

$$
v = u - \gamma s.
$$

So $v > 0$, meaning the gene is being induced, exactly when

$$
\frac{u}{s} > \gamma.
$$

**The arrow is a comparison of one ratio against one number.** Everything else about the
gene — its expression level, its variance, how many cells express it — enters only through
that ratio. And $\gamma$ is not measured; it is fitted, classically from the slope of a
steady-state line through the extreme cells <Citation id="lamanno2018velocity" />, and in
later methods from a full kinetic model fitted per gene
<Citation id="bergen2020scvelo" /> or from a latent-variable model fitted jointly
<Citation id="gayoso2024velovi" />.

## How wrong can γ be, and what does that cost

Suppose the true rate is $\gamma$ and the estimate is $k\gamma$. Then every cell whose ratio
lies between the two changes sign: it was on one side of the threshold and is now on the other.
The cells outside that band keep their direction.

Taking $u/s$ as lognormal about $\gamma$ with log-standard-deviation $\sigma$ — a reasonable
description of a gene at steady state with cell-to-cell variability — the reversed fraction is

$$
\left|\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln k}{\sigma}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\right|.
$$

At $\sigma = 0.5$ and a two-fold error, that is **41.72%** of cells. Not 41.72% at random:
every one of them is called *repressing* when it is inducing, because the threshold moved one
way. A figure built from these arrows will show a coherent flow in the wrong direction.

The direction of the dependence on $\sigma$ is the part that catches people out. **A
tighter gene is more vulnerable, not less.** If every cell has nearly the same ratio, the whole
population sits just above or just below the threshold, and moving the threshold past it flips
all of them together: at $\sigma = 0.25$ a three-fold error reverses essentially every cell. A
gene with wide cell-to-cell variation keeps most of its cells far from the boundary, so a
modest error touches only the ones near it — 25.59% at $\sigma = 1.0$ against 49.72% at
$\sigma = 0.25$ for the same two-fold error.

That is the reverse of the usual intuition about noise, and it means the genes that look
cleanest are the ones whose arrows are least robust.

<WorkedExample
  title="What a two-fold error does to a figure"
  given={[
    'A gene whose u/s ratios are lognormal about the true γ',
    'Log-standard-deviation σ = 0.5',
    'γ estimated two-fold too high',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — where the threshold sits.** A cell is called inducing when $u/s > \gamma$. With the
estimate at $2\gamma$, the cells reclassified are exactly those with

$$
\gamma < \frac{u}{s} < 2\gamma.
$$

**Step 2 — how many cells that is.** On the log scale the band runs from 0 to $\ln 2 = 0.693147$
standard-deviations-worth, i.e. to $0.693147/0.5 = 1.386294$ standard deviations:

$$
\Phi(1.386294) - \Phi(0) = 0.917171 - 0.5 = 0.417171,
$$

so **41.72%** of the gene's cells reverse.

**Step 3 — the direction.** All of them the same way. The threshold moved up, so cells that
were above it and below $2\gamma$ are now below: every reversed cell goes from "inducing" to
"repressing". The figure does not become noisy; it becomes coherently wrong.

**Step 4 — the same error on a tighter and a looser gene.** At $\sigma = 0.25$ the same
two-fold error reverses **49.72%** — essentially the entire half of the distribution above
$\gamma$. At $\sigma = 1.0$ it reverses **25.59%**.

**Step 5 — what would be needed to rule it out.** An independent estimate of $\gamma$, which
means a measurement of degradation rate — metabolic labelling, a transcription block, anything
that observes the same cells twice. Absent that, the arrows are a consequence of the fit, and
the honest statement is conditional: *if* the fitted rates are right, the flow runs this way.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="intuition" title="What the model assumes that a snapshot cannot check">

Three assumptions carry the whole inference, and none is testable from the data being analysed.

**That β and γ are constant across cells.** They are fitted once per gene, so a cell type with
different splicing kinetics is being measured against another cell type's rates. This is the
assumption that fails most often in practice, and it fails hardest exactly where cell states
differ most — which is where the arrows are being read <Citation id="bergen2021challenges" />.

**That the extreme cells are at steady state.** The classical estimator fits the slope through
cells assumed to have reached equilibrium. Transient states are the interesting ones and are by
definition not at equilibrium; later methods drop the assumption by fitting full kinetics
instead <Citation id="bergen2020scvelo" />.

**That unspliced counts mean what they appear to.** Intronic reads come from pre-mRNA, but also
from intron retention, from internal priming, and from annotation choices about where a gene
ends. Two pipelines can produce materially different $u$ for the same library.

None of this makes velocity useless. It makes it a model-based inference whose conclusions
should be reported with the model, exactly as a phylogeny is reported with its substitution
model — not as an observation.

</Callout>

## Where this sits in the track

Velocity is the only tool in the curriculum that claims a *direction* from a snapshot, and it
is worth being clear about what the alternatives supply. A trajectory method supplies an
ordering and, through lesson 15's density argument, the relative durations — but not which end
is the beginning <Citation id="trapnell2014monocle" />
<Citation id="saelens2019trajectory" />. Velocity supplies the direction, conditional on the
fitted kinetics. A time course supplies both unconditionally, and is the only one of the three
that does.

The practical reading, then: use velocity to generate a directional hypothesis, state the
fitted $\gamma$ and the method that produced it, and check whether the conclusion survives a
plausible perturbation of it — which this lesson's formula makes a one-line calculation
<Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

## Summary

- Under the two-state model the spliced velocity is $v = u - \gamma s$, so with $\beta$ scaled
  to 1 a cell is called inducing exactly when $u/s > \gamma$. **The arrow is one ratio compared
  against one fitted number.**
- Misestimating $\gamma$ by a factor $k$ reverses every cell whose ratio lies between the true
  and estimated values — a fraction $|\Phi(\ln k/\sigma) - 1/2|$.
- At $\sigma = 0.5$, a **two-fold error reverses 41.72%** of cells, and all of them the same
  way. The figure becomes coherently wrong rather than noisy.
- **Tighter genes are more vulnerable**: 49.72% at $\sigma = 0.25$ against 25.59% at
  $\sigma = 1.0$ for the same error. The genes that look cleanest have the least robust arrows.
- Three untestable assumptions carry the inference: constant kinetics across cells, steady state
  in the cells used for fitting, and that intronic reads mean pre-mRNA.
- Velocity gives a direction conditional on a model. A time course gives one unconditionally.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading the sensitivity off the formula" difficulty="warm-up">

A gene has $\sigma = 0.5$.

(a) What fraction of cells reverse if $\gamma$ is 25% too high?

(b) What fraction if it is 50% too high?

(c) Compare with a two-fold error, and comment on the shape.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $k = 1.25$, so $\ln k/\sigma = 0.223144/0.5 = 0.446287$ and the fraction is
$\Phi(0.446287) - 0.5 = 0.1723$, or **17.23%**.

(b) $k = 1.5$ gives $\ln 1.5/0.5 = 0.810930$ and $\Phi(0.810930) - 0.5 = 0.2913$, or
**29.13%**.

(c) A two-fold error gives 41.72%. So the damage accumulates fast at first and then saturates
towards the ceiling of 50% — the first 25% of error already costs a sixth of the cells, and
doubling the error from 1.5× to 3× adds less than the first 1.5× did. There is no regime in
which a small error in $\gamma$ is harmless.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Two genes, one error" difficulty="core">

Two genes are fitted with the same relative error in $\gamma$, a factor of 2. Gene A has
$\sigma = 0.25$; gene B has $\sigma = 1.0$.

(a) Which gene has the more reproducible-looking scatter of u against s?

(b) Which loses more arrows to the error?

(c) Reconcile (a) and (b), and say what it implies for how genes should be chosen for a velocity
figure.

<div slot="solution">

(a) Gene A. A small $\sigma$ means the ratio $u/s$ is nearly the same in every cell, so the
points lie close to a line through the origin and the fit looks excellent.

(b) Gene A, and by a wide margin: **49.72%** against **25.59%**. Nearly every cell above the
true threshold in gene A lies below the inflated one, because they are all packed close to it.

(c) The apparent quality of the fit and the robustness of the direction run in opposite
directions here. A tight scatter means the cells carry little independent information about
where the threshold belongs — they are all saying the same thing — so the fitted $\gamma$ is
poorly constrained *and* an error in it moves everything at once. Choosing genes for a figure
by how clean their phase portrait looks therefore selects for exactly the genes whose arrows are
least trustworthy. Selecting on variability in $u/s$, or reporting the sensitivity alongside the
arrows, would be the honest alternative.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="What a claim of direction requires" difficulty="stretch">

A paper reports that velocity arrows show a population differentiating from state X into state
Y, and that this reverses a previously published ordering.

(a) What is the minimum the paper must report for the claim to be checkable?

(b) The authors note their arrows are consistent across three velocity methods. How much does
that establish?

(c) Propose one experiment that would settle it, and say what it costs relative to the
sequencing already done.

<div slot="solution">

(a) The fitted $\gamma$ per gene, the method and its assumptions, and the distribution of $u/s$
per gene — from which a reader can compute the reversal fraction for any perturbation of
$\gamma$ using this lesson's formula. Without those, the arrows are unfalsifiable.

(b) Less than it appears. The three methods differ in how they fit kinetics, but all of them
compare a ratio against a rate estimated from the same counts, and all inherit the same three
assumptions — constant kinetics across cells, steady state where it is assumed, and intronic
reads meaning pre-mRNA. Agreement rules out an implementation error and does not address a
shared modelling assumption. If a cell type genuinely splices faster, every method will be
wrong about it in the same direction.

(c) Metabolic labelling: a pulse of a modified nucleotide marks newly made transcripts, so new
and old can be separated in the same cells and the direction read without fitting $\gamma$ at
all. Relative to the sequencing already done it is a modest addition — the same library
preparation with one extra step and a chemical conversion — and it converts a model-conditional
claim into an observation. A time course does the same and is simpler still where the system
can be sampled repeatedly.

</div>

</Exercise>
