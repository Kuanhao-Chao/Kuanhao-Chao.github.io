## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson treated one trait at a time. Nothing in nature or in a breeding
programme does.

An allele that increases growth rate may also delay reproduction. A locus that raises milk
yield may lower fertility. When the same alleles affect two traits — through pleiotropy, or
because they sit close enough together to be inherited as a unit — the traits acquire a
**genetic correlation**, and the consequence is immediate and often unwelcome:

> **A trait can respond to selection it was never selected on, and it can respond in the
> wrong direction.**

This is not an exotic case. It is the ordinary situation, and it is why dairy fertility
declined for two decades while the industry selected hard on yield, and why a
single-trait breeder's equation applied trait by trait gives systematically wrong answers.

## The correlated response

Selection on trait $x$ produces a response in trait $y$:

$$
CR_y = i\,h_x\,h_y\,r_g\,\sigma_{P_y}
$$

Compare this with the single-trait $R = i\,h^2\,\sigma_P$ and one difference stands out: the
correlated response uses **$h$, not $h^2$**, and takes one $h$ from each trait.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why one h from each trait">

Read the formula as a path the selection has to travel.

Selection acts on the **phenotype** of $x$, but only the part of that phenotype which is
breeding value gets transmitted. Getting from the phenotype of $x$ into the breeding value
of $x$ costs a factor of $h_x$ — that is what a correlation between phenotype and breeding
value *is*.

Then $r_g$ carries it across from the breeding value of $x$ to the breeding value of $y$.

Finally it has to come back **out** into the phenotype of $y$ to be measured, which costs a
factor of $h_y$.

So the journey is phenotype → breeding value → breeding value → phenotype, and it picks up
$h_x \cdot r_g \cdot h_y$ on the way. The single-trait case is the same journey with $x = y$
and $r_g = 1$, giving $h \cdot 1 \cdot h = h^2$ — which is where the square in the ordinary
breeder's equation comes from. It was never a square; it was always two separate factors
that happened to be equal.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample
  title="Selecting on yield, losing fertility"
  given={[
    { label: 'Selected fraction', value: 'top 10%' },
    { label: 'Heritabilities', value: 'yield 0.30, fertility 0.05' },
    { label: 'Genetic correlation', value: 'r_g = −0.35' },
    { label: 'σ_P for fertility', value: '10 units' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the pieces.** At the top 10%, $i = 1.754983$. The two square roots are

$$
h_{\text{yield}} = \sqrt{0.30} = 0.547723, \qquad h_{\text{fert}} = \sqrt{0.05} = 0.223607
$$

**Step 2 — the correlated response.**

$$
CR = 1.754983 \times 0.547723 \times 0.223607 \times (-0.35) \times 10 = -0.752292
$$

Fertility falls by 0.752292 units per generation, and **nobody selected on fertility at
all**. The programme selected on yield; the genetic correlation did the rest.

**Step 3 — put it in context.** If the same intensity were applied directly to fertility,
the response would be $R = i\,h^2\,\sigma_P = 1.754983 \times 0.05 \times 10 = 0.877492$
units per generation upward.

So the damage done by ignoring the correlation is comparable in size to the progress that
could be made by attending to it. Over twenty generations the two differ by roughly 32
units of fertility — which is not an abstraction; it is the reason dairy fertility fell for
two decades and why modern selection indices carry fertility explicitly.

</WorkedExample>

## The G matrix, and why the response is not parallel to the selection

With more than two traits the bookkeeping needs matrices.
<Citation id="lande1979multivariate" /> gave the generalisation, and
<Citation id="lande1983selection" /> the form that is used:

$$
\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

The step that repays attention is $\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{s}$.

The **selection differential** $\mathbf{s}$ is what you observe: how much the selected
parents differ from the population, trait by trait. But if two traits are phenotypically
correlated, selecting on one *automatically* shifts the observed mean of the other, whether
or not anyone cared about it. $\mathbf{s}$ therefore mixes direct selection with the
by-product of selection on everything else.

The **selection gradient** $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ strips that out. It is the vector of partial
regression coefficients of fitness on each trait, holding the others constant — the *direct*
selection. Multiplying by $\mathbf{P}^{-1}$ is what performs the holding-constant.

<WorkedExample
  title="Direct selection pushes a trait down; it goes up"
  given={[
    { label: 'G', value: '[[40, 20], [20, 30]]' },
    { label: 'P', value: '[[100, 30], [30, 80]]' },
    { label: 's', value: '(10, 0) — selection applied to trait 1 only' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the selection gradient.** $\det\mathbf{P} = 100(80) - 30(30) = 7100$, so

$$
\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{s}
= \frac{1}{7100}\begin{pmatrix} 80 & -30 \\ -30 & 100 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.112676 \\ -0.042254 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Look at the second entry. Nobody selected on trait 2 — $s_2 = 0$ — and yet the *direct*
selection on it is **negative**. That is what the observed $s_2 = 0$ actually means: trait 2
is phenotypically correlated with trait 1, so if there were no direct selection against it,
its mean would have risen. Holding it flat required pushing it down.

**Step 2 — the response.**

$$
\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\beta}
= \begin{pmatrix} 40 & 20 \\ 20 & 30 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0.112676 \\ -0.042254 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 3.661972 \\ 0.985915 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 3 — read the second entry again.** Direct selection on trait 2 was $-0.042254$, and
trait 2 **increased** by $0.985915$. The genetic covariance of $+20$ dragged it up with
trait 1, and it more than overcame the direct selection against it.

Selection pushed one way and the trait went the other. Nothing is paradoxical here — $G$
simply does not commute with anybody's intentions — but it means that a trait's observed
trajectory says very little on its own about what was being selected.

</WorkedExample>

## Genetic constraint: the line of least resistance

$\mathbf{G}$ has eigenvectors, and the leading one, $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$, is the direction in
which the population carries the most additive genetic variance. In the example it lies at
38.0° and accounts for **79.5%** of the total genetic variance across the two traits.

That concentration has a consequence <Citation id="schluter1996leastresistance" /> made
precise: populations evolve most readily along $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$, and among related
species the directions of divergence tend to align with it. Selection in a direction where
$\mathbf{G}$ has little variance produces almost no response, however hard it is applied.

Two cautions follow. <Citation id="blows2005limits" /> points out that with many traits,
$\mathbf{G}$ is often close to singular in some directions — there are combinations of
traits with essentially **no** additive variance at all, and selection along them is simply
refused. And <Citation id="kirkpatrick2009dimensions" /> makes the dimensional point: the
number of directions in which a population can actually respond is usually far smaller than
the number of traits measured, so counting traits overstates the room to manoeuvre.

<Callout variant="warning" title="A genetic correlation is a property of a population, not a law">

$r_g$ is built from $\mathbf{G}$, and $\mathbf{G}$ is built from allele frequencies. Both
change. A correlation of $-0.35$ today is not a permanent trade-off; it is a statement about
the alleles currently segregating.

Two consequences. Selection itself erodes the correlation, because it preferentially fixes
the pleiotropic alleles responsible for it — long-term selection experiments routinely break
correlations that looked structural. And a correlation estimated in one population does not
transfer to another with different allele frequencies.

Sampling makes this worse. <Citation id="robertson1959correlation" /> derived the sampling
variance of $r_g$, and it is large: genetic correlations need far more data than
heritabilities to pin down, and a literature value quoted without a standard error should
be treated as a hypothesis rather than a measurement.

</Callout>

## G×E: the same trait in two environments is two traits

The final idea in this module is a change of viewpoint, and it is due to
<Citation id="falconer1952environment" />. A trait measured in environment A and the same
trait measured in environment B are **not one trait** — they are two traits, connected by a
genetic correlation.

If $r_g = 1$ the environments rank genotypes identically and nothing is lost by ignoring the
distinction. If $r_g < 1$ they do not, and selecting in one environment delivers only a
correlated response in the other — which the formula above already prices, with $h_A$,
$h_B$ and $r_g$ in their usual places.

A rule of thumb widely used in animal breeding and generally attributed to Robertson puts
the practical threshold at $r_g \approx 0.8$: below it, the two environments are different
enough that selecting separately in each is worth the cost of running two programmes.

<Citation id="via1985plasticity" /> takes the same machinery in the evolutionary direction,
treating the **reaction norm** — the function mapping environment to phenotype — as the
thing under selection, which is how plasticity itself becomes a trait that can evolve.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="When is indirect selection better?" difficulty="warm-up">

Trait $x$ has $h^2_x = 0.4$; trait $y$, which you actually care about, has $h^2_y = 0.2$ and
$\sigma_{P_y} = 5$. They are correlated at $r_g = 0.6$. Selecting the top 20%,
(a) compute the correlated response in $y$ from selecting on $x$, (b) compare it with
selecting on $y$ directly, (c) derive the general criterion.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** With $i = 1.399810$, $h_x = 0.632456$ and $h_y = 0.447214$:

$$
CR_y = 1.399810 \times 0.632456 \times 0.447214 \times 0.6 \times 5 = 1.187778
$$

**(b)** Direct selection on $y$ gives
$R_y = i\,h^2_y\,\sigma_{P_y} = 1.399810 \times 0.2 \times 5 = 1.399810$. The indirect route
delivers 84.9% of the direct one, so here direct selection wins.

**(c)** Dividing one by the other, everything cancels except

$$
\frac{CR_y}{R_y} = \frac{i h_x h_y r_g \sigma_{P_y}}{i h_y^2 \sigma_{P_y}} = r_g\,\frac{h_x}{h_y}
$$

which is $0.6 \times 0.632456/0.447214 = 0.848528$, matching (b) exactly. **Indirect
selection beats direct when $r_g h_x > h_y$** — here the break-even correlation is
$h_y/h_x = 0.707107$.

The criterion is why indirect selection is used at all. It wins when the correlated trait is
much more heritable, or much cheaper or faster to measure — a juvenile trait standing in for
an adult one, or a marker panel standing in for a slaughter measurement. The formula has no
term for cost, so the arithmetic is only half the decision.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Selecting on both traits" difficulty="core">

Using the same $\mathbf{G}$ and $\mathbf{P}$, compute $\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ for
$\mathbf{s} = (10, 5)$ and for $\mathbf{s} = (10, -5)$. Compare both with the worked
example's $(10, 0)$ and say what changed.

<div slot="solution">

| $\mathbf{s}$ | $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ | $\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(10, 0)$ | $(0.112676,\ -0.042254)$ | $(3.661972,\ 0.985915)$ |
| $(10, 5)$ | $(0.091549,\ 0.028169)$ | $(4.225352,\ 2.676056)$ |
| $(10, -5)$ | $(0.133803,\ -0.112676)$ | $(3.098592,\ -0.704225)$ |

**What changed.** Adding modest positive selection on trait 2 more than doubles its response
— and *also* increases trait 1's response, from 3.661972 to 4.225352, even though the
selection on trait 1 is unchanged. That is the genetic covariance working in the helpful
direction for once.

The third row is the important one. To make trait 2 actually decline, the programme has to
select against it hard enough to overcome the covariance: at $s_2 = -5$ the response finally
turns negative, at $-0.704225$. **The sign reversal in the worked example is not
inevitable** — it happens when direct opposition is too weak to beat the pull of the
correlated trait, and the whole point of an index is to choose $\mathbf{s}$ knowing that.

Note also that trait 1's response *falls* in the third row, to 3.098592. Fighting the
correlation costs progress on the trait you wanted. There is no arrangement of these numbers
in which the correlation is free.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="One programme or two?" difficulty="stretch">

A trait is measured in two environments with $h^2_A = 0.35$ and $h^2_B = 0.25$, and the
cross-environment genetic correlation is $r_g = 0.373877$, the value in Figure 2. With
$\sigma_{P_B} = 6$ and selection at the top 20%: (a) how much progress in environment B does
selecting in A deliver, against selecting in B directly? (b) What does the rule of thumb
say? (c) What would make you decide otherwise?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** With $h_A = 0.591608$ and $h_B = 0.5$:

$$
CR_B = 1.399810 \times 0.591608 \times 0.5 \times 0.373877 \times 6 = 0.928866
$$

against a direct response of $R_B = 1.399810 \times 0.25 \times 6 = 2.099714$. The ratio is
$0.442377$, which is $r_g h_A/h_B$ again — selecting in A buys **44%** of the progress that
selecting in B would.

**(b)** $r_g = 0.3739$ is far below the 0.8 threshold, so the rule of thumb says the two
environments are effectively different traits and warrant separate programmes.

**(c)** Three things would change the decision, and none of them is in the formula.

*Cost.* Two programmes cost roughly twice as much and each runs at half the size, which
lowers selection intensity and raises inbreeding in both. A 44% response at full scale can
beat a 100% response at half scale.

*The precision of $r_g$ itself.* By Robertson's result the sampling variance of a genetic
correlation is large. If 0.3739 carries a standard error of 0.15, the decision is being made
on an estimate that is consistent with anything from 0.08 to 0.67 — and the honest first
move is to estimate it better, not to reorganise the programme.

*Which environment you are actually selling into.* If environment B is 90% of the market,
the comparison is not between one programme and two but between selecting in the wrong
environment and selecting in the right one.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A genetic correlation makes traits respond to selection they never received.**
  $CR_y = i\,h_x\,h_y\,r_g\,\sigma_{P_y}$, with one $h$ from each trait because the selection
  has to travel from a phenotype into a breeding value, across, and back out.
- **Selecting on yield with $r_g = -0.35$ costs 0.752292 units of fertility a generation**,
  comparable to what selecting on fertility directly would gain.
- **$\mathbf{s}$ is what you observe; $\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{s}$ is the
  direct selection.** A trait under no observed selection can be under substantial direct
  selection, and vice versa.
- **The response is $\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and is not parallel to
  $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.** In the worked example direct selection on trait 2 is negative and
  the trait rises by 0.985915 anyway. It takes $s_2 = -5$ before it actually declines.
- **Populations evolve along $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$**, which here carries 79.5% of the genetic
  variance. Directions in which $\mathbf{G}$ is near-singular refuse selection however hard
  it is applied.
- **A trait in two environments is two traits.** Non-parallel reaction norms are what make
  $r_g < 1$; rank changes are the form that matters, and below $r_g \approx 0.8$ the two
  environments are usually worth separate programmes — subject to cost, to how precisely
  $r_g$ is known, and to which environment you are breeding for.
