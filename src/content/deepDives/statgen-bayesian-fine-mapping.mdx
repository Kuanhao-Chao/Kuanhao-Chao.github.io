## The question this lesson answers

A genome-wide significant hit identifies a **region**, not a variant. The lesson on linkage
disequilibrium explains why: the causal site and every variant correlated with it produce
nearly the same marginal statistic, and at $r^2 = 0.99$ the difference between their
$z$-scores is noise.

So the output of a GWAS is a peak, and the question fine-mapping answers is: given the
peak, **how should belief be distributed over the variants inside it?**

That is a Bayesian question, and it has three parts. A likelihood ratio for each variant
against the null. A prior over which variant is causal — including, crucially, the
possibility that **none of them is**. And a summary of the resulting posterior that a
biologist can act on.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why ranking by p-value does not work here">

The obvious thing to do with a peak is take the variant with the smallest $p$-value and call
it causal. It is worth being precise about why that fails, because the failure is not that
the top variant is *usually* wrong — it is that the ranking carries almost no information.

Two variants in LD at $r^2 = 0.99$ have $z$-scores that differ by roughly the noise in a
single study. Which of them comes out on top is close to a coin flip, and re-running the same
analysis in a different sample of the same population would often reverse it. A ranking whose
order is not reproducible is not a ranking.

What *is* stable is the group. The causal variant and its near-perfect tags are collectively
supported by the data every time, even though their internal order is not. So the honest
output is a **set** with a probability attached, not a winner.

That is what makes this a Bayesian problem rather than a testing problem. A $p$-value asks
whether a variant could be null; here every variant in the peak clears that easily by virtue
of its neighbours. The question is how belief should be *divided* among them, which requires
a prior — and once you have written down a prior over which variant is causal, you have to
decide what to do about the possibility that none of them is.

</Callout>

## Wakefield's approximate Bayes factor

Computing a Bayes factor properly needs the full likelihood.
<Citation id="wakefield2008abf" /> showed that a normal approximation gets you almost all
the way there from summary statistics alone — the $z$-score and the standard error:

$$
\mathrm{BF}_{01} = \sqrt{\frac{V+W}{V}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{z^2}{2}\cdot\frac{W}{V+W}\right)
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="This curriculum writes Bayes factors as BF₀₁, always">

The formula above is the evidence for the **null over the alternative**. Smaller means more
evidence that the variant has an effect — which reads backwards the first time and is worth
stating explicitly, because the alternative convention is equally common.

$\mathrm{BF}_{10}$ is the exact reciprocal, $1/\mathrm{BF}_{01}$. Both are correct; using
both without saying which is which is how two write-ups end up appearing to contradict each
other over the same locus. This curriculum fixes $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ throughout, and where a
result is conventionally stated the other way — colocalisation is the case below — the
reciprocal is taken explicitly rather than silently.

The parameter $W$ is a genuine prior and it does affect the answer: 0.04 corresponds to
believing the true effect is within about $\pm 0.4$ standard deviations, which is generous
for a complex trait. A $W$ chosen much too large penalises the alternative for being vague
and shrinks every PIP.

</Callout>

## Posterior inclusion probabilities, and the null nobody includes

Convert the Bayes factors into posterior probabilities with a prior over which variant is
causal. Writing it in the $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ direction, the term for variant $j$ is
$\pi_j / \mathrm{BF}_{01,j}$, and

$$
\mathrm{PIP}_j = \frac{\pi_j / \mathrm{BF}_{01,j}}{\pi_0 + \sum_k \pi_k / \mathrm{BF}_{01,k}}
$$

The $\pi_0$ in the denominator is the prior probability that **no variant in the region is
causal**, and it is the term most write-ups drop.

<WorkedExample
  title="Fine-mapping a locus of eight variants"
  given={[

  ]}

**Step 1 — the Bayes factors.** With $W = 0.04$, the peak variant at $z = 6.5$ gives

$$
\mathrm{BF}_{01} = \sqrt{\frac{0.04004762}{0.00004762}}\;
\exp\!\left(-\frac{42.25}{2}\cdot 0.998811\right) = 1.990\times10^{-8}
$$

Tiny, meaning overwhelming evidence *against* the null — which is what $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$
being small means.

**Step 2 — the posterior probabilities.**

$$
\mathrm{PIP} = (0.000000,\; 0.000021,\; 0.114882,\; 0.770194,\; 0.114882,\; 0.000021,\; 0.000000,\; 0.000000)
$$

The causal variant takes 77%, and its two closest tags take 11.5% each. The remaining five
variants take essentially nothing.

**Step 3 — the credible set.** The smallest set whose PIPs reach 0.95 is
$v_3$, $v_4$ and $v_5$, with coverage $0.99996$. Two variants would give only
$0.770194 + 0.114882 = 0.885076$, short of the threshold, so three are needed.

**Step 4 — the purity.** The smallest pairwise $|r|$ inside that set is $0.7000$. That is
the honest summary *at this sample size*: the data localise the signal to three variants and
no further. More data does help — every $z$ grows as $\sqrt{N}$ while the LD between the
variants does not, so this same locus resolves to a **single-variant** credible set by
$N \approx 95{,}500$. What sample size cannot fix is $|r| = 1$: two variants in perfect LD
have identical likelihoods, and no quantity of data separates them. Moving that limit rather
than approaching it takes recombination, or a population whose LD structure differs.

**Step 5 — note what the PIPs sum to.** Here they sum to $1.000000$, and $\pi_0$ appears to
have done nothing. That is correct: when the evidence is this strong, $\pi_0 = 0.05$ is
negligible against the enormous $\sum_k \pi_k/\mathrm{BF}_{01,k}$. The next section is where
it earns its place.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Drop π₀ and the method cannot say 'nothing here'">

Set $\pi_0 = 0$ and the denominator becomes $\sum_k \pi_k/\mathrm{BF}_{01,k}$ alone, so the
PIPs are forced to sum to one. That is not a normalisation convenience — it is the assertion
that **some variant among those tested is definitely causal**.

At a locus with a real signal the assertion is harmless. At a locus without one it is
exactly wrong, and it is where confident credible sets around noise come from.

Take eight variants whose largest $z$ is 1.7 — no signal at all. With $\pi_0 = 0.05$ the
PIPs sum to $0.576378$, no 95 percent set can be formed, and the method correctly declines
to name a causal variant. Drop $\pi_0$ and the same PIPs are renormalised to sum to
$1.000000$; a "95 percent credible set" now exists, contains all eight variants, and has a
**purity of 0.0100** — a set of essentially uncorrelated variants, which is the signature of
a set assembled from noise rather than from LD.

That is why purity is reported alongside coverage, and why <Citation id="wang2020susie" />
discards sets below 0.5. A high-coverage, low-purity set is not a weak result; it is a sign
that the model has grouped unrelated things.

</Callout>

## Beyond one causal variant

The construction above assumes exactly one causal variant in the region. Real loci often have
more, and a single-causal model handles a second signal badly — it produces a credible set
straddling both, with low purity.

<Citation id="benner2016finemap" /> searches over configurations of multiple causal variants
directly. <Citation id="wang2020susie" /> takes a different route: it represents the locus as
a **sum of single effects**, fitting one single-causal model per signal and iterating, which
yields one credible set per signal with a purity attached to each. That decomposition is why
SuSiE's output is a *list* of sets rather than one set, and why a locus can legitimately
return a tight set for one signal and a useless one for another.

Priors need not be uniform either. <Citation id="weissbrod2020polyfun" /> uses functional
annotations — the same stratified-LDSC machinery from <Citation id="finucane2015sldsc" />
and <Citation id="finucane2018celltype" /> — to set $\pi_j$ per variant, so a variant in a
conserved enhancer starts with more prior mass than one in unannotated intergenic sequence.
That sharpens fine-mapping when the annotations are right and biases it when they are not,
which is the same trade every informative prior makes.

## Colocalisation: is it the *same* variant?

A GWAS peak and an expression QTL peak in the same region raise an obvious hypothesis: the
variant that changes the gene's expression is the variant that changes the trait. It is
obvious and frequently wrong, because at a locus dense with variants two independent signals
land near each other often.

<Citation id="giambartolomei2014coloc" /> formalises the question as five hypotheses:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **H₀** | no causal variant for either trait |
| **H₁** | a causal variant for trait 1 only |
| **H₂** | a causal variant for trait 2 only |
| **H₃** | causal variants for both, but **different** ones |
| **H₄** | a **shared** causal variant |

H₃ and H₄ are the pair that matters, and the difference between them is whether a gene is
implicated or merely nearby.

<WorkedExample
  title="Four loci, four answers"
  given={[
    { label: 'GWAS peak', value: 'z = 2.1 … 6.5 … 1.8 across eight variants' },
    { label: 'Priors', value: 'p₁ = p₂ = 10⁻⁴, p₁₂ = 10⁻⁵' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

Colocalisation is conventionally computed from Bayes factors in the $\mathrm{BF}_{10}$
direction, so the $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ values above are inverted first —
$\mathrm{BF}_{10} = 1/\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ — which is exactly the kind of bridge the fixed
convention exists to make visible rather than silent.

| second trait | PP₀ | PP₁ | PP₂ | PP₃ | PP₄ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| same peak, weaker | 0.0000 | 0.0001 | 0.0000 | 0.0008 | **0.9991** |
| peak shifted three variants | 0.0000 | 0.0003 | 0.0002 | **0.9947** | 0.0048 |
| no signal | 0.0002 | **0.9940** | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.0058 |
| no signal in either | **0.9999** | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 |

**Step 1 — read row two against row one.** Both second traits have a strong signal in the
same region. Shifting where the peak sits moves the answer from PP₄ = 0.9991 to
PP₃ = 0.9947 — from "the same variant does both" to "two different variants, close
together". A test that only asked *is there a signal in both* would report the same thing
for both rows.

**Step 2 — check the prior that carries the argument.** $p_{12}$ is the per-variant prior
that a variant is causal for both traits, and it is doing more work than the data in
ambiguous cases. On row one, varying it over two orders of magnitude gives

$$
p_{12} = 10^{-6} \Rightarrow \mathrm{PP}_4 = 0.9910,
\quad
10^{-5} \Rightarrow 0.9991,
\quad
10^{-4} \Rightarrow 0.9999
$$

Here the conclusion is robust. It is not always, and a colocalisation reported without a
sensitivity analysis over $p_{12}$ has not been checked.

</WorkedExample>

<Citation id="hormozdiari2016ecaviar" /> takes an alternative route that allows multiple
causal variants per trait, which matters because coloc's single-causal assumption can turn a
genuine shared signal into H₃ when a second independent signal is present. The
expression-QTL lesson in the Genomic Data track covers what the QTL side of this requires.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading a Bayes factor in the right direction" difficulty="warm-up">

A variant has $\mathrm{BF}_{01} = 2.5\times10^{-6}$. (a) What does that say? (b) What is
the corresponding $\mathrm{BF}_{10}$? (c) A second variant at the same locus has
$\mathrm{BF}_{01} = 3.2$. What does that say?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Strong evidence **against** the null — the data are 400,000 times more likely under
an effect than under no effect. Small $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ means a likely causal variant, which
is the direction that needs stating because it inverts the usual intuition about large Bayes
factors.

**(b)** $\mathrm{BF}_{10} = 1/(2.5\times10^{-6}) = 400{,}000$.

**(c)** $\mathrm{BF}_{01} = 3.2$ means the data are 3.2 times *more* likely under the null,
so this variant is mild evidence for no effect. In a fine-mapping calculation it will
receive a PIP near zero — and note that it contributes $\pi_j/3.2$ to the denominator, a
small positive amount, so it is not ignored, merely outvoted.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="A credible set that should not be trusted" difficulty="core">

A fine-mapping run returns a 95% credible set of 14 variants with coverage 0.961 and purity
0.08. (a) What does the purity tell you? (b) Give two explanations. (c) What would
distinguish them?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** That some pair of variants in the set has $|r| = 0.08$ — essentially uncorrelated.
A credible set is supposed to be the group of variants the data cannot distinguish, and
variants that uncorrelated *are* distinguishable. Something has gone wrong.

**(b)** Two explanations. Either **there is no real signal**, and the set is an artefact of
renormalising probabilities across a locus with nothing in it — which is what dropping
$\pi_0$ produces, and the worked example's null locus had purity 0.0100. Or **there are
multiple causal variants**, and a single-causal model has smeared one set across two
independent signals.

**(c)** Look at the strongest $z$-score in the region. If nothing approaches genome-wide
significance, the first explanation is likely and the honest output is no set at all. If
there are two clearly separated peaks, the second is likely, and refitting with a
multiple-causal method — SuSiE or FINEMAP — should return two sets, each with acceptable
purity.

The general rule: **coverage without purity is not a result.** Coverage can be manufactured
by the prior; purity cannot.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A colocalisation that proves less than claimed" difficulty="stretch">

A paper reports PP₄ = 0.82 for a GWAS locus and an eQTL for gene X, and concludes gene X
mediates the association. (a) What would you check first? (b) The GWAS locus has two
independent signals. Why does that matter? (c) What would make the claim convincing?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The sensitivity of PP₄ to $p_{12}$. At PP₄ = 0.82 the result is not overwhelming,
and $p_{12}$ is a prior that is rarely justified from data. If PP₄ falls below 0.5 at
$p_{12} = 10^{-6}$ and rises above 0.95 at $10^{-4}$, the conclusion is a restatement of the
prior. Also check PP₃: if PP₃ is 0.15 the two hypotheses are not well separated.

**(b)** Because coloc assumes **at most one causal variant per trait per region**. With two
independent GWAS signals, one colocalising and one not, the single-causal model is misspecified
— and the usual failure is that it dilutes the shared signal, pushing probability toward H₃.
A PP₄ of 0.82 under a misspecified model could be understating or overstating; the direction
is not predictable, which is worse than a known bias. eCAVIAR or a SuSiE-based colocalisation
handles multiple signals directly.

**(c)** Three things. Colocalisation redone allowing multiple causal variants, per signal.
Replication in an independent eQTL dataset, ideally in the tissue the disease acts in rather
than whichever tissue had the largest sample. And direct evidence that the variant changes
the gene's expression — a reporter assay or a base edit — because colocalisation is a
statement about statistical coincidence, not about mechanism.

Even at PP₄ = 0.99, colocalisation says the same variant is causal for both traits. It does
not say the trait effect is *mediated by* that gene's expression: the variant may affect
both independently, and the direction of mediation is not identified by the coloc model at
all.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A significant locus names a region, not a variant.** Fine-mapping distributes posterior
  probability across the variants in it, from summary statistics alone.
- **Wakefield's approximate Bayes factor** needs only $z$, $V$ and a prior variance $W$.
  This curriculum writes it as $\mathrm{BF}_{01}$ — evidence for the null — so **small means
  a likely causal variant**, and $\mathrm{BF}_{10}$ is its exact reciprocal.
- **π₀ is not optional.** With it, the PIPs at a null locus sum to 0.5764 and no 95% set can
  be formed. Without it they are forced to 1.0000, and the method returns a set of all eight
  variants with a purity of 0.0100 — a confident-looking answer assembled from noise.
- **Purity is the diagnostic, coverage is not.** The worked example's real set has coverage
  0.99996 and purity 0.7000: three variants the data genuinely cannot separate. Sets below
  0.5 purity are discarded.
- **The resolution limit is LD, not sample size.** More people will not split variants at
  $r = 0.95$; only recombination or a different LD structure will.
- **Colocalisation asks whether it is the same variant**, not whether both traits have a
  signal. Shifting a second trait's peak by three variants moves the answer from
  PP₄ = 0.9991 to PP₃ = 0.9947.
- **p₁₂ carries the colocalisation argument**, and a result reported without a sensitivity
  analysis over it has not been checked. Nor does PP₄ establish mediation — only that one
  variant is causal for both.
