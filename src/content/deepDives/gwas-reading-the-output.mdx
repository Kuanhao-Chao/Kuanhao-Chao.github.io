## The question this lesson answers

Ten million rows came back. Which ones are real, and are the numbers next to them right?

Two different failures live here. The first is the threshold: pick it wrong and you either
publish noise or miss everything. The second is subtler and more common — the loci that
*are* real come with effect estimates that are systematically too large, and the papers
reporting them almost never say so.

<Callout variant="intuition">

The threshold is not a statement about biology or about your study. It is a statement about
how many questions you asked. Testing a million effectively independent positions and asking
for a 5% chance of *any* false positive across all of them gives $0.05/10^6 = 5 \times
10^{-8}$, and the number has held up because the effective count is set by human LD structure
rather than by array density <Citation id="peer2008threshold" /><Citation id="dudbridge2008threshold" />
— adding more variants to a chip does not add independent tests.

</Callout>

## Why not control the false discovery rate?

<WorkedExample
  title="The same scan under Bonferroni and under Benjamini-Hochberg"
  given={[
    { label: 'Effective tests', value: 'm = 1,000,000' },
    { label: 'FWER', value: 'α = 0.05 → 5 × 10⁻⁸' },
    { label: 'FDR', value: 'q = 0.05' },
    { label: 'Top p-values', value: '1.2e-12, 8.0e-10, 3.1e-9, 4.4e-8, 9.7e-8, 2.2e-7, 8.0e-7, 3.5e-6' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"

**Step 1 — Bonferroni.** The cutoff is $\alpha/m = 5 \times 10^{-8}$ and does not depend on
what the scan found. Four of the eight p-values clear it.

**Step 2 — Benjamini-Hochberg.** Sort the p-values and compare each against a *moving*
bound <Citation id="benjamini1995fdr" />: reject the $k$ smallest, where $k$ is the largest
index with

$$
p_{(k)} \leq \frac{k}{m}\,q .
$$

| k | p₍ₖ₎ | bound (k/m)q | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1.2e-12 | 5.0e-8 | pass |
| 2 | 8.0e-10 | 1.0e-7 | pass |
| 3 | 3.1e-9 | 1.5e-7 | pass |
| 4 | 4.4e-8 | 2.0e-7 | pass |
| 5 | 9.7e-8 | 2.5e-7 | pass |
| 6 | 2.2e-7 | 3.0e-7 | pass |
| 7 | 8.0e-7 | 3.5e-7 | fail |
| 8 | 3.5e-6 | 4.0e-7 | fail |

BH rejects **6**, with an effective threshold of $2.2 \times 10^{-7}$, against Bonferroni's
4. It found two more loci and expects $0.05 \times 6 = 0.30$ of its six to be false.

**Step 3 — now scale it up, which is where the argument actually is.** The BH bound is
proportional to $k$, so the threshold a scan ends up with depends on how much signal it has:

| discoveries | BH threshold | versus Bonferroni | expected false |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | 3.0e-7 | 6× looser | 0.3 |
| 500 | 2.5e-5 | 500× looser | 25.0 |
| 5,000 | 2.5e-4 | 5,000× looser | 250.0 |

For a highly polygenic trait — which is most of them
<Citation id="visscher2017gwas" /> — FDR control at $q = 0.05$ hands back a catalogue in
which **twenty-five or two hundred and fifty entries are expected to be wrong**, with no
indication of which.

**Step 4 — so which is right?** It depends entirely on what happens next. If the output
feeds a polygenic score, where thousands of weights are summed and a few wrong ones wash
out, FDR is the better choice and using $5 \times 10^{-8}$ throws away real predictive
signal. If the output is a list of genes for laboratory follow-up, each false entry costs
years, and family-wise control is worth its power. The published GWAS convention serves the
second case, which is why score-building pipelines routinely and correctly ignore it.

</WorkedExample>

## The effect estimate is too big

A locus enters the catalogue because its statistic crossed a threshold. Statistics fluctuate,
so among loci with a true effect near the threshold, the ones that got in are
disproportionately those that fluctuated *upward*. The published estimate is therefore biased,
and the bias has a name and a formula <Citation id="zollner2007winners" />.

For a locus whose true effect is exactly at the genome-wide threshold, $z = 5.4513$, the
expected published value conditional on discovery is $6.2492$ — inflated by **14.64%**. For a
locus whose true effect is *below* the threshold at $z = 5.0$, and which therefore only
appears at all when it fluctuates up, the expectation is $6.1057$, inflated by 22.11%.

The inflation collapses as the true effect grows: at $z = 7$ it is 1.83%, and at $z = 9$ it
is 0.01%. So the curse is concentrated exactly where new discoveries are — the marginal
loci in every fresh scan — and absent from the handful of large-effect loci everyone already
knows about.

<Callout variant="warning" title="What this predicts, and what it explains">

Two consequences follow, and both are routinely misread.

**Replication will show smaller effects.** Not because the original was wrong, and not
because the replication cohort differs, but because the original was conditioned on
discovery and the replication is not. A replication that recovers 70% of the discovery
effect at a marginal locus is a *successful* replication.

**A polygenic score built on raw discovery effects will over-predict** in training and
under-perform out of sample, because every weight near the threshold is inflated. Correcting
for it <Citation id="palmer2017winners" /> is one of the reasons shrinkage methods
out-perform raw effect sizes, a point the score lesson takes up in detail.

</Callout>

## Reading the two plots

**The Manhattan plot** shows position against $-\log_{10} p$. It answers one question well —
*where* are the signals — and is routinely asked two it cannot answer. A tall peak is not a
strong effect; it is a small p-value, which mixes effect size with allele frequency and
sample size. And a wide peak is not a large region of causal variation; it is one signal
spread across everything in LD with it, which is the subject of the next two lessons.

**The QQ plot** shows observed against expected quantiles, and is the more informative of the
two — but not in the way it is usually read. The folk rule is that early departure across the
whole distribution means inflation while tail-only departure means real signal. That rule is
false in exactly the regime this track cares about. Under
$\mathbb{E}[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + (Nh^2/M)\ell_j$, a polygenic trait lifts **every** statistic
in proportion to how much genome the variant tags: at $N = 10^5$, $h^2 = 0.3$ and
$M = 10^6$, a variant with an LD score of 50 already expects $\chi^2 = 2.5$. A clean,
well-powered scan of a polygenic trait therefore leaves the diagonal early, bulk and all —
which is the same fact that makes $\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}$ useless, since $\lambda$ *is* the
median of that bulk.

So curve shape alone cannot separate confounding from polygenicity, and the diagnostic is the
LD score regression intercept, the subject of
[Population Structure](/deep_dives/gwas-population-structure/)
<Citation id="devlin1999gc" />. What a flat bulk with a tail-only departure actually indicates
is a *sparse* architecture or a study too small to lift the bulk yet — informative, but not
what "real signal" looks like for a typical complex trait.

Neither plot is a substitute for the catalogue <Citation id="sollis2023gwascatalog" />: the
question "has this locus been seen before?" is answered by looking, not by squinting at a
figure.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A scan with two hits" difficulty="warm-up">

A scan of $m = 10^6$ effective tests returns exactly two p-values below $10^{-6}$:
$3.0 \times 10^{-9}$ and $6.0 \times 10^{-8}$.

(a) Which clear Bonferroni at $\alpha = 0.05$?

(b) Which clear BH at $q = 0.05$?

(c) Explain why the answers differ by so little here.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Only the first. The cutoff is $5 \times 10^{-8}$, and $6.0 \times 10^{-8}$ is above
it.

**(b)** Both. The bounds are $1 \times 5 \times 10^{-8} = 5.0 \times 10^{-8}$ for the
smallest and $2 \times 5 \times 10^{-8} = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ for the second;
$3.0 \times 10^{-9} \leq 5.0 \times 10^{-8}$ and $6.0 \times 10^{-8} \leq 1.0 \times 10^{-7}$,
so $k = 2$.

**(c)** Because BH's advantage grows with the number of discoveries and there are two. The
threshold it reaches is $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$, twice Bonferroni's — and it expects
$0.05 \times 2 = 0.1$ false discoveries, which is a tenth of a locus. When a scan finds
almost nothing the two procedures nearly agree, which is worth knowing because it is the
regime in which people most often reach for FDR hoping for rescue.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="How much of the catalogue is wrong?" difficulty="core">

A consortium reports 1,200 loci at $q = 0.05$ using BH, on $m = 10^6$ effective tests.

(a) What p-value threshold did that correspond to?

(b) How many of the 1,200 are expected to be false?

(c) The consortium proposes following up all 1,200 in the laboratory at roughly two years and
one postdoc each. Comment.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $(1200/10^6) \times 0.05 = 6.0 \times 10^{-5}$ — **1,200 times looser** than
$5 \times 10^{-8}$.

**(b)** $0.05 \times 1200 = 60$.

**(c)** Sixty of the follow-ups are expected to be chasing nothing, at a cost of roughly 120
postdoc-years. That is the concrete meaning of "controlling the false discovery rate at 5%",
and it is why the convention for a *discovery catalogue* is family-wise control. If the
consortium's real purpose is a polygenic score, the 1,200 are fine and the follow-up plan is
the problem — the two uses want different thresholds from the same scan, and there is no
reason to report only one.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A replication that looks like a failure" difficulty="stretch">

A discovery cohort reports a locus at $z = 5.60$. An independent replication of the same size
returns $z = 4.30$, which is not genome-wide significant, and a reviewer calls the original a
false positive.

(a) What does the winner's curse predict the replication should see?

(b) Is the reviewer right?

(c) What analysis would settle it?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** If the true effect is near the discovery threshold, the published 5.60 is inflated.
Working backwards, a true $z$ of about 5 produces an expected published value of 6.1057, and
a true $z$ at the threshold of 5.4513 produces 6.2492 — so a discovery reported at 5.60
is consistent with a true effect somewhere below 5. A same-sized replication, which is *not*
conditioned on discovery, should then return something near that true value, and 4.30 is
entirely compatible.

**(b)** No — or at least, the evidence offered does not support the claim. "Failed to reach
genome-wide significance in replication" is the expected outcome for a true marginal locus,
because the replication has to clear the same bar without the upward fluctuation that got the
discovery over it. The reviewer is applying a discovery threshold to a replication, where the
relevant question is whether the effect is in the same direction and of a compatible
magnitude.

**(c)** A fixed-effects meta-analysis of the two cohorts, testing the combined estimate
against the threshold once, plus a heterogeneity test to check the two are estimating the
same thing. If the combined estimate clears and the heterogeneity does not, the locus stands
and the replication succeeded. Reporting a de-biased discovery estimate
<Citation id="palmer2017winners" /> alongside the raw one would have prevented the argument.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **The threshold counts questions, not variants.** $5 \times 10^{-8}$ is Bonferroni over
  about $10^6$ effectively independent tests, and denser arrays do not change it because LD
  sets the effective count.
- **BH's threshold moves with the signal**, at $(k/m)q$. Six discoveries make it 6× looser
  than Bonferroni; five hundred make it 500× looser and expect 25 to be false.
- **Which control is right depends on what happens next.** Family-wise for a catalogue that
  will be followed up in a laboratory; false-discovery for a score that sums thousands of
  weights. The same scan can honestly report both.
- **Discovery effect estimates are biased upward**, by 14.64% for a locus at exactly the
  threshold and 22.11% for one just below it, falling to almost nothing by $z = 7$.
- **A smaller effect on replication is the expected result**, not a failure — and the same
  bias is why raw discovery weights make poor polygenic scores.
- **A Manhattan peak's height is not effect size and its width is not a region.** The QQ plot
  is the more informative of the two, and the catalogue answers the novelty question that
  neither plot can.
