## The question this lesson answers

Quality control is where a GWAS is won or lost, and it is the part most often run as a
recipe. The published protocols <Citation id="anderson2010qc" /><Citation id="laurie2010qc" />
give the thresholds; the tools <Citation id="purcell2007plink" /><Citation id="chang2015plink2" />
implement them in a few commands. What the recipe does not say is that every filter is a
trade, and that the sample-level filters are charged against the study's power at a rate
that depends on which arm the sample came from.

<Callout variant="intuition">

Every quality-control filter is protecting the analysis against **a correlation between a
technical artefact and the phenotype**. That is the whole subject. A variant that fails in
5% of samples is harmless if the failures are scattered at random; it manufactures a
genome-wide significant hit if the failures are concentrated in cases. Nothing else about a
filter matters as much as whether the thing it removes is associated with the outcome.

</Callout>

## The passes, in an order that works

Order matters because the passes interact, and running them in the wrong sequence lets one
undo another.

**1 — Variant-level, first pass, loose.** Remove variants with very low call rate before
computing anything per-sample, because a handful of catastrophic variants will otherwise make
every sample look bad.

**2 — Sample-level.** Call rate, then heterozygosity, then sex concordance, then relatedness.
Each is cheap to compute and expensive to get wrong.

**3 — Variant-level, second pass, strict.** Call rate again on the surviving samples,
Hardy-Weinberg in controls, minor allele frequency, and differential missingness between
arms.

**4 — Population structure.** Which is a large enough subject to be the next lesson.

Re-running step 3 after step 2 is the part pipelines skip. Removing bad samples changes every
variant's call rate, and a variant that failed only in the samples you just removed should
come back.

## Relatedness, and which member to drop

Cryptic relatedness inflates association statistics because related samples are not
independent observations <Citation id="astle2009structure" />. The standard remedy is to
break every pair above a threshold by removing one member. What the remedy does not specify
is *which* member — and by the arithmetic of the design lesson, that choice is worth sixteen
times the cost of the pruning itself.

**Step 1 — from IBD probabilities to one number.** Relationship inference
<Citation id="manichaikul2010king" /> returns, for each pair, the probability of sharing 0, 1
or 2 alleles IBD. The summary is

$$
\widehat{\Pi} = P(\mathrm{IBD}=2) + \tfrac{1}{2}P(\mathrm{IBD}=1) .
$$

| pair | z₀ | z₁ | z₂ | PI_HAT | reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 0.00 | 0.00 | 1.00 | 1.0000 | duplicate or MZ twin |
| B | 0.25 | 0.50 | 0.25 | 0.5000 | full siblings |
| C | 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.00 | 0.2500 | second degree |
| D | 0.75 | 0.25 | 0.00 | 0.1250 | first cousins |

Pairs B and C are above the cut and must be broken. Pair D is below it and stays. Pair A is
not a relationship at all — a PI_HAT of 1 between two samples with different identifiers
means the same person was genotyped twice, or two samples were swapped, and it is a data
problem rather than a pedigree.

**Step 2 — why 0.185.** The classes above are not points on a continuum; they are the only
values a standard pedigree produces. The gap between second-degree relatives at $1/4$ and
first cousins at $1/8$ is the widest one, and $0.185$ sits inside it — close to its midpoint
of $0.1875$. That is the entire justification. It is not calibrated against a false-positive
rate, and moving it to 0.2 or 0.15 changes nothing, because nothing is expected to land in
between.

**Step 3 — the bill.** Four hundred pairs must be broken, so 400 samples leave. If every one
is a case:

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{1/9{,}600 + 1/40{,}000} = 30{,}967.74 ,
$$

a loss of $1{,}032.26$. If every one is a control:

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{1/10{,}000 + 1/39{,}600} = 31{,}935.48 ,
$$

a loss of $64.52$. **The same 400 people cost sixteen times more from the case arm** — the
same factor the design lesson found, and for the same reason: the derivative of a harmonic
mean is much steeper in its smaller argument.

**Step 4 — so choose.** Where a related pair contains one case and one control, drop the
control. Where it contains two controls, drop either. Where it contains two cases, you have
lost a case whatever you do. A pipeline that removes whichever sample has the lower call
rate — the common default — is optimising the wrong quantity, and on a 4:1 study it will
throw away roughly the arm ratio's worth of power for no reason.

</WorkedExample>

## The filters that catch artefacts, not people

**Heterozygosity.** An unusually *low* autosomal heterozygosity is inbreeding; an unusually
*high* one is contamination, because mixing two people's DNA manufactures heterozygotes
neither of them carries. Written as an inbreeding coefficient,

$$
F = \frac{O - E}{N - E} ,
$$

contamination shows up as $F$ going negative. The filter is conventionally a few standard
deviations either side of the cohort mean, which means it is defined relative to the batch —
a genuinely inbred cohort will flag half its samples if the bound is copied from a protocol
written for another population.

**Sex concordance.** The same statistic on the X chromosome separates the sexes cleanly:
males are hemizygous, so $F \approx 1$; females sit near 0. A sample landing between them is
a swap, a contamination, or a real sex-chromosome aneuploidy, and the three are
distinguishable only by looking at the Y and at the intensity data. A mismatch against the
recorded sex is the single most reliable indicator that sample identifiers have been mixed
up, which is why it is worth running even in a study that will never analyse the X.

**Hardy-Weinberg, in controls only.** A variant far from Hardy-Weinberg proportions is
usually a genotyping failure — a cluster plot the calling algorithm split wrongly. But a
*real* association also pushes cases away from equilibrium, so filtering on HWE across the
whole cohort removes true positives. Test in controls, apply the filter to both. This is the
one QC step where the conventional recipe and the naive reading of the statistic point in
opposite directions.

**Differential missingness.** The filter that catches what the others miss. A variant called
in 99% of controls and 97% of cases is not a missing-data problem; it is a variant whose
genotypes are associated with case status through the assay rather than through biology, and
it will produce a clean-looking association. Test the difference in call rate between arms
directly and remove the variants that fail, regardless of how good their overall call rate
looks.

<Callout variant="warning" title="QC thresholds are population-specific">

Every numeric threshold above was calibrated on cohorts of predominantly European ancestry,
and several of them are not transferable. Heterozygosity bounds depend on the population's
history; Hardy-Weinberg deviations arise from structure as well as from bad calling; and
because imputation quality is lower for cohorts poorly matched to the reference panel, a
fixed variant-level filter removes more variants from exactly those cohorts
<Citation id="martin2019" />. Thresholds inherited from a protocol are a starting point, not
a standard, and the haplotype structure they assume <Citation id="hapmap2005" /> is not
universal.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading three pairs" difficulty="warm-up">

Compute PI_HAT and classify:

(a) $z_0 = 0.00,\; z_1 = 1.00,\; z_2 = 0.00$

(b) $z_0 = 0.60,\; z_1 = 0.35,\; z_2 = 0.05$

(c) $z_0 = 0.25,\; z_1 = 0.50,\; z_2 = 0.25$

Which of (a) and (c) would a PI_HAT threshold distinguish, and what would?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\widehat{\Pi} = 0.00 + 0.5(1.00) = 0.5000$ — parent-offspring.

**(b)** $\widehat{\Pi} = 0.05 + 0.5(0.35) = 0.2250$. Above the cut, so one member goes. It
does not sit cleanly on a class, which usually means a more distant or a mixed relationship,
or noise in the estimate.

**(c)** $\widehat{\Pi} = 0.25 + 0.5(0.50) = 0.5000$ — full siblings.

**Neither PI_HAT distinguishes (a) from (c)**: both are first-degree relationships and both
give exactly 0.5. What separates them is $z_0$. A parent and child share at least one allele
IBD at *every* locus, so $z_0 = 0$ exactly; full siblings share none at about a quarter of
loci. Any tool reporting only the summary throws that away, which is why relationship
inference reports all three probabilities.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Pruning a balanced study" difficulty="core">

Repeat the worked example's step 3 for a **balanced** study: 25,000 cases and 25,000
controls, breaking 400 pairs.

(a) What does dropping 400 cases cost? What does dropping 400 controls cost?

(b) Explain the difference from the 4:1 study.

(c) What does this say about when the "which member?" question is worth engineering effort?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Before pruning, $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 50{,}000$. Dropping 400 from either arm gives
$4/(1/24{,}600 + 1/25{,}000) = 49{,}596.77$, a loss of $403.23$ — **identical either way**,
because the study is symmetric in its two arms.

**(b)** In the 4:1 study the arms are far apart, and a harmonic mean is far more sensitive to
its smaller argument, so the two choices differ by a factor of sixteen. At 1:1 the derivative
with respect to each arm is the same and the choice is worth nothing. The factor is
$(N_{\mathrm{controls}}/N_{\mathrm{cases}})^2$: at 4:1 that is 16, at 1:1 it is 1.

**(c)** Only when the study is unbalanced — and the more unbalanced, the more it matters. A
1:1 study can let the pipeline choose by call rate and lose nothing. A 10:1 study is throwing
away a hundredfold difference by doing the same, and should choose deliberately.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A variant that passes every filter" difficulty="stretch">

A variant has an overall call rate of 98.5%, sits at Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in controls
($p = 0.4$), has a minor allele frequency of 12%, and an imputation INFO of 0.91. It comes
out at $p = 3 \times 10^{-9}$.

(a) Which standard filter has it passed that it should not have?

(b) The call rate is 99.4% in controls and 96.1% in cases. Is that a large difference?

(c) What would you check before believing the association, and what would convince you?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** None of them tests what matters here. Overall call rate, HWE in controls, MAF and
INFO are all computed **without reference to phenotype**, so none of them can see a
difference *between arms*. The variant passes every listed filter precisely because the
listed filters are blind to the failure mode.

**(b)** It is enormous. The missingness differs by 3.3 percentage points, and — this is the
point — the missing genotypes are not missing at random with respect to genotype, because
assays fail preferentially on particular clusters. A systematic difference between arms in
*which* genotypes fail to call produces an allele-frequency difference between arms that has
nothing to do with the phenotype. A missingness test between cases and controls would return
a p-value far smaller than the association itself.

**(c)** First, the differential-missingness test — if it fails, stop. Second, the cluster
plots for that variant in each arm; a variant whose calling failed asymmetrically usually
looks obviously wrong to a human eye and passes every automated threshold. Third,
genotyping batch against case status, since if cases and controls were genotyped in different
batches every variant is at risk and this one is just the most visible. What would convince
me is replication in a cohort where cases and controls were assayed together, which is the
only design that removes the confound rather than testing for it.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Every filter is protecting against a correlation between a technical artefact and the
  phenotype.** A failure scattered at random is harmless; the same failure concentrated in
  one arm manufactures associations.
- **Order the passes so they do not undo one another**, and re-run the variant filters after
  removing samples — a variant that failed only in the samples you just dropped should come
  back.
- **PI_HAT is a set of expected values with gaps**, not a smooth score. The 0.185 cut is the
  midpoint of the widest gap, between $1/4$ and $1/8$, and moving it within that gap changes
  nothing.
- **Relatedness pruning is a decision, not a step.** Breaking 400 pairs in a 4:1 study costs
  1,032 effective samples from the case arm and 65 from the control arm — a factor of
  $(N_{\mathrm{controls}}/N_{\mathrm{cases}})^2 = 16$, which vanishes at 1:1 and grows with
  imbalance.
- **Test Hardy-Weinberg in controls only**, because a real association pushes cases away from
  equilibrium and filtering on the whole cohort removes true positives.
- **Differential missingness is the filter the others cannot replace**, because every other
  variant-level filter is computed without reference to phenotype and is therefore blind to
  the one failure mode that produces false associations.
