## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson gave the breeder's equation, which says what happens *if* you select the
right parents. It said nothing about how to find them, and the practical problem is harder
than it looks for one reason: **the individuals you most want to rank usually have no
record**.

A young bull has no milk yield and never will. A seedling has no harvest. In both cases the
decision has to be made now, and the only evidence is what relatives have done. Worse, those
relatives were themselves measured in different herds, different years and different
management systems, so their records are not comparable until those effects are removed —
and removing them requires knowing the genetic merit you are trying to estimate.

BLUP solves both problems at once, in one system of equations. It is not a clever heuristic;
it is the best linear unbiased predictor in a precise sense, and <Citation id="henderson1975blup" />
is where the properties are established. What made it *usable* was a computational trick,
and that trick is the centre of this lesson.

## The mixed model, and the trick that made it computable

Write the records as a sum of what you must remove and what you want to estimate:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{e},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{A}\sigma^2_g,
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \mathbf{I}\sigma^2_e
$$

The fixed effects $\mathbf{b}$ are nuisances to be estimated and discarded; the random
effects $\mathbf{u}$ are the breeding values, and they are *random* precisely because they
have a distribution the pedigree specifies — that is the whole content of the previous
lesson's relationship matrix.

The direct route is generalised least squares, which needs
$\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\sigma^2_g + \mathbf{I}\sigma^2_e$
inverted. $\mathbf{V}$ is $n \times n$ in the number of *records*, which for a national dairy
evaluation is tens of millions, and it is dense. That computation has never been possible and
never will be.

Henderson's insight was that $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ both fall out of a
single joint system that never mentions $\mathbf{V}$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z} \\
\mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{A}^{-1}\lambda
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{b}} \\ \hat{\mathbf{u}} \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}\end{pmatrix}
$$

The matrix that does appear is $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$, and by
<Citation id="henderson1976inverse" /> that can be written down directly from the pedigree
without forming $\mathbf{A}$ — and it is extremely sparse, with non-zeros only among parents
and offspring. **The dense inverse was replaced by a sparse one that never needs to be
computed.** That is why national genetic evaluations exist.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why BLUP shrinks, and why that is not a compromise">

The $\lambda$ added to the diagonal is doing something specific, and calling it
"regularisation" undersells it.

An animal's record is its breeding value plus environment plus non-additive genetics. Taking
the record at face value would credit all of that to the animal. Shrinking it toward the mean
by the right amount is not caution — it is the **conditional expectation** of the breeding
value given the data, which is the estimate that minimises squared error. BLUP is the
posterior mean under a normal prior, and $\lambda$ is what converts prior variance into
shrinkage.

The size of the shrinkage is set by how much evidence there is. One record on the animal
itself shrinks a lot; fifty daughters shrink very little. The same equation handles both
without any special-casing, because the amount of information enters through
$\mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}$ and the relationship structure enters through
$\mathbf{A}^{-1}$.

And because $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ connects every individual to its relatives, the system produces
an estimate for an animal with **no record at all** — its equation has zero on the left from
$\mathbf{Z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}$ and is driven entirely by its relatives. That is the property
the whole method exists for.

</Callout>

**Step 1 — the relationship matrix.** Calves 4 and 5 are full sibs, so $A_{45} = 0.5$; calf 6
shares only the dam, so $A_{46} = A_{56} = 0.25$. Each sire is related to its own offspring at
0.5 and to the other sire's offspring at 0.

**Step 2 — set up the system.** With one fixed effect (the mean), $\mathbf{X}$ is a column of
ones and $\mathbf{Z}$ maps each record to its calf. The random block gets
$\mathbf{A}^{-1}\lambda$ added, which for $\lambda = 2$ puts $2 \times 2 = 4$ on the diagonal
for each calf and couples every parent to its offspring at $-2$.

**Step 3 — solve.** The estimated mean is

$$
\hat\mu = 4.129412
$$

which is **not** the average of the three records, 4.166667. The records are not independent —
two of them share a sire — so the generalised least-squares estimate weights them
differently from a simple average. This is the second thing BLUP is for.

**Step 4 — read the breeding values.**

| animal | record | deviation | BLUP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sire A | — | — | $+0.223529$ |
| sire B | — | — | $-0.223529$ |
| dam D | — | — | $0.000000$ |
| calf 4 | 4.5 | $+0.370588$ | $+0.163529$ |
| calf 5 | 5.1 | $+0.970588$ | $+0.283529$ |
| calf 6 | 2.9 | $-1.229412$ | $-0.335294$ |

**Step 5 — three things to notice.** Every calf is shrunk toward zero, and by a lot: with
$h^2 = 1/3$, roughly two-thirds of each deviation is attributed to environment. The two
sires get non-zero estimates without records, ranked correctly — A's two calves are both
above average, B's one calf is well below. And the dam gets exactly zero, because she is the
dam of *all three* calves and so contributes identically to every record: her effect cannot
be separated from the overall mean, and BLUP correctly declines to guess.

</WorkedExample>

## λ is a heritability wearing different clothes

The variance ratio and the heritability are the same number:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2_e}{\sigma^2_g} = \frac{1 - h^2}{h^2},
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
h^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda}
$$

Re-solving the worked example at three values makes the consequence concrete:

| $\lambda$ | $h^2$ | $\hat\mu$ | sire A | calf 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 0.6667 | 4.087500 | $+0.475000$ | $+0.625000$ |
| 2 | 0.3333 | 4.129412 | $+0.223529$ | $+0.283529$ |
| 9 | 0.1000 | 4.155932 | $+0.064407$ | $+0.080196$ |

A low-heritability trait is shrunk almost to nothing, which is correct: if only a tenth of
the variance is transmissible, a record is mostly noise about breeding value. Note also that
$\hat\mu$ drifts toward the raw mean of 4.166667 as $h^2$ falls — as the records become
uninformative about genetics, the correlation structure they induce weakens and GLS
approaches ordinary least squares.

## From A to G: genomic selection

Everything so far uses the *expected* relationship from the pedigree. Two full sibs get
$A_{ij} = 0.5$ because that is what full sibs share on average. They do not actually share
exactly half — Mendelian segregation is random, and the realised fraction varies with a
standard deviation of roughly 0.04.

<Citation id="meuwissen2001genomic" /> proposed replacing the pedigree matrix with one
estimated from dense markers, and predicting breeding values for animals with genotypes but
no phenotypes. <Citation id="vanraden2008" /> gave the form that became standard:

$$
\mathbf{G} = \frac{\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}}{\sum_j 2p_j(1-p_j)},
\qquad
W_{ij} = g_{ij} - 2p_j
$$

Substituting $\mathbf{G}$ for $\mathbf{A}$ in Henderson's equations gives **GBLUP**, and
nothing else changes. What changes is what the matrix knows: $\mathbf{G}$ captures realised
sharing rather than expected sharing, which is why it can distinguish two full sibs at all —
and, as <Citation id="habier2007relationship" /> showed, a large part of the accuracy of
genomic prediction comes precisely from this better estimate of relationship rather than from
tracking causal variants.

<WorkedExample
  title="GBLUP is ridge regression on markers"
  given={[
    { label: 'Animals', value: '5' },
    { label: 'Markers', value: '6, coded 0/1/2' },
    { label: 'Variance ratio λ', value: '2' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

Two apparently different methods, computed independently.

**Route 1 — GBLUP.** Build $\mathbf{G}$ from the markers, then solve for breeding values
directly, one number per animal:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{G}\left(\mathbf{G} + \lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (0.294451,\; -0.244158,\; -0.474334,\; 0.423848,\; 0.000193)
$$

**Route 2 — SNP-BLUP.** Never build $\mathbf{G}$. Instead fit a ridge regression for the
*marker* effects, with a penalty $\lambda_\beta = \lambda \sum_j 2p_j(1-p_j)$, and then sum
each animal's marker effects:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \left(\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W} + \lambda_\beta \mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf{e}},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{W}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (0.294451,\; -0.244158,\; -0.474334,\; 0.423848,\; 0.000193)
$$

**They agree to $1.1\times10^{-16}$** — machine precision, not approximation. The two routes
are the same estimator written in two bases, and which one you compute is purely a question
of whether you have more animals or more markers: SNP-BLUP inverts a matrix of size $m$, and
GBLUP one of size $n$.

This equivalence is the organising fact of the field, and <Citation id="deloscampos2013regression" />
extends it: a whole family of methods — BayesA, BayesB, the Bayesian lasso — differ from
GBLUP only in the prior placed on marker effects. GBLUP is the member of that family with a
normal prior, which is exactly the infinitesimal model of Module 2.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="A sample-centred G has no inverse, and the textbook equations need one">

Henderson's equations as written above contain $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$. Substituting
$\mathbf{G}$ and reaching for $\mathbf{G}^{-1}$ fails, and not for a numerical reason.

If the allele frequencies used for centring are computed from the same sample, then every
column of $\mathbf{W}$ sums to zero by construction, so $\mathbf{W}$ has rank at most $n-1$
and every row of $\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}/s$ sums to zero as well.
$\mathbf{G}$ is **singular by construction**, and inverting it throws.

Three ways out, all in use: centre on external reference frequencies; add a small ridge to
the diagonal, usually written $\mathbf{G} + 0.01\mathbf{I}$, or blend with $\mathbf{A}$; or
use the equivalent non-inverse form $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}$,
which never inverts $\mathbf{K}$ alone and is what the worked example above uses. The third
is exact and needs no fudge, which is why it is worth knowing that the two forms are
identical wherever both are defined.

</Callout>

## What a reference population buys

Accuracy rises with the size of the training population, the heritability, and inversely with
how many independent chromosome segments have to be estimated:

$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{N h^2}{N h^2 + M_e}}
$$

This is <Citation id="daetwyler2008" />'s relation, and it is the same expression as the
polygenic-score accuracy ceiling in Module 5, written in the vocabulary of animal breeding.

## Why genomic selection actually changed dairy cattle

The accuracy of a genomic prediction on a young bull is *worse* than a progeny test — around
0.65 against 0.99. Genomic selection nonetheless roughly doubled the rate of genetic gain,
and the reason has nothing to do with accuracy.

Genetic gain per year is

$$
\Delta G = \frac{i\, r\, \sigma_A}{L}
$$

where $L$ is the generation interval. Progeny testing requires waiting for a bull's daughters
to lactate, which puts $L$ near six years. A genomic prediction is available at birth, which
puts it near two. So

$$
\frac{\Delta G_{\text{genomic}}}{\Delta G_{\text{progeny}}}
= \frac{0.65/2}{0.99/6} = \frac{0.325}{0.165} = 1.97
$$

Nearly a doubling, bought entirely by **not waiting** — and paid for with a third of the
accuracy. <Citation id="hayes2009dairy" /> is the review that documented the transition as it
happened. The general lesson generalises past cattle: when a prediction sits inside a cycle,
its value depends on when it arrives as much as on how good it is.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $h^2 = 1/(1+\lambda)$: 0.6667, 0.3333 and 0.1000.

**(b)** Taking the deviation at each solution's own $\hat\mu$, the surviving fractions are
roughly 62%, 29% and 8%.

**(c)** The fraction retained tracks the heritability, because BLUP is the conditional
expectation of breeding value given the record — and the fraction of a record that is
breeding value *is* the heritability.

That last identity is exact in the simplest case. For a single own record and no relatives,
BLUP reduces to $\hat u = h^2(y - \mu)$, which is the breeder's equation from the previous
lesson applied to one individual. Everything else BLUP does — relatives, unequal information,
fixed effects — is a generalisation of that one line.

<Exercise n={2} title="How large a reference population?" difficulty="core">

A breeding programme has a trait with $h^2 = 0.30$ and estimates $M_e = 10{,}000$.
(a) How many training records are needed for accuracies of 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9? (b) The
programme currently has 20,000 and wants to reach 0.9. Comment. (c) What else could be
changed?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Invert $r^2 = Nh^2/(Nh^2 + M_e)$ to get $N = r^2 M_e / \left[h^2(1-r^2)\right]$:

| target $r$ | $N$ required |
| --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 11,111 |
| 0.7 | 32,026 |
| 0.9 | 142,105 |

**(b)** Going from 20,000 to 142,105 is a seven-fold increase in phenotyping for a jump from
0.61 to 0.90. The cost grows as $r^2/(1-r^2)$, which diverges — the last stretch toward 1 is
unbounded, exactly as with the polygenic-score ceiling. For most programmes 0.9 is not a
target, it is a statement that the budget was never checked.

**(c)** Three things, in rough order of practicality. Reduce $M_e$ by working within a
narrower population — $M_e$ scales with effective population size, which is why a single
breed predicts better than a mixture and why across-breed prediction is so much harder.
Raise the effective heritability by taking repeated measurements. Or accept the accuracy and
spend the money on shortening the generation interval instead, which by the previous section
is usually the better purchase.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Why the textbook equations fail on a genomic matrix" difficulty="stretch">

You build $\mathbf{G}$ from markers, centring on allele frequencies computed from the same
five animals, and try to form Henderson's equations. (a) Show that every row of
$\mathbf{G}$ sums to zero. (b) What does that imply, and what happens when you try?
(c) Give two fixes and say which is exact.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Centring uses $\hat p_j = \sum_i g_{ij}/(2n)$, so each column of
$\mathbf{W}$ has $\sum_i W_{ij} = \sum_i g_{ij} - 2n\hat p_j = 0$ exactly. Then the $i$-th row
sum of $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}$ is
$\sum_k \sum_j W_{ij}W_{kj} = \sum_j W_{ij}\left(\sum_k W_{kj}\right) = 0$.

**(b)** A matrix whose rows all sum to zero has the all-ones vector in its null space, so it
is singular and has no inverse. Attempting it raises a singular-matrix error — and this is
not a rounding artefact that a tolerance could absorb, it is exact and structural.

**(c)** Two fixes. *Blend or ridge*: use $\mathbf{G} + \delta\mathbf{I}$ or a weighted average
of $\mathbf{G}$ and $\mathbf{A}$, which is standard practice and also guards against
genotyping error, but introduces an arbitrary constant. *Use the equivalent non-inverse form*
$\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}$, which
never inverts $\mathbf{K}$ on its own; $\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I}$ is non-singular for
any $\lambda > 0$ because $\mathbf{K}$ is positive semi-definite.

The second is exact — it agrees with Henderson's equations to machine precision wherever
those can be formed, and remains defined where they cannot. A third option, centring on
external reference frequencies, avoids the problem at its source but makes the matrix depend
on which reference you chose.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **BLUP estimates fixed effects and predicts breeding values in one system**, and its
  central practical property is that individuals with no record still get estimates, driven
  entirely by their relatives through $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$.
- **Henderson's equations avoid the $n \times n$ phenotypic covariance entirely.** The only
  inverse that appears is $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$, which is sparse and can be written straight from
  the pedigree. That is why national evaluations on tens of millions of records are possible.
- **Shrinkage is the conditional expectation, not a compromise**, and $\lambda = (1-h^2)/h^2$
  controls it. At $h^2 = 1/3$ a record's deviation of $+0.971$ becomes an estimate of
  $+0.284$.
- **An effect confounded with a fixed effect is returned as exactly zero** — the dam of all
  three calves in the worked example gets 0.000000, because nothing in the data distinguishes
  her from the mean.
- **GBLUP is Henderson's equations with $\mathbf{G}$ in place of $\mathbf{A}$**, and it is
  algebraically identical to ridge regression on marker effects — verified here to
  $1.1\times10^{-16}$. Which you compute depends only on whether you have more animals or
  more markers.
- **A sample-centred $\mathbf{G}$ is singular by construction.** Every row sums to zero, so
  $\mathbf{G}^{-1}$ does not exist; the non-inverse form is the exact way around it.
- **Accuracy follows $r = \sqrt{Nh^2/(Nh^2+M_e)}$**, and the real gain from genomic selection
  was not accuracy at all: predicting at birth rather than after a progeny test cut the
  generation interval from six years to two, nearly doubling annual gain despite an accuracy
  of 0.65 against 0.99.
