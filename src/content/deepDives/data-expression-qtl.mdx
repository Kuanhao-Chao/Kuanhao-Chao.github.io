## The question this page answers

A GWAS hit is a position, and positions are not actionable. The variant is usually
non-coding, the nearest gene is frequently not the right one, and the locus may span several
plausible candidates.

Molecular QTLs are the bridge: find variants that change something measurable about a gene —
how much RNA it makes, how that RNA is spliced, how much protein appears — and ask whether the
variant driving the trait is the same one.

<Callout variant="intuition">
The naive approach is to look for an eQTL at the GWAS locus and, finding one, conclude the
GWAS acts through that gene. At a locus dense with variants in linkage disequilibrium, this
almost always finds something, and the something is frequently a coincidence.

The real question is not "is there a signal in both datasets" but **"is it the same variant"**.
Two independent causal variants a few kilobases apart, in LD with each other, produce two
overlapping association peaks that look identical to one shared variant unless you ask
carefully.

Colocalisation is the careful asking. It enumerates the possibilities — neither, one, the
other, both-but-different, both-and-shared — and puts a posterior on each. Everything below
is about the last two, because they are the two the eye cannot separate.
</Callout>

## Three molecular phenotypes, and a portal over them

**<Dataset id="gtex" />** is the reference tissue atlas <Citation id="gtex2020" />: cis-eQTLs
and sQTLs across 49 tissues. Two limits are worth carrying. Its donors are post-mortem adults,
so regulation that only happens during development, or on immune stimulation, or in a rare
cell type, is largely invisible — which is a common and benign reason a GWAS locus has no
GTEx eQTL. And summary statistics are open while genotypes are controlled.

**<Dataset id="eqtl-catalogue" />** reprocesses many published studies through one pipeline
<Citation id="kerimov2021eqtl" />. That uniformity is the product: studies using different
annotations, covariates and normalisations are not comparable as published, and colocalising
across them without reprocessing compares pipelines as much as biology.

**sQTLs** ask a different question from eQTLs — not how much RNA but which isoform — and are
typically quantified from intron excision ratios rather than gene-level counts
<Citation id="li2018leafcutter" />. A variant can leave total expression untouched while
switching the protein produced, which no eQTL will detect.

**<Dataset id="ukb-ppp" />** measures protein abundance <Citation id="sun2023pqtl" />, one
step closer to function. Its characteristic artefact is worth knowing: a variant inside the
epitope an affinity assay binds changes *measured* abundance without changing the protein, so
some cis-pQTLs are purely technical.

**<Dataset id="opentargets-genetics" />** assembles all of it per locus and trains a
Locus-to-Gene model over the features <Citation id="mountjoy2021l2g" />. L2G is a prediction,
and being trained on known gene-disease pairs it inherits a bias toward genes that were
already known.

## The five hypotheses

For one genomic region and two traits, colocalisation enumerates
<Citation id="giambartolomei2014coloc" />:

- **H0** — neither trait has a causal variant here.
- **H1** — trait 1 only. **H2** — trait 2 only.
- **H3** — both, at *different* causal variants.
- **H4** — both, at the *same* causal variant.

$$
\text{PP}_k \;\propto\; \text{prior}_k \times \sum \text{ABF}
$$

with per-variant priors $p_1$, $p_2$ for a single-trait causal variant and $p_{12}$ for a
shared one. H3 sums over distinct variant pairs; H4 sums over the same variant in both.

<Callout variant="note" title="These Bayes factors point the other way">
The approximate Bayes factors used here are $\mathrm{BF_{10}}$ — alternative over null —
so a larger value is *more* evidence that the variant is associated, and the sums above grow
with the strength of the signal.

That is the **reciprocal** of the $\mathrm{BF_{01}}$ convention this curriculum uses for
Wakefield's ABF in fine-mapping, where the factor is written null over alternative. Both are
standard in their own literature and neither is wrong; mixing them inside one calculation
inverts the answer silently, which is why the direction is stated here rather than assumed.
</Callout>

Five variants at a locus. In case A both traits peak at the **third**; in case B the eQTL
peaks at the **second** instead. Nothing else differs.

**Step 1 — case A, shared peak.**

$$
\text{PP4} = 1.0000
$$

The evidence is overwhelming and unambiguous: one variant drives both, and the gene is
implicated.

**Step 2 — case B, adjacent peaks.**

$$
\text{PP3} = 0.9523, \qquad \text{PP4} = 0.0262
$$

Both traits still have a strong signal in this region — an analysis that only checked "is
there an eQTL here?" would return yes in both cases and implicate the gene both times. The
posterior says the second case is two separate causal variants that happen to be neighbours,
and the gene is **not** implicated by it.

**Step 3 — note what separates them.** One index. The peak moved by a single variant, and the
conclusion inverted. This is why colocalisation is not a formality laid over an overlap
check: the overlap is the same in both cases and the answer is opposite.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="A high PP4 can be built from almost no QTL evidence">
Take a locus with an overwhelming GWAS signal and an eQTL that barely registers — a single
approximate Bayes factor of 25, which is not a discovery by any threshold anyone uses. The
posteriors come out as

$$
\text{PP1} = 0.2849, \qquad \text{PP4} = 0.7122
$$

A PP4 above 0.7 is routinely reported as a colocalisation, and here it rests on an eQTL that
would not survive multiple-testing correction on its own.

The mechanism is structural rather than a bug. When one trait's evidence is enormous, H1 and
H4 both explain it, and what separates them is only the modest additional evidence from trait
two weighed against the ratio of $p_{12}$ to $p_1$. A little QTL evidence goes a long way,
because the prior is already saying that a variant causal for the GWAS is fairly likely to be
causal for expression too.

The remedy is to read PP4 beside the QTL's own significance, and to treat colocalisation as
uninformative — rather than negative — where the QTL is underpowered
<Citation id="wallace2021coloc" />. This is also why single-signal coloc is increasingly
replaced by methods that fine-map both traits first and colocalise credible sets.
</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading the whole posterior" difficulty="warm-up">

At a locus with a strong GWAS signal and a completely flat eQTL — every eQTL Bayes factor
equal to 1 — the posteriors are PP0 0.0090, PP1 0.9006, PP2 0.0000, PP3 0.0001, PP4 0.0901.
What does this say, and what would reporting PP4 alone have suggested?

<div slot="solution">

It says **trait 1 only**: there is a causal variant for the GWAS here and no evidence of one
for expression. PP1 at 0.90 is the conclusion; PP4 at 0.09 is the residual probability that
the same variant also affects expression undetectably.

Reporting PP4 = 0.09 alone would read as "not colocalised", which is correct here but for the
wrong reason — it would read identically at a locus where both traits are absent, or where the
eQTL is present but distinct. The five posteriors sum to one and are meant to be read
together; a single one of them is a projection that discards which alternative it lost to.

Check the sum as a habit: $0.0090 + 0.9006 + 0.0000 + 0.0001 + 0.0901 = 0.9998$, which is 1
up to the rounding shown.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="What one Bayes factor buys" difficulty="core">

Compare the flat-eQTL locus above with the same locus when a single variant gains an eQTL
Bayes factor of 25. How much does PP4 move, and is that proportionate?

<div slot="solution">

$$
\text{PP4}: 0.0901 \;\longrightarrow\; 0.7122
$$

An eight-fold increase in the posterior, from a piece of evidence that on its own is
unremarkable — a Bayes factor of 25 against a null is "worth a look", not "established".

It is proportionate *given the priors*, and that is the point. The likelihood ratio between H4
and H1 at the shared variant is roughly the eQTL's Bayes factor there scaled by
$p_{12}/p_1$, so a factor of 25 in the data becomes a large swing in the posterior because the
prior ratio is only ten-to-one against. The arithmetic is doing what it was told.

What is not proportionate is the *reporting*. "PP4 = 0.71" in a table looks like the same kind
of statement as "PP4 = 0.71" at a locus with a genome-wide-significant eQTL, and it is not.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The prior nobody examines" difficulty="stretch">

Keep that locus fixed and vary only $p_{12}$, the prior that a variant is causal for both
traits, across $10^{-6}$, $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-4}$. What happens, and what should be reported?

<div slot="solution">

| $p_{12}$ | PP1 | PP4 | reads as |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| $10^{-6}$ | 0.7934 | 0.1983 | not colocalised |
| $10^{-5}$ | 0.2849 | 0.7122 | probably colocalised |
| $10^{-4}$ | 0.0384 | 0.9612 | confidently colocalised |

The same data, three incompatible conclusions, from a parameter that is almost never stated in
a results table and is usually left at a package default.

$p_{12}$ encodes a genuine belief: how often is a variant that affects a trait *also* one that
affects this gene's expression. There is no universal answer — it depends on the trait, the
tissue, and how the region was selected — and the default of $10^{-5}$ is a convention rather
than an estimate.

Three things follow:

- **Report the priors** with the posteriors, as you would report a significance threshold.
- **Show sensitivity** when PP4 is near a decision boundary. A colocalisation that survives
  $p_{12} = 10^{-6}$ is a different claim from one that needs $10^{-4}$.
- **Prefer strong evidence on both sides.** At the shared-peak locus in the worked example,
  PP4 stays essentially 1 across this whole range: when both traits have decisive evidence, the
  prior stops mattering. Prior sensitivity is a symptom of weak data, not a flaw in the method.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A GWAS locus names a position, not a gene.** Molecular QTLs bridge the gap by linking
  variants to expression (eQTL), splicing (sQTL) or protein abundance (pQTL) — and a variant
  can change the isoform without changing the amount, which only an sQTL sees.
- **The question is not "is there a signal in both" but "is it the same variant".** At a locus
  in LD the first question almost always answers yes.
- **Colocalisation enumerates five hypotheses**, and the whole difficulty lives in H3 against
  H4. Moving the eQTL peak by a single variant turned PP4 = 1.0000 into PP3 = 0.9523.
- **A high PP4 can rest on very little QTL evidence.** A single Bayes factor of 25 took PP4
  from 0.0901 to 0.7122; read PP4 beside the QTL's own significance, and treat an underpowered
  QTL as uninformative rather than negative.
- **The shared-causal prior decides borderline cases.** The same locus gives PP4 of 0.1983,
  0.7122 or 0.9612 at $p_{12}$ of $10^{-6}$, $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-4}$. Report the priors, and
  show sensitivity when it is close.
- **Uniform reprocessing matters.** Colocalising across studies with different pipelines
  compares pipelines as much as biology, which is what the eQTL Catalogue exists to remove.
