## The question this lesson answers

The previous lessons built the machinery for testing one variant at a time. For rare
variants that machinery does not work, and the reason is arithmetic rather than
methodological.

Sample size scales as $1/q^2$, and $q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$ carries the allele frequency
linearly. Halve the frequency and you double the sample required. Below about 0.1% the
requirement passes what any cohort can supply, **even for effects large enough that no
common variant has ever had one**.

So rare variants are not tested individually. They are aggregated — a gene's variants
collapsed into a single test — and every method in this lesson is a different answer to one
question: *how* should they be aggregated?

## The power cliff

Take a variant with $\beta = 0.5$ — half a phenotypic standard deviation per allele, which
would be an extraordinary effect for a complex trait — and ask how many people are needed at
genome-wide significance and 80% power, using $N \ge 39.60/q^2$ from Module 4's opening
lesson.

<WorkedExample
  title="What frequency costs"
  given={[
    { label: 'Effect β', value: '0.5 SD per allele' },
    { label: 'Threshold', value: '5 × 10⁻⁸, 80% power' },
    { label: 'Constant', value: '(z_{α/2} + z_β)² = 39.600989' },

>

**Step 1 — the variance explained at four frequencies.**

$$
q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2 = 2p(1-p)(0.25)
$$

| MAF | $q^2$ | $N \ge 39.60/q^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | $2.3750\times10^{-2}$ | 1,668 |
| 1% | $4.9500\times10^{-3}$ | 8,001 |
| 0.1% | $4.9950\times10^{-4}$ | 79,282 |
| 0.01% | $4.9995\times10^{-5}$ | 792,099 |

**Step 2 — read the scaling.** Each tenfold drop in frequency costs a tenfold rise in sample
size, because $q^2$ is linear in $p$ when $p$ is small. Between a 5% variant and a 0.01% one
the requirement rises 475-fold, and the effect size never changed.

**Step 3 — see why this is fatal rather than inconvenient.** 792,099 people is larger than
most biobanks, for a variant with an effect ten times anything a common-variant GWAS reports.
For the effects rare variants *actually* have, and at the frequencies they *actually* occur,
single-variant testing is not underpowered — it is impossible.

**Step 4 — what aggregation buys.** Twenty variants each at 0.01%, collapsed into one test,
behave roughly like a single variant at 0.2%. That moves the requirement from 792,099 to
under 40,000. **Aggregation is not a refinement of rare-variant analysis; it is the thing
that makes it exist.**

</WorkedExample>

## Collapsing: burden tests

The first family of methods collapses a gene's variants into a single burden score per
person and tests that. <Citation id="li2008cmc" /> introduced the combined multivariate and
collapsing approach; <Citation id="madsen2009weighted" /> added frequency weighting;
<Citation id="morris2010evaluation" /> compared the variants of the idea systematically.

In score form the burden statistic is

$$
Q_{\mathrm{burden}} = \left(\sum_j w_j S_j\right)^2
$$

— weight each variant's score, add them up, *then* square.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="What the Beta weight actually does, and what it does not">

The standard weight is the Beta density $w_j = \mathrm{Beta}(\mathrm{MAF}_j;\,1,\,25)$,
which for the default parameters is simply

$$
w_j = 25\,(1 - \mathrm{MAF}_j)^{24}
$$

It is easy to read that 25 as saying a singleton gets twenty-five times the weight of a 1%
variant. It does not. The 25 is the density's **height at zero**, not a ratio, and across
the rare range the weight is remarkably flat: a near-singleton gets $1.27\times$ the weight
of a 1% variant, or $1.62\times$ once SKAT squares it.

What the weight actually does is **suppress common variants**. Against a 5% variant the
ratio is $3.42\times$, and squared, $11.73\times$. So the Beta weight is not a sharp prior
favouring the very rarest alleles; it is a soft filter keeping common variants from
dominating a test that was built for rare ones.

That distinction matters when choosing parameters. If you want the rarest alleles to
dominate, Beta(1, 25) will not do it, and increasing the second parameter is what changes
the tilt.

</Callout>

## The flaw: directions cancel

The burden statistic squares **after** summing, which is exactly the right thing to do when
every variant in the gene pushes the same way — and exactly the wrong thing when they do not.

A gene containing both loss-of-function alleles that lower a trait and gain-of-function
alleles that raise it will have scores of both signs. The weighted sum cancels, and the
burden test sees nothing at all.

<WorkedExample
  title="Two genes, identical except for three minus signs"
  given={[
    { label: 'Five variants, MAF', value: '0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.008, 0.010' },
    { label: 'Gene A scores', value: '+4, +3, +5, +2, +3' },
    { label: 'Gene B scores', value: '+4, −3, +5, −2, −4' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

Both genes have the same variants, the same frequencies, therefore the same weights, and the
same score *magnitudes*. Only three signs differ.

**Step 1 — the weights.** $w_j = 25(1-\mathrm{MAF}_j)^{24}$ gives

$$
(24.4068,\; 23.8272,\; 22.1663,\; 20.6167,\; 19.6420)
$$

**Step 2 — the weighted sums.** For gene A every term adds:

$$
\sum_j w_j S_j = 380.1000
$$

For gene B they fight each other:

$$
\sum_j w_j S_j = 17.1762
$$

**Step 3 — the burden statistics.** Squaring:

$$
Q^A_{\mathrm{burden}} = 380.1000^2 = 144{,}476,
\qquad
Q^B_{\mathrm{burden}} = 17.1762^2 = 295
$$

A factor of **490**, produced entirely by three sign flips.

**Step 4 — now SKAT, which squares before summing.**

$$
Q_{\mathrm{SKAT}} = \sum_j w_j^2 S_j^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
Q^A_{\mathrm{SKAT}} = 32{,}097, \quad Q^B_{\mathrm{SKAT}} = 34{,}797
$$

Essentially unchanged — gene B is if anything slightly *larger*, because its scores are
marginally bigger in magnitude. Squaring first makes sign irrelevant.

**Step 5 — the trade.** In gene A the burden statistic is $4.50\times$ SKAT's; in gene B,
SKAT's is $117.9\times$ the burden's. Neither test dominates. Each is optimal for a
different truth, and the truth is not known in advance.

</WorkedExample>

## SKAT, and then SKAT-O

<Citation id="wu2011skat" /> derived the sequence kernel association test as a
variance-component score test. Instead of asking whether the gene's variants have a non-zero
*mean* effect, it asks whether they have a non-zero *variance* of effects — a question that
does not care about sign:

$$
Q_{\mathrm{SKAT}} = \sum_j w_j^2 S_j^2
$$

The price is real. Against a genuinely unidirectional signal SKAT is less powerful than the
burden test, because it spends degrees of freedom accommodating a possibility that is not
present.

<Citation id="lee2012skato" /> resolved the choice by refusing to make it. SKAT-O takes the
convex combination

$$
Q_\rho = \rho\,Q_{\mathrm{burden}} + (1-\rho)\,Q_{\mathrm{SKAT}}
$$

and maximises over $\rho$, paying a modest multiple-testing cost for the search. $\rho = 1$
recovers the burden test, $\rho = 0$ recovers SKAT, and the optimum lands wherever the gene's
architecture puts it. That is why SKAT-O rather than either component is the default in
modern pipelines — not because it is more powerful than the best of the two, but because it
does not require knowing which is the best of the two.

<Callout variant="warning" title="At biobank scale the asymptotics fail before the methods do">

Everything above assumes the score statistic is approximately normal, which is the
assumption Module 1 flagged as the first to break with sparse counts. In rare-variant
analysis it breaks routinely and in the dangerous direction.

Consider a binary trait with 5,000 cases and 400,000 controls, and a variant carried by
thirty people. The case-control ratio is 1:80 and the carrier count is tiny; the normal
approximation to the score is badly skewed, and the resulting $p$-values are
**anti-conservative** — too small, so the test over-rejects. Whole exome-wide scans run this
way produce inflation that has nothing to do with population structure and that
LD score regression will not diagnose.

<Citation id="zhou2020saigegene" /> addresses it with a saddlepoint approximation to the
score's distribution, which is accurate in the tail where the normal approximation is not.
<Citation id="backman2021exome" /> is the analysis at the scale that made the problem
unavoidable, and <Citation id="povysil2019collapsing" /> reviews the design decisions —
which variants to include, how to define a mask, what to do about ancestry — that determine
a collapsing analysis's result at least as much as the choice between burden and SKAT.

The order of operations matters here: a beautifully chosen kernel on a badly calibrated
score is still a badly calibrated test.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="Where SKAT-O lands" difficulty="warm-up">

Using the two genes from the worked example, compute $Q_\rho$ at
$\rho = 0,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1$ for each. Where is each maximised, and what does that
say about the method?

<div slot="solution">

| $\rho$ | gene A | gene B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 (pure SKAT) | 32,097 | 34,797 |
| 0.25 | 60,192 | 26,172 |
| 0.5 | 88,286 | 17,546 |
| 0.75 | 116,381 | 8,921 |
| 1 (pure burden) | 144,476 | 295 |

Gene A is maximised at $\rho = 1$ and gene B at $\rho = 0$ — the two opposite corners.

**What it says:** the optimal $\rho$ is a property of the gene, not of the study, and it is
different for two genes in the same analysis. A method that fixed $\rho$ in advance would be
wrong on roughly half the genome, and no amount of care in choosing it would help, because
the choice would have to be made per gene and the information needed to make it is what the
test is trying to discover.

Note also that $Q_\rho$ is linear in $\rho$, so its maximum over $[0,1]$ is always at an
endpoint for a fixed dataset. SKAT-O's real work is in the *null distribution* of the
maximised statistic, which accounts for having searched — the search itself is not the hard
part.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="How many genes, and at what threshold" difficulty="core">

A gene-based exome scan tests roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes. (a) What Bonferroni
threshold applies, and what $\chi^2_1$ critical value? (b) Why is this not
$5\times10^{-8}$? (c) A colleague argues the gene-based threshold should be even looser
because the tests are correlated. Evaluate.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $0.05/20{,}000 = 2.50\times10^{-6}$, with a $\chi^2_1$ critical value of $22.1665$ —
the same numbers Module 4's opening lesson derived for this design.

**(b)** Because $5\times10^{-8}$ is $0.05$ divided by roughly a million *effectively
independent common-variant tests*. A gene-based scan performs 20,000 tests, not a million,
so applying the genome-wide threshold holds the family-wise error at $0.001$ rather than
$0.05$ and discards real signal for nothing. The correction should match the number of tests
actually performed.

**(c)** The colleague is directionally right and it usually does not help much. Gene-based
tests are less correlated than common-variant tests, because genes are separated by
recombination and rare variants tag almost nothing outside their own gene — so the
effective number of tests is close to 20,000, and Bonferroni is close to correct rather than
badly conservative. That is the opposite of the common-variant case, where a million
effective tests stand in for ten million performed.

Where the argument does bite is in multi-mask analyses: running four variant masks per gene
is 80,000 tests, and those *are* strongly correlated with one another because they are
nested subsets of the same variants. Bonferroni over 80,000 would be genuinely conservative
there.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A gene that only one test can see" difficulty="stretch">

You analyse a gene with a strong biological prior and get $p = 0.4$ from a burden test.
(a) What single check would you run before concluding there is no signal? (b) The gene
contains both truncating and missense variants. How does that change the analysis?
(c) The trait is binary with 3,000 cases and 300,000 controls. What else would you check?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Run SKAT on the same gene. A burden $p$ of 0.4 with a SKAT $p$ of $10^{-6}$ is the
signature of directional cancellation, and it is not rare — the worked example's gene B has
a burden statistic 490 times smaller than gene A's while carrying essentially the same
information. Better still, run SKAT-O, which does both and adjusts for having done so.

**(b)** It makes cancellation the *expected* case rather than a possibility. Truncating
variants generally remove function; missense variants can remove, reduce, or occasionally
increase it. Aggregating both into one burden score is close to guaranteed to mix
directions.

The standard response is to test them as separate masks — truncating alone, missense alone,
both together — accepting the multiple-testing cost. That is a better use of prior knowledge
than choosing a kernel, because it puts a biological hypothesis into the variant set rather
than into the test statistic.

**(c)** The case-control ratio, which is 1:100. With that imbalance and rare carrier counts,
the normal approximation to the score statistic is skewed and $p$-values are
anti-conservative — so a *significant* result needs checking before a null one does. Confirm
the analysis used a saddlepoint approximation or an exact method rather than the asymptotic
score test, and check whether the inflation appears across all genes rather than in this one.

The two failure modes point in opposite directions, which is why both checks are needed: a
burden test can hide a real signal, and an uncorrected asymptotic test can manufacture one.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Single-variant testing dies below about 0.1% frequency.** At MAF 0.01%, an effect of
  half a standard deviation still needs 792,099 people at genome-wide significance —
  a 475-fold rise over the same effect at 5%. Aggregation is what makes rare-variant
  analysis possible at all.
- **Burden tests collapse first and square second**, $Q = (\sum_j w_j S_j)^2$, which is
  optimal when every variant pushes the same way.
- **They fail when directions mix.** Flipping three signs in a five-variant gene — changing
  nothing else — drops the burden statistic from 144,476 to 295, a factor of 490.
- **SKAT squares first and sums second**, $Q = \sum_j w_j^2 S_j^2$, so sign cannot cancel.
  On the same two genes it returns 32,097 and 34,797 — an eight percent difference.
- **Neither dominates**: burden is $4.50\times$ SKAT on the unidirectional gene, SKAT is
  $117.9\times$ burden on the mixed one. SKAT-O mixes them and maximises over $\rho$, which
  lands at opposite endpoints for the two genes.
- **The Beta(1, 25) weight is flatter than its name suggests.** A near-singleton gets
  $1.27\times$ the weight of a 1% variant, not $25\times$; the weight's real work is
  suppressing common variants, at $3.42\times$ against a 5% variant.
- **Calibration fails before the kernel choice matters.** With unbalanced case-control ratios
  and small carrier counts the asymptotic score test is anti-conservative, and a saddlepoint
  or exact method is required before any of the above means anything.
