## The question this track answers

Someone shows you a peak on a Manhattan plot and asks what it means.

The peak itself is a modest statement: in this sample, genotype at this position correlates
with this trait more strongly than chance would produce across a million tests. Almost
nothing a reader actually wants follows from it directly. Which variant in the peak is doing
the work? How much of the trait does the whole genome explain, and how much of *that* has
been found? Would a score built from these results predict anything in a person? Does the
exposure the peak implicates *cause* the disease?

Each of those is a separate machine with its own inputs, its own failure modes, and its own
literature. This curriculum is those machines, in the order that they depend on each other.

<Callout variant="intuition">

Every method in this track is a way of reading one sentence: **an observed trait is a genetic
part plus everything else.** Module 2 asks what the genetic part is made of and how it travels
between generations. Module 3 asks how big it is. Module 4 asks which positions contribute to
it. Module 5 asks what may honestly be done with the answer. Module 1 supplies the arithmetic
all four use to decide whether a number is real. The differences between the methods are
mostly differences in what they are willing to assume about "everything else".

</Callout>

## The five modules

**Module 1 — Mathematical Foundations.** A likelihood, its first two derivatives, and the
three tests that fall out of them. The likelihood framework all three descend from is
Fisher's <Citation id="fisher1922likelihood" />; every later method in this curriculum is one
of the three tests wearing different clothing, so their disagreements are worth meeting once,
on a table small enough to check by hand. This is also where the genome-wide threshold comes
from, and where the two situations that break the χ² approximation are named. The module then
asks what a threshold is *for*: controlling the chance of any false positive at all is one
answer, and controlling the fraction of discoveries that are wrong <Citation id="benjamini1995fdr" />
is a different one, with a different price and a different procedure.

**Module 2 — Populations & Inheritance.** What a locus is before anyone tests it. Allele
frequencies under random mating, the drift that moves them in a finite population
<Citation id="wright1931drift" />, and the linkage disequilibrium that makes a tested variant
a proxy for its neighbours rather than a thing in itself <Citation id="hill1968" />. The last
of these is why Module 5 exists: the variant with the smallest p-value in a region is usually
not the causal one, and nothing in Module 4 can tell them apart.

The module then asks two questions of the same data. How far apart have populations drifted —
which turns out to have a sharp answer, and to decide whether principal components can see the
structure at all <Citation id="patterson2006eigen" /> — and whether anything *pushed* them,
which is the difference between an allele's frequency and the age that frequency implies.

**Module 3 — Quantitative Genetics.** How much genetic signal a trait has at all. Fisher's
reconciliation of Mendelian inheritance with continuous variation
<Citation id="fisher1918infinitesimal" /> gives the variance decomposition; heritability is
the ratio it defines, and it is routinely over-read <Citation id="visscher2008" />. Estimating
it from genotypes rather than pedigrees <Citation id="yang2011gcta" /> is what turned it into
a measurable quantity in unrelated people, and predicting breeding values from dense markers
<Citation id="meuwissen2001genomic" /> is the same mathematics pointed at a different goal.
Heritability is a ceiling: no predictor built from additive genetic data can beat it, and
knowing its value tells you how much of the problem is left.

The module closes by asking what a between-family estimate actually contains. Parents who
resemble each other, and parents who build the environment their children grow up in
<Citation id="kong2018nurture" />, both put something into that comparison that is not the
direct effect of a genotype — and a sibling comparison removes all of it algebraically.

**Module 4 — Association & Discovery.** Finding the positions. Marginal tests at millions of
sites, the population structure that inflates them, mixed models that absorb it, aggregation
tests for variants too rare to test one at a time, and meta-analysis to combine studies that
were never designed together. Distinguishing genuine polygenicity from confounding
<Citation id="bulik2015ldsc" /> is the diagnostic that makes the rest interpretable.

**Module 5 — Inference, Prediction & Causality.** What may be done with a hit. Fine-mapping
resolves an association into a small set of candidate variants
<Citation id="wang2020susie" />; putting expression in between turns a set of variants into a
ranked list of genes, at a cost that is exactly computable
<Citation id="gusev2016fusion" />; polygenic scores turn thousands of small effects into one
number per person; Mendelian randomisation uses genotype as an instrument to ask whether an
exposure causes an outcome <Citation id="daveysmith2003mr" />; and sequence models supply
functional predictions that are only evidence once they have been calibrated.

## One variant, five modules

The modules are easier to keep straight when one variant passes through all of them. The
variant below is unremarkable — a common SNP with a small effect, the kind that makes up most
of a GWAS catalogue — and the trace is entirely arithmetic.

**Module 1 — what would count as evidence?** Testing about a million effectively independent
positions, a Bonferroni correction on 0.05 gives the familiar genome-wide threshold
$\alpha = 5 \times 10^{-8}$. On the χ² scale with one degree of freedom that is

$$
\chi^2 \geq 29.7168, \qquad \text{equivalently } |z| \geq 5.4513 .
$$

Nothing about the variant enters yet. This is the bar, fixed by how many questions are being
asked at once.

**Module 2 — is the test valid here?** The effect allele here is also the minor one, at a
frequency of 0.30, so a sample of a thousand people is expected to contain
$1000 \times 0.30^2 = 90$ homozygous carriers. (Module 2 writes that frequency $q$ and
reserves $p$ for the major allele; $2p(1-p)$ below is symmetric in the two, so only the
genotype counts care which is which.) The χ² approximation is comfortable at
that count; it is *not* comfortable in the sparse cell a variant at 3% frequency would
produce, which is where Module 1's exact-test boundary applies. Note also what this variant
is: one marker in a block of correlated neighbours. Whatever the test finds belongs to the
block, not to this position.

**Module 3 — how much is there to find?** The variance a single additive variant explains is

$$
q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2 = 2(0.30)(0.70)(0.05)^2 = 1.05 \times 10^{-3}.
$$

That is 0.105% of the trait — and against a heritability of $h^2 = 0.30$, just 0.35% of the
genetic signal that exists. A variant like this is a normal member of a polygenic
architecture, not a disappointment.

**Module 4 — how many people to find it?** Power for a marginal test is governed entirely by
$q^2$. For 80% power at the threshold above,

$$
N \geq \frac{39.60}{q^2} = \frac{39.600989}{1.05 \times 10^{-3}} = 37{,}716 .
$$

The constant is $(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 = 39.600989$, written $39.60$ throughout this
curriculum; the division above uses it unrounded, which is the difference between 37,715 and
37,716.

Thirty-eight thousand people, and the variant clears genome-wide significance. By the
standards of Module 4 this is a success and it gets a row in the catalogue.

**Module 5 — what is it worth?** Now build a polygenic score from that same study. With
$M$ independent variants and heritability $h^2$, the expected accuracy of a score trained at
sample size $N$ is

$$
R^2 = \frac{h^2}{1 + M/(N h^2)} .
$$

At $N = 37{,}716$ this gives $R^2 = 3.3565 \times 10^{-3}$ — **0.34% of the trait**. The
entire genome-wide score, built from the same data that made this variant a discovery,
explains 3.2 times what the single variant does, and 1.1% of the heritability available.

The remaining distance is expensive. Reaching half the ceiling, $R^2 = 0.15$, needs
$N = 3{,}333{,}334$ — 88 times the discovery sample. Reaching nine tenths of it,
$R^2 = 0.27$, needs $N = 30{,}000{,}001$, or 795 times. The last tenth is not reachable at
any $N$; the formula approaches $h^2$ and never arrives.

**And as an instrument?** In Mendelian randomisation the strength of a single instrument is
$F = (\hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE})^2$, which for one variant is exactly its association $\chi^2$ in
the exposure GWAS. At $N = 37{,}716$ that is $Nq^2 = 39.60$ — and the equality is the point,
not a coincidence: it is the same non-centrality the power calculation just solved for. Since
the genome-wide threshold is $\chi^2 \geq 29.7168$, **anything that clears discovery is
already a strong instrument**, four times past the conventional $F > 10$. Instrument strength
and discovery significance are one number wearing two names. Prediction is the odd one out.

The gap between Module 4's answer and Module 5's answer is the single most useful thing to
carry out of this curriculum. Discovery and prediction are different problems with different
sample-size requirements, and a result that is impressive as one can be worthless as the
other. Most public confusion about genetics lives in exactly that gap.

## What each method estimates

The curriculum's methods, with the fact that most often decides whether one of them applies to
a problem: the assumption whose failure invalidates it. Nothing here is a substitute for the
lesson — it is an index for finding the right one.

<div class="dd-scroll-x">

| Method | What it estimates | What it needs | First assumption to fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Wald / score / likelihood-ratio tests](/deep_dives/statgen-mathematical-foundations/) | Whether a parameter differs from zero | A likelihood and an estimate | The χ² approximation — sparse counts and boundary parameters break it |
| [Hardy–Weinberg test](/deep_dives/statgen-population-infinitesimal/) | Whether genotype counts match allele frequencies | Genotype counts in one sample | Random mating — but genotyping error fails it far more often |
| [LD measures (D′, r²)](/deep_dives/statgen-linkage-disequilibrium/) | Correlation between two loci | Genotypes, or haplotypes | The reference panel’s ancestry matches the sample’s |
| [Linkage analysis, QTL mapping](/deep_dives/statgen-pedigrees-linkage-qtl/) | Map position of a locus | Phenotyped pedigrees or crosses | The marker map and the assumed inheritance model |
| [Breeder’s equation](/deep_dives/statgen-quantitative-genetics-selection/) | Response to one generation of selection | h² and the selection differential | h² stays constant as the population responds |
| [GREML / GCTA](/deep_dives/statgen-heritability-greml/) | SNP-based heritability | Individual genotypes and phenotypes | Causal variants are tagged as well as the average SNP |
| [BLUP, genomic selection](/deep_dives/statgen-blup-genomic-selection/) | Breeding values for selection candidates | Genotypes, phenotypes, a relationship matrix | Variance components are treated as known when they were estimated |
| [Multivariate models, G×E](/deep_dives/statgen-multivariate-genetics-gxe/) | Genetic correlation; interaction | Several traits, or several environments | The scale of measurement — interaction can vanish under transformation |
| [Linear mixed model GWAS](/deep_dives/statgen-association-linear-mixed-models/) | Per-variant effect, structure controlled | Individual genotypes and phenotypes | Relatedness and stratification are fully captured by the relatedness matrix |
| [LD score regression](/deep_dives/statgen-ldsc-sldsc/) | SNP heritability, genetic correlation, confounding | Summary statistics and an LD reference | The LD reference matches the GWAS sample’s ancestry |
| [Burden, SKAT, SKAT-O](/deep_dives/statgen-rare-variant-association/) | Gene-level rare-variant effect | Sequencing plus functional annotation | Burden assumes one direction of effect; SKAT assumes its weights |
| [Meta-analysis](/deep_dives/statgen-meta-analysis-replication/) | Pooled effect across studies | Per-study effects and standard errors | Fixed effects assume every study estimates the same parameter |
| [Bayesian fine-mapping](/deep_dives/statgen-bayesian-fine-mapping/) | Posterior inclusion probability (PIP), credible sets | Summary statistics and in-sample LD | The causal variant is present in the data; PIPs are normalised against an explicit null π₀ |
| [Polygenic scores](/deep_dives/statgen-polygenic-risk-scores/) | A predicted value for one person | Training effects and target genotypes | Effect sizes, LD and allele frequencies transfer to the target ancestry |
| [Mendelian randomisation](/deep_dives/statgen-mendelian-randomization/) | Causal effect of an exposure on an outcome | Two GWAS, and instruments | No horizontal pleiotropy — instruments act only through the exposure |
| [Sequence models](/deep_dives/statgen-deep-learning-synthesis/) | A functional score for a variant | Sequence, and labelled variants to calibrate against | The score is calibrated; an uncalibrated score is not evidence |

</div>

<Callout variant="key" title="Reading the table">

The fourth column is doing most of the work. Nearly every method in this list is
straightforward to run and produces a number regardless of whether its assumption holds — that
is what makes them dangerous. An LD reference from the wrong population still yields a
heritability estimate; a fixed-effects meta-analysis of heterogeneous studies still yields a
pooled effect with a small standard error; a polygenic score still returns a percentile for a
person whose ancestry is nowhere in the training data. Each lesson spends most of its length
on how to tell whether its assumption is holding.

</Callout>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="The same variant in a different trait" difficulty="warm-up">

Keep the variant from the worked example — $p = 0.30$, $\beta = 0.05$ — but move it to a trait
with $h^2 = 0.60$ rather than 0.30.

(a) Does $q^2$ change? Does the sample size needed to discover the variant change?

(b) At the *same* $N = 37{,}716$, what does the polygenic score's $R^2$ become?

(c) Explain in one sentence why one of those answers changed and the other did not.

<div slot="solution">

**(a) Neither changes.** $q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2$ contains no $h^2$ — it is a property of the
variant, not of the trait around it. So $q^2 = 1.05 \times 10^{-3}$ still, and
$N \geq 39.60/q^2 = 37{,}716$ still. Discovery power depends on how much variance *this
variant* explains, and a trait being more heritable overall does not make any one of its
variants easier to find.

**(b)** $R^2 = 0.60/(1 + 10^6/(37{,}716 \times 0.60)) = 0.013277$ — 1.33% of the trait, against
0.34% before. Roughly a fourfold improvement from a twofold change in $h^2$.

**(c)** $h^2$ enters prediction twice and discovery not at all. It is the ceiling, and it also
sits inside the shrinkage term $M/(Nh^2)$, so a more heritable trait both has more to give and
needs fewer samples to give it. That squared dependence is why the gain is about $2^2$ rather
than $2$.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Which module owns the question?" difficulty="core">

For each question, name the module — and the lesson — that answers it, and say what would go
wrong if you reached for a neighbouring module instead.

(a) "The lead SNP in this locus is intronic. Is that the causal variant?"

(b) "Our polygenic score has $R^2 = 0.08$ in Europeans and 0.02 in West Africans. Is the score
broken?"

(c) "Our λ is 1.18. Is the study confounded?"

(d) "This gene has twelve rare missense variants across 400 cases. Should we test them
individually?"

(e) "Higher circulating vitamin D correlates with lower risk. Should people take supplements?"

<div slot="solution">

**(a) Module 5 — [Bayesian Fine-mapping](/deep_dives/statgen-bayesian-fine-mapping/).** The
lead SNP is the most significant marker, which is not the same as the causal variant; Module 2
explains why they usually differ. Reaching for Module 4 here means reading a marginal p-value
as a causal claim, which is the single most common error in the field.

**(b) Module 5 — [Polygenic Risk Scores](/deep_dives/statgen-polygenic-risk-scores/).** The
score is not broken; its transferability assumption failed. Effect sizes, LD and allele
frequencies all differ across ancestries, and the score was fitted in one of them. Module 3
would tell you the trait's heritability, which is not what changed.

**(c) Module 4 —
[Association & Linear Mixed Models](/deep_dives/statgen-association-linear-mixed-models/),
then [LDSC](/deep_dives/statgen-ldsc-sldsc/).** An inflation factor above 1 is what a truly
polygenic trait *should* produce, so λ alone cannot answer the question. The LD score
regression intercept separates the two, and dividing every statistic by λ would deflate a
genuine polygenic signal.

**(d) Module 4 —
[Rare Variant Association](/deep_dives/statgen-rare-variant-association/).** Individually
they are far below the power cliff — a variant seen a handful of times cannot reach
$5 \times 10^{-8}$ at any effect size worth having. Aggregate them to the gene.

**(e) Module 5 —
[Mendelian Randomisation](/deep_dives/statgen-mendelian-randomization/).** A correlation in
observational data supports no recommendation. Module 4 can establish that variants near the
vitamin D pathway associate with risk; only an instrumental-variable argument gets from there
to what a supplement would do.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Where does the next million people go?" difficulty="stretch">

A consortium can add one million samples to a trait with $h^2 = 0.30$ and
$M = 1{,}000{,}000$, currently at $N = 500{,}000$.

(a) Compute the score's $R^2$ before and after, using the formula from the worked example.

(b) Suppose instead the million samples were spent on deep phenotyping of the existing
500,000, halving measurement error and thereby raising the *measured* $h^2$ from 0.30 to 0.36.
Compute $R^2$ at $N = 500{,}000$ with the new heritability.

(c) Which investment bought more? State the condition on the current $N$ under which the
answer flips — and say why a consortium might still choose the option that buys less $R^2$.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** At $N = 500{,}000$: $R^2 = 0.30/(1 + 10^6/150{,}000) = 0.039130$, or 3.91%. At
$N = 1{,}500{,}000$: $R^2 = 0.093103$, or 9.31%. The million samples buy **5.40 percentage
points**.

**(b)** With $h^2 = 0.36$ at the original $N = 500{,}000$:
$R^2 = 0.36/(1 + 10^6/180{,}000) = 0.054915$, or 5.49% — **1.58 percentage points**.

**(c)** More samples wins, 9.31% against 5.49%. But the ordering is not permanent: solving
$R^2(N + 10^6,\, h^2{=}0.30) = R^2(N,\, h^2{=}0.36)$ gives a crossover at
$N \approx 1{,}595{,}256$. Above that, a fixed million extra samples buys a shrinking increment
— the curve is flattening — while a higher ceiling lifts every point on it.

A consortium might still take the smaller $R^2$. Better phenotyping raises $h^2$ for *every*
analysis the cohort supports, not just this score, and it is the only one of the two options
that improves results already collected. Extra samples of the same ancestry, meanwhile, do
nothing at all for the transferability problem in Exercise 2(b).

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- The curriculum is five modules: the **arithmetic of evidence**, the **structure of
  populations**, the **size of the genetic signal**, the **discovery of positions**, and the
  **inferences the positions support**. They depend on each other in that order.
- **Discovery and prediction are different problems.** One common variant explaining 0.105%
  of a trait is found at $N = 37{,}716$; the whole score built at that sample size explains
  0.34% of the trait, and reaching half of $h^2$ costs 88 times more people.
- **Heritability is a ceiling, not a target.** $R^2 = h^2/(1 + M/(N h^2))$ approaches $h^2$
  and never reaches it, so the last increment of accuracy is always the most expensive.
- **Instrument strength and discovery significance are the same number.** For a single
  variant $F = Nq^2$, which is its association $\chi^2$ — 39.60 here — so anything
  genome-wide significant is automatically a strong instrument, while still contributing
  almost nothing to a polygenic score.
- **Every method here returns a number whether or not its assumptions hold.** The comparison
  table's last column, not its first, is what decides whether a method applies.
