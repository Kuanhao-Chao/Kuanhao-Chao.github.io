## The question this lesson answers

The previous lesson estimated heritability by comparing people who differ in relatedness —
twins, or unrelated individuals with a genomic relationship matrix
<Citation id="yang2011gcta" />. Every one of those comparisons is **between families**, and a between-family comparison contains more than the
direct effect of the genotype on the person carrying it.

It contains at least three other things. Parents who resemble each other make offspring whose
parents' contributions are correlated, which changes the variance without changing any allele
frequency. Parents also build the environment their children grow up in, and they build it
partly with their own genotypes. And families differ in ancestry, which the previous lesson on
population structure showed is worth quantifying rather than assuming away.

That is the concrete form of a warning the heritability lesson made in general terms
<Citation id="visscher2008" />: the ratio is a property of a population, and a population
contains structure that the ratio does not distinguish from genetics.

All three are removed by one design: compare siblings with each other. This lesson works out
what each of the three is worth, so that the difference between a population estimate and a
within-family estimate becomes a number rather than a worry.

## Assortative mating changes the variance without changing the alleles

People do not choose partners at random with respect to height, education or almost any other
trait. Spousal correlations of 0.2 to 0.4 are ordinary, and for education they run higher.

<Citation id="fisher1918infinitesimal" /> worked out the consequence in the paper that founded
the field. The key observation is negative: **assortment does not change allele frequencies.**
It changes who mates with whom, not which alleles exist at what rate, so the variance
generated afresh each generation by Mendelian segregation is untouched. Under random mating
that segregation variance is $V_{A_0}/2$, and it stays $V_{A_0}/2$ forever.

What does change is the covariance between the two parental contributions. Writing the
offspring's breeding value as the mid-parent value plus a segregation term,

$$
V_A = \frac{V_A + V_A + 2\rho_A V_A}{4} + \frac{V_{A_0}}{2}
= \frac{V_A(1+\rho_A)}{2} + \frac{V_{A_0}}{2}
$$

which rearranges to

$$
V_A = \frac{V_{A_0}}{1 - \rho_A}
$$

The correlation between mates' *breeding values* follows from the correlation between their
phenotypes: regressing $A$ on $P$ has slope $h^2$, so
$\mathrm{Cov}(A_s, A_d) = h^4\mu V_P$ and therefore $\rho_A = \mu h^2$.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The h² in that formula is not the one you started with">

It is tempting to write $V_A = V_{A_0}/(1 - \mu h_0^2)$ and stop. That is wrong, and it
understates the answer, because assortment raises $h^2$ — which raises $\rho_A$, which raises
$V_A$ again. The relation is implicit and has to be solved.

Substituting $h^2 = V_A/(V_A + V_E)$ turns it into a quadratic in $V_A$:

$$
(1-\mu)V_A^2 + (V_E - V_{A_0})V_A - V_{A_0}V_E = 0
$$

At $h_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the linear term vanishes and the root is exactly
$\sqrt{V_{A_0}V_E/(1-\mu)}$.

</Callout>

**The naive answer.** $1/(1 - 0.4 \times 0.5) = 1.25$, giving $V_A = 0.625$.

**The right one.** With $V_{A_0} = V_E = 0.5$ the linear term is zero, so

$$
V_A = \sqrt{\frac{V_{A_0}V_E}{1-\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{0.6}} = 0.645497
$$

a ratio of $0.645497/0.5 = 1.290994$ — **exactly $\sqrt{5/3}$**. Heritability rises with it,
to $0.645497/1.145497 = 0.563508$, and the breeding-value correlation between mates settles at
$\rho_A = 0.4 \times 0.563508 = 0.225403$.

**Check it against the implicit relation.** $1/(1-0.225403) = 1.290994$, the same ratio — which
is what "solved self-consistently" means.

**Simulation.** An infinitesimal model with 200,000 individuals reaches 0.64907 after forty
generations, within 0.6% of the closed form, with a realised spousal correlation of 0.39907
and a sibling breeding-value correlation of 0.61405.

</WorkedExample>

## What that does to a twin study

Falconer's estimator, derived in the heritability lesson, is $h^2 = 2(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}})$
and $c^2 = 2r_{\mathrm{DZ}} - r_{\mathrm{MZ}}$. Both rest on dizygotic twins sharing exactly
half their additive variance.

Under assortment they share more. The correlation between full sibs' breeding values is

$$
r^{A}_{\mathrm{DZ}} = \frac{1+\rho_A}{2}
$$

which at $\rho_A = 0.225403$ is $0.612702$ rather than $0.5$. So $r_{\mathrm{DZ}}$ rises, the
$\mathrm{MZ}-\mathrm{DZ}$ gap shrinks, and both halves of the decomposition move:

$$
\hat{h}^2 = h^2(1-\rho_A), \qquad \hat{c}^2 = h^2\rho_A
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="The direction most summaries get backwards">

Assortative mating is usually described as inflating heritability estimates. It does inflate
the **true** heritability — that is the whole of the previous section. What it does to the
**twin estimate** of that heritability is the opposite: it deflates it, and books the
difference as shared environment.

At $h^2 = 0.563508$ and $\rho_A = 0.225403$ the twin design reports $\hat{h}^2 = 0.436492$
against a truth of $0.563508$, and $\hat{c}^2 = 0.127017$ where the truth is zero. Simulation
gives 0.4393 and 0.1268.

So a twin study of a strongly assorted trait will report a substantial shared-environment
component with no shared environment anywhere in the generating model. Family resemblance that
is entirely genetic gets attributed to upbringing.

</Callout>

## Genetic nurture: the parents' genotype in the child's environment

A parent's genotype affects the child twice. Half of it is transmitted, and acts directly. All
of it shapes the household — the books, the vocabulary, the neighbourhood — and acts on the
child through the environment. <Citation id="kong2018nurture" /> demonstrated the second
channel cleanly by showing that the alleles a parent did **not** transmit still predict the
child's educational attainment.

Model it as

$$
Y = \delta A_{\mathrm{child}} + \eta A_{\mathrm{mid}} + E
$$

A population regression of $Y$ on the child's breeding value picks up both terms, because the
child's value is correlated with the mid-parent value it half came from:

$$
\beta_{\mathrm{pop}} = \delta + \eta\,\frac{1+\rho_A}{2}
$$

A **sibling difference** cancels $A_{\mathrm{mid}}$ exactly — the two siblings have the same
parents — leaving

$$
\beta_{\mathrm{within}} = \delta
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="What a population estimate contains"
  given={[
    { label: 'Indirect over direct, η/δ', value: '0.50' },
    { label: 'ρ_A at equilibrium', value: '0.225403' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

$$
\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{pop}}}{\beta_{\mathrm{within}}} = 1 + 0.5 \times \frac{1.225403}{2} = 1.30635
$$

So a population GWAS overstates the direct effect by 31%. At $\eta = \delta$ the factor is
$1.6127$; at $\eta/\delta = 0.25$ it is $1.15318$. Under random mating the same expression
collapses to $1 + \eta/2\delta$, so the assortment contributes only the difference between
$(1+\rho_A)/2$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ — a modest part of the total, and the nurture term is the
larger effect.

Simulation with 200,000 sibling pairs recovers all three: 1.1562, 1.3070 and 1.6168 against
predictions of 1.15320, 1.30635 and 1.61270 — and $\beta_{\mathrm{within}}$ returns
0.9970, 0.9988 and 0.9971 against a true $\delta$ of 1, which is the point: **the
sibling-difference estimate is unbiased by construction, not approximately.**

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why the sibling difference is so clean">

Two siblings have the same parents, the same ancestry and the same household. Every term in
the model that is a property of the *family* rather than of the *individual* is identical for
both, so subtracting one from the other removes it exactly — not approximately, not
conditionally on a covariate being correctly specified, but algebraically.

What remains is Mendelian segregation: the coin-flips that decided which of each parent's two
alleles went to which child. That randomisation is the closest thing genetics has to a
randomised trial <Citation id="young2018relatedness" />, and it is why within-family estimates
carry a causal warrant that population estimates do not.

</Callout>

## What it costs

The design is not free, and the cost is power.

Siblings share half their genome, so the variance available to a within-family regression is
about half of what a population sample offers — and under assortment it is
$(1-\rho_A)/2 = 0.387298$ of the population additive variance rather than one half, because
assortment inflates the denominator while leaving segregation variance alone. That figure is
exact: $1-\rho_A$ is $V_{A_0}/V_A = \sqrt{0.6}$ here, so the surviving fraction is
$\sqrt{0.15}$. Recovering the same standard error therefore takes on the order of two and a
half times as many individuals,
and they have to come in sibling pairs, which no biobank has in unlimited supply.

That is the trade <Citation id="okbay2022polygenic" /> and others make explicitly: population
estimates for discovery, where power is scarce and some confounding is tolerable, and
within-family estimates for anything that has to be causal. It is the same division of labour
that Mendelian randomisation makes in Module 5, and for the same reason.

<Exercise n={1} title="A more heritable, more assorted trait" difficulty="warm-up">

A trait has $h_0^2 = 0.8$ and a spousal correlation of 0.4. (a) What is the equilibrium
additive variance, taking the pre-assortment phenotypic variance as 1? (b) What is the
equilibrium heritability? (c) Compare the inflation with the $h_0^2 = 0.5$ case.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $V_{A_0} = 0.8$, $V_E = 0.2$, $a = 0.6$, $b = 0.2 - 0.8 = -0.6$, $c = -0.16$. Then
$V_A = [0.6 + \sqrt{0.36 + 0.384}]/1.2 = [0.6 + 0.862554]/1.2 = 1.218795$.

**(b)** $h^2 = 1.218795/(1.218795 + 0.2) = 0.859035$.

**(c)** The ratio is $1.218795/0.8 = 1.523494$, against $1.290994$ at $h_0^2 = 0.5$. Assortment
compounds: a more heritable trait has a larger $\rho_A = \mu h^2$ for the same $\mu$, which
inflates $V_A$ more, which raises $h^2$ further. The effect is strongest exactly where
heritability was already high, which is why height and education — both heritable and both
strongly assorted — are the traits where this matters most.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Reading a twin study that reports shared environment" difficulty="core">

A twin study of educational attainment reports $\hat{h}^2 = 0.40$ and $\hat{c}^2 = 0.18$. The
spousal correlation for education is about 0.5. (a) If all of the apparent shared environment
were an assortment artefact, what would the true heritability be? (b) What would $\rho_A$ have
to be? (c) Is that consistent with $\mu = 0.5$?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** With $\hat{h}^2 = h^2(1-\rho_A)$ and $\hat{c}^2 = h^2\rho_A$, the two sum to $h^2$
regardless of $\rho_A$: $h^2 = 0.40 + 0.18 = 0.58$.

**(b)** $\rho_A = \hat{c}^2/h^2 = 0.18/0.58 = 0.310345$.

**(c)** $\rho_A = \mu h^2 = 0.5 \times 0.58 = 0.29$, close to the 0.310345 required. So the
whole of the reported shared environment is consistent with assortment and none of it need be
environmental at all.

That is not proof that it is — genuine shared environment exists, and separating the two needs
a design that does not rely on the MZ/DZ contrast. But a twin study on its own cannot
distinguish them, and reporting $c^2$ for a strongly assorted trait without saying so
overstates what the design can support.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="How much nurture would explain the gap?" difficulty="core">

A within-family GWAS of educational attainment finds effect sizes about 60% of the population
ones. Taking $\rho_A = 0.29$: (a) what $\eta/\delta$ would account for that entirely?
(b) What else could account for it?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\beta_{\mathrm{pop}}/\beta_{\mathrm{within}} = 1/0.6 = 1.666667$. Setting
$1 + (\eta/\delta)(1.29/2) = 1.666667$ gives $\eta/\delta = 0.666667/0.645 = 1.033592$ — an
indirect effect slightly *larger* than the direct one.

**(b)** Population stratification is the other candidate, and it produces exactly the same
signature: a between-family association that a sibling comparison removes. The previous
lesson's machinery is what separates them — residual stratification should scale with $N F_{ST}\delta^2$
and be visible in the LD score intercept, while genetic nurture should not.

A third possibility is that the within-family estimate is itself attenuated, since sibling
differences have lower variance and any genotyping error is proportionally larger relative to
the signal. All three are usually present, which is why attributing the whole gap to nurture
is a claim rather than a measurement.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="The cost of the design" difficulty="stretch">

You have 500,000 unrelated individuals and can instead recruit sibling pairs. (a) Roughly how
many pairs match the population design's power for a direct effect, at
$\rho_A = 0.225403$? (b) Why might you still prefer the smaller within-family study?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The within-family additive variance available is $(1-\rho_A)/2 = \sqrt{0.15} = 0.387298$ of the
population value, and each pair contributes one difference. Power scales with the product of
sample size and variance explained, so matching it needs roughly
$500{,}000/0.387299 \approx 1{,}291{,}000$ individuals in pairs — about 645,000 pairs, which
is more sibling pairs than exist in any current biobank.

**(b)** Because the two designs estimate different things. The population study estimates
$\delta + \eta(1+\rho_A)/2$ with a standard error; the within-family study estimates $\delta$.
If the question is "what would happen if we changed this genotype", only the second is an
answer, and a precise estimate of the wrong quantity is not progress. If the question is
"which loci are worth investigating", the first is the right tool and its confounding is
tolerable.

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Assortment changes no allele frequencies**, so segregation variance is untouched at
  $V_{A_0}/2$ and the whole effect runs through the correlation between parental
  contributions.
- **The equilibrium is $V_{A_0}/(1-\rho_A)$ with $\rho_A = \mu h^2$**, solved
  self-consistently because $h^2$ is itself raised. At $h_0^2 = \tfrac12$ and $\mu = 0.4$ the
  answer is exactly $\sqrt{5/3} = 1.290994$; the naive $1/(1-\mu h_0^2) = 1.25$ understates it.
- **Equilibrium takes about ten generations.** A population whose mating patterns changed
  recently is at neither value.
- **Twin studies are biased in the direction most summaries reverse.** Dizygotic twins share
  $(1+\rho_A)/2 = 0.612702$ of their additive variance, so $\hat{h}^2 = h^2(1-\rho_A) = 0.436492$
  understates a true 0.563508 and $\hat{c}^2 = h^2\rho_A = 0.127017$ appears where no shared
  environment exists.
- **Genetic nurture is separable and computable**: a population regression returns
  $\delta + \eta(1+\rho_A)/2$ where a sibling difference returns $\delta$, so an indirect
  effect half the size of the direct one inflates the population estimate by 1.30635.
- **The sibling difference removes all of it algebraically**, because everything that is a
  property of the family cancels. What is left is Mendelian segregation — the nearest thing
  genetics has to randomisation.
- **The cost is power**: only $(1-\rho_A)/2 = \sqrt{0.15} = 0.387298$ of the additive variance survives the
  differencing, so matching a population study takes roughly two and a half times the people,
  in pairs.
