## The question this page answers

The previous page benchmarked predictors against an assay, where the truth was measured. Here
the truth is *inferred*: nobody has demonstrated which non-coding variant at a GWAS locus is
causal, so a benchmark has to decide, and it decides using fine-mapping.

That makes the benchmark's construction part of its result in a way that a DMS benchmark's is
not — and the construction also forces the choice of metric.

<Callout variant="intuition">
Causal variants are rare. At a typical GWAS locus, dozens or hundreds of variants are
associated and one or a few are responsible; genome-wide, the fraction of variants that do
anything is tiny.

A benchmark that reflected that honestly would be almost entirely negatives, and a model that
answered "no" to everything would score superbly on any metric that rewards being right on
average. So benchmarks build a *matched* set instead — each putative causal variant against a
fixed number of controls — and that ratio is a design choice which then determines what the
scores mean.

The consequence is the thing most often skipped: **an AUPRC has no fixed interpretation.** Its
floor is the fraction of positives, so 0.38 is good on one benchmark and poor on another, and
AUROC — which does have a fixed floor of 0.5 — is exactly the metric that hides the problem.
</Callout>

## How the labels are made

**<Dataset id="traitgym" />** builds two sets <Citation id="benegas2025traitgym" />. The
Mendelian half takes curated causal non-coding variants for **113 traits** from OMIM — 338 of
them, against 3,380 variants in total. The complex half takes **83 traits** from UK Biobank
fine-mapping: **1,140 causal** against 11,400 in total. Both are deliberately one positive to
nine controls.

The complex-trait positives are variants with a posterior inclusion probability above $0.9$ in
a credible set *and* genome-wide significance at $5\times10^{-8}$; controls are variants with
PIP below $0.01$ in every trait. The gap between those thresholds is doing real work — it
excludes the ambiguous middle rather than forcing it into one class or the other.

**<Dataset id="ukb-finemapping" />** is where those posteriors come from
<Citation id="wu2026finemapping" />, computed by methods such as SuSiE
<Citation id="wang2020susie" /> and FINEMAP <Citation id="benner2016finemap" />. A benchmark
built this way inherits everything the fine-mapping got wrong — most importantly its
sensitivity to a mismatch between the LD reference panel and the study population, which can
move a credible set onto the wrong variant entirely.

It also inherits a subtler assumption. A posterior inclusion probability is normalised across
the variants at a locus, so unless the null is given explicit prior mass $\pi_0$ — the
probability that the locus contains *no* causal variant at all — the posteriors are
conditioned on one being there and must sum to one regardless. At a locus where the signal is
in fact driven by something unmodelled, that normalisation manufactures a high PIP for
whichever variant fits best. Positives selected at PIP above 0.9 therefore carry a residue of
loci where the truth was "none of these", and no threshold on PIP alone can remove it.

**GeneticsGym** is the coding-variant counterpart, evaluating causal-variant prediction for
complex traits restricted to protein-coding variation <Citation id="benegas2025traitgym" />.
The split matters: a model that does well on coding variants has not thereby shown anything
about regulatory ones, and the two benchmarks exist separately for that reason.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Two things worth correcting about how this is usually described">
**The retention criterion is not agreement between fine-mappers.** It is often stated that
variants enter the benchmark when SuSiE and FINEMAP agree on the PIP to within some
tolerance. That describes a filter applied upstream, when the fine-mapping resource itself was
built. TraitGym's own criterion is the one above: PIP above 0.9 with genome-wide significance
for positives, PIP below 0.01 for controls. Merging the two makes the benchmark sound more
conservative than it is.

**The one-to-nine ratio is not incidental.** It is what fixes the AUPRC baseline at 0.1, and
without it the reported AUPRC values cannot be read at all. A benchmark's positive rate belongs
next to its scores.
</Callout>

## Why the metric follows from the design

<WorkedExample
  title="One ranking, two verdicts"
  given={[
    { label: 'Examples', value: '100 variants: 10 causal, 90 controls' },
    { label: 'The model', value: 'ranks the bulk well, but eight controls outrank every positive' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the ROC verdict.**

$$
\text{AUROC} = 0.9111
$$

Against a floor of $0.5$, that reads as a strong model — the sort of number that appears in an
abstract.

**Step 2 — the precision-recall verdict.**

$$
\text{AUPRC} = 0.3782, \qquad \text{baseline} = \pi = \frac{10}{100} = 0.1000
$$

**Step 3 — look at the top of the ranking.** The eight highest-scoring variants are all
controls, so the first true positive is the **ninth** prediction:

$$
P@8 = \frac{0}{8} = 0.0000, \qquad
P@9 = \frac{1}{9} = 0.1111, \qquad
P@10 = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2000
$$

Precision at the point where the first hit arrives is $0.1111$ — indistinguishable from
picking at random.

**Step 4 — see why the two disagree.** Eight false positives out of ninety controls is a false
positive rate of under nine per cent, and ROC measures exactly that: a small horizontal step.
Precision measures how many of the *predictions you would act on* are right, and by that
measure the model has failed completely at the top of its own ranking
<Citation id="davis2006prroc" />.

**Step 5 — which one describes the use.** Nobody follows up all hundred variants. They follow
up the top few, which is the region ROC is least sensitive to and precision-recall is entirely
about <Citation id="saito2015prplot" />.

</WorkedExample>

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="What a score is worth against its floor" difficulty="warm-up">

The model above scores AUROC 0.9111 and AUPRC 0.3782. Express each relative to the floor a
random predictor would achieve on the same set, and say which comparison is more informative.

<div slot="solution">

A random ranking gives $\text{AUROC} = 0.5$ regardless of the class balance, and
$\text{AUPRC} = \pi = 0.1$ on a one-to-nine set.

$$
\frac{0.9111}{0.5} = 1.8222, \qquad \frac{0.3782}{0.1} = 3.7820
$$

By the ratio the model looks *better* on precision-recall — nearly four times random against
under twice. But the ratio is the wrong comparison for AUROC, whose useful range is the
half-interval above 0.5 rather than the whole interval above 0.

The informative statement is the absolute one: the model recovers 38% of the area under a
perfect precision-recall curve on a problem where 10% is free, and its first eight predictions
are wrong. Neither ratio conveys that, which is why benchmarks report AUPRC *and* its baseline
rather than a normalised score.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="What a follow-up budget actually buys" difficulty="core">

A laboratory can validate ten variants. Using the same ranking, how many causal variants do
they find, and how does that change at eighteen?

<div slot="solution">

$$
P@10 = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2000
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{two hits from ten experiments}
$$

$$
P@18 = \frac{10}{18} = 0.5556
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{all ten, from eighteen experiments}
$$

The eight false positives sit entirely at the top, so the first ten experiments are spent
clearing them and the next eight find everything. A budget of ten yields two hits; a budget
of eighteen yields ten.

This is the practical content of the AUPRC-versus-AUROC difference, and it is invisible in an
AUROC of 0.9111. It also shows why precision at a *fixed, realistic k* is often the most
honest single number a benchmark can report — it corresponds to a decision somebody actually
makes, whereas an area under a curve integrates over operating points nobody will use.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Why AUPRC values do not transfer between benchmarks" difficulty="stretch">

Keep the model and the negative distribution fixed and enlarge the control set from nine
controls per positive to twenty-seven, then to ninety-nine. What happens to each metric, and
what follows for comparing published scores?

<div slot="solution">

Drawing the additional controls from the same distribution — so the fraction of high-scoring
controls is unchanged — gives:

| positives : controls | baseline $\pi$ | AUROC | AUPRC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 : 9 | 0.1000 | 0.9111 | 0.3782 |
| 1 : 27 | 0.0357 | 0.9111 | 0.1786 |
| 1 : 99 | 0.0100 | 0.9111 | 0.0579 |

**AUROC does not move at all.** It is built from true and false positive *rates*, each
normalised within its own class, so multiplying the number of controls changes neither.

**AUPRC falls by more than a factor of six.** Precision is positives over predicted-positives,
and adding controls adds candidates for the denominator at every threshold. Nothing about the
model changed.

Three consequences:

- **An AUPRC without its baseline is unreadable.** 0.0579 on the 1:99 set is the same model as
  0.3782 on the 1:9 set.
- **AUPRC values from different benchmarks are not comparable**, even for the same task, unless
  the designs match. This is a real hazard when a paper compares its number to one published
  against a different control set.
- **AUROC's stability here is not a virtue.** It is stable because it is insensitive to the
  thing that changed, and that thing — how many candidates you must sift to find a causal
  variant — is precisely the difficulty of the real problem.

The honest reading is that AUROC measures separability and AUPRC measures separability *at the
prevalence you built*, so the benchmark's design has to be quoted with the score.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **The labels are inferred, not measured.** Causal status comes from fine-mapping, so a
  benchmark inherits the fine-mapping's assumptions — including its sensitivity to LD
  reference mismatch.
- **TraitGym is 338 causal over 113 Mendelian traits (3,380 total) and 1,140 over 83 complex
  traits (11,400 total)**, at a deliberate one-to-nine ratio. Positives need PIP above 0.9 and
  genome-wide significance; controls need PIP below 0.01 in every trait, which excludes the
  ambiguous middle rather than assigning it.
- **The ratio fixes the AUPRC baseline at 0.1**, and that baseline is part of the result.
- **AUROC and AUPRC can disagree completely.** The same ranking scored 0.9111 and 0.3782,
  because eight controls outranked every causal variant — a small step on a ROC axis and a
  total failure at the top of the list.
- **The top of the list is where decisions are made.** Ten experiments returned two hits;
  eighteen returned all ten. Precision at a realistic k often says more than either area.
- **AUROC is invariant to the positive-to-control ratio and AUPRC is not**: 0.3782, 0.1786 and
  0.0579 are one unchanged model at 1:9, 1:27 and 1:99. Published AUPRCs are not comparable
  across benchmarks with different designs.
