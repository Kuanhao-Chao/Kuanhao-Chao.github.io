## The question this lesson answers

A GWAS reports associations at ten million variants. The array it ran on typed perhaps
650,000. Where did the other 94% come from, and how much should you trust them?

The short answer is that they were inferred, one chromosome at a time, from a panel of
sequenced haplotypes — and that the inference comes with a per-variant confidence which
almost nobody reads carefully enough. This lesson is about reading it.

<Callout variant="intuition">

Imputation works for the same reason a GWAS works at all: nearby variants travel together.
A chromosome you have just typed is not a random string. It is a **patchwork of stretches
that already exist in the reference panel**, spliced at the places where its ancestors
recombined. If you can work out which panel haplotype each stretch was copied from, you can
read off every variant in that stretch — including the ones your array never touched.

</Callout>

## What the array measures, and what it does not

A genotyping array carries probes for a fixed, chosen set of sites. The choice is the point:
sites are picked so that the rest of the genome is *predictable* from them, using the
haplotype block structure mapped by <Citation id="gabriel2002" /> and catalogued by
<Citation id="hapmap2005" />. A well-chosen tag captures its neighbours; a badly-chosen one
captures nothing.

Three consequences follow, and all three are permanent for that dataset:

- **Nothing off the array and off the panel exists.** A variant absent from both is not rare
  in your results — it is missing from them.
- **Array content is ancestry-specific.** Tag choices that work in one population work less
  well in another, because the blocks are not the same.
- **Batch is confounded with everything.** Two arrays, or two versions of one array, do not
  fail in the same places. This is a quality-control problem, treated in the next lesson.

## The copying model

The model that fills the gaps <Citation id="listephens2003" /> describes a new haplotype as a
mosaic of the reference haplotypes. Walking along the chromosome, a hidden state says which
panel haplotype is currently being copied, and two parameters govern the walk:

- a **switch rate**, controlling how often copying jumps to a different panel haplotype —
  this is recombination, so the rate follows the genetic map rather than physical distance;
- a **mismatch rate**, allowing the copy to differ from its template — this absorbs mutation
  since the common ancestor, and genotyping error.

Given the typed sites, standard forward-backward inference returns, for every untyped site, a
posterior over the three genotypes. Everything modern imputation software does
<Citation id="das2016" /><Citation id="browning2018" /> is an efficiency argument on top of
that model, not a different one.

The panel matters more than the algorithm. <Citation id="tgp2015" /> made genome-wide
imputation routine; larger and deeper panels <Citation id="taliun2021" /> push accuracy down
to rarer variants, because a rare allele can only be imputed if some panel haplotype carries
it.

## Reading an imputed variant

<WorkedExample
  title="Eight individuals at one imputed site, and what the site is worth"

**Step 1 — from posterior to dosage.** Imputation returns three probabilities per person, not
a genotype. The dosage is their expected value:

$$
D_i = P(g_i = 1) + 2\,P(g_i = 2) .
$$

| individual | P(0) | P(1) | P(2) | dosage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.98 | 0.02 | 0.00 | 0.02 |
| 2 | 0.95 | 0.05 | 0.00 | 0.05 |
| 3 | 0.10 | 0.85 | 0.05 | 0.95 |
| 4 | 0.05 | 0.90 | 0.05 | 1.00 |
| 5 | 0.60 | 0.35 | 0.05 | 0.45 |
| 6 | 0.02 | 0.18 | 0.80 | 1.78 |
| 7 | 0.99 | 0.01 | 0.00 | 0.01 |
| 8 | 0.30 | 0.60 | 0.10 | 0.80 |

Individual 5 is the interesting one. A hard call would round 0.45 to a reference homozygote
and throw away the 35% chance of a heterozygote; the dosage keeps it, which is why
association software takes dosages.

**Step 2 — the frequency, and the variance a perfect call would have.** The mean dosage is
$0.632500$, so $\hat\theta = 0.316250$ and a perfectly-called variant at that frequency would
have genotype variance

$$
2\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta) = 0.432472 .
$$

**Step 3 — the variance actually present.** The dosages themselves vary by

$$
\mathrm{Var}(D) = 0.339494 ,
$$

which is less, because uncertainty pulls every dosage toward the mean. That shortfall *is*
the missing information.

**Step 4 — the metric.**

$$
\hat{r}^2 = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(D)}{2\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)}
= \frac{0.339494}{0.432472} = 0.785008 .
$$

**Step 5 — what it is worth.** The quantity is a ratio of variances, and variance is what an
association test consumes, so it acts as a **multiplier on the effective sample size**:

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\text{variant}} = N_{\mathrm{eff}} \times \hat{r}^2 .
$$

In the 32,000-effective-sample study from the previous lesson, this variant is worth
$32{,}000 \times 0.785008 \approx 25{,}120$ effective samples. Three variants at $\hat{r}^2$ of 0.95, 0.55 and 0.32
are worth 30,400, 17,600 and 10,240 — and since $q^2 \geq 39.60/N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the
smallest effects they can detect are $1.3027 \times 10^{-3}$, $2.2501 \times 10^{-3}$ and
$3.8673 \times 10^{-3}$. Same study, same threshold, a threefold spread in what is findable.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="The metric can exceed 1, and that is not a bug">

$\hat{r}^2$ compares an observed variance with an expected one, and in a small sample the
observed variance can land above $2\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)$ by chance. Rounding the eight
dosages above to hard calls gives $\hat{r}^2 = 1.127273$ — a number that cannot mean
"112% of the information". Implementations cap the metric at 1, and it is only stable in
samples large enough for the denominator to be a fair expectation. Never compute it on a
handful of people and believe the result, which is exactly what this worked example just
did for the sake of showing the arithmetic.

</Callout>

## The filter is not ancestry-neutral

An INFO threshold looks like a uniform quality bar and behaves like a selective one. Two
biases run in the same direction.

**Rare variants impute worse.** A rare allele is carried by few panel haplotypes, so there is
less evidence for the copy that contains it. Imputation quality therefore falls as frequency
falls — and, since rare variants are where the larger effects tend to sit, a fixed INFO cut
removes preferentially the variants a study would most want to find.

**Distance from the panel costs quality.** Imputation is only as good as the match between
the target haplotypes and the reference. A cohort whose ancestry is poorly represented in the
panel gets systematically lower INFO across the genome, loses more variants to the same
filter, and ends up with a smaller effective study than its headcount suggests. This is one
of the concrete mechanisms behind the unequal performance of genetic results across
populations <Citation id="martin2019" />, and it is decided before any analysis is run.

Neither problem is fixed by lowering the threshold, which just trades false negatives for
noise. They are fixed by a better-matched panel.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A dosage that is not a genotype" difficulty="warm-up">

An individual has posteriors $P(0) = 0.20$, $P(1) = 0.50$, $P(2) = 0.30$.

(a) What is the dosage?

(b) What would a hard call be, and how much information does it discard?

(c) Two individuals have dosages 1.10 and 1.10. Must they have the same posteriors?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $D = 0.50 + 2(0.30) = 1.10$.

**(b)** The most likely genotype is the heterozygote at $P = 0.50$, so a hard call gives 1.
That call is right half the time; the other half it is off by one in one of two directions.
The dosage keeps the asymmetry — 1.10 rather than 1 records that the alternate homozygote is
more likely than the reference homozygote.

**(c)** No. $(0.20, 0.50, 0.30)$ and $(0.45, 0.00, 0.55)$ both give $D = 1.10$, and they are
completely different states of knowledge — the second is confident that the individual is a
homozygote and unsure which. Dosage is a summary, and summarising loses this. It is also why
$\hat{r}^2$ is computed from the spread of dosages rather than from the posteriors directly.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Pricing the threshold" difficulty="core">

A study has $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 50{,}000$.

(a) A variant is imputed at $\hat{r}^2 = 0.45$. What is it worth, and what is the smallest
$q^2$ it can detect?

(b) What $\hat{r}^2$ would the study need for that variant to detect $q^2 = 10^{-3}$?

(c) The team proposes relaxing the filter from 0.3 to 0.2 to keep more variants. In units of
detectable effect, what does the marginal variant at 0.2 actually offer?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $50{,}000 \times 0.45 = 22{,}500$ effective samples, so
$q^2 \geq 39.600989/22{,}500 = 1.7600 \times 10^{-3}$.

**(b)** Solve $39.600989/(50{,}000\,\hat{r}^2) = 10^{-3}$, giving
$\hat{r}^2 = 0.792020$. A variant would have to be imputed nearly as well as it could be
typed.

**(c)** At $\hat{r}^2 = 0.2$ the variant is worth 10,000 effective samples and can detect
$q^2 \geq 3.9601 \times 10^{-3}$ — against $2.6401 \times 10^{-3}$ at the 0.3 threshold, and
$7.9202 \times 10^{-4}$ at perfect calling. So the variants the relaxation admits are ones
the study can only see if their effects are **five times** the study's floor. They are not
free: each one is another test in the multiple-testing burden, which the later lesson on
reading the output makes precise. Relaxing an INFO filter adds tests faster than it adds
power.

</div>
</Exercise>

Cohort A is well represented in the reference panel and has median $\hat{r}^2 = 0.90$ across
variants at 1% frequency. Cohort B is not, and has median $\hat{r}^2 = 0.55$ at the same
frequency. Both have 20,000 effective samples, and both apply INFO > 0.3.

(a) At a variant where each hits its median, what is each cohort's detection limit?

(b) How many effective samples would cohort B need to match cohort A's limit?

(c) The two are meta-analysed together and the locus does not replicate in B. Give two
explanations that have nothing to do with biology.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** A: $20{,}000 \times 0.90 = 18{,}000$, so $q^2 \geq 2.2001 \times 10^{-3}$.
B: $20{,}000 \times 0.55 = 11{,}000$, so $q^2 \geq 3.6001 \times 10^{-3}$ — a 64% higher bar
from imputation alone.

**(b)** B needs $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \times 0.55 = 18{,}000$, so $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 32{,}727$ —
it must recruit 1.64 times as many people to run the same analysis. That ratio is exactly
$0.90/0.55$, and it applies to the whole genome, not to one variant.

**(c)** First, **power**: B's effective study is smaller at every variant, so a true effect is
simply less likely to clear the threshold — a failure to replicate is the expected outcome,
not evidence against the locus. Second, **the filter is applied after imputation**, so B loses
more variants entirely; if the lead variant in A fell below 0.3 in B, B did not test it at
all, and "did not replicate" would mean "was never examined". Checking whether the variant is
present in B's results at all is the first thing to do, and it is skipped surprisingly often.

</div>

## Summary

- **Most variants in a GWAS were never measured.** They are inferred from a reference panel by
  a copying model that treats a new chromosome as a mosaic of panel haplotypes, switching at
  a recombination-driven rate and mismatching at a mutation-driven one.
- **The output is a posterior, summarised as a dosage** $D = P(1) + 2P(2)$. Hard-calling
  discards the uncertainty; association software takes dosages for that reason.
- **INFO is a ratio of variances**, $\hat{r}^2 = \mathrm{Var}(D)/2\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)$,
  and it behaves as a **multiplier on the effective sample size**. A variant at 0.32 in a
  32,000-effective study carries the information of 10,240.
- **An INFO filter is therefore a power decision.** The conventional 0.3 threshold costs a
  factor of 3.33 in the smallest detectable effect, and relaxing it adds multiple-testing
  burden faster than it adds power.
- **The filter is not applied evenly.** Quality falls with allele frequency and with distance
  from the panel's ancestry, so a fixed threshold removes preferentially the rarer variants
  and penalises cohorts the panel represents badly.
