## The question this lesson answers

Two lessons in this curriculum treat population structure as something to be corrected.
Association testing describes it as a mechanism that manufactures signal out of nothing, and
LD score regression offers a way to tell it apart from real polygenicity. Neither says **how
much structure there is**, and without that number three questions have no answers: whether a
given sample has enough of it to matter, whether principal components will find it, and how
much a scan is inflated if they do not.

This lesson supplies the quantity. It is one number, F_ST, and it turns out to sit at the
centre of a much larger story than "a correction to apply" — because the same F_ST that makes
ancestry visible to PCA is the F_ST that inflates every null test in the genome, and the two
effects scale with sample size in the same direction. Collecting more people makes the
confounding worse and the correction better at the same time.

## F_ST is a variance ratio, and the numerator is the interesting part

Take one variant and two populations. The allele frequency is $p_1$ in the first and $p_2$ in
the second, with mean $\bar{p}$. Wright's insight <Citation id="wright1931drift" /> was that the
right way to describe differentiation is not the raw gap $p_1 - p_2$ — which depends on where
in the frequency range you happen to be — but the gap as a fraction of the variance the
frequency could have had:

$$
F_{\mathrm{ST}} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(p)}{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}
$$

The denominator is the variance of a Bernoulli draw at the mean frequency, which is the most
variance a two-allele locus can carry. So F_ST is bounded in $[0, 1]$, it is 0 when the
populations have identical frequencies and 1 when each is fixed for a different allele, and —
crucially — it is comparable across variants of very different frequency. That last property
is what makes it a genome-wide summary rather than a per-variant curiosity.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="F_ST answers 'how surprised should I be that these two people differ?'">

Pick two chromosomes from the same population, and one from each of the two. Both pairs will
sometimes carry different alleles. F_ST is the *extra* probability of difference the second
draw gives you over the first, as a fraction of the second.

That is Hudson's form exactly, and it is the one the estimator below uses:
$F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 1 - H_W/H_B$, where $H_W$ is the mean heterozygosity within a population
and $H_B = p_1(1-p_2) + p_2(1-p_1)$ is the chance two chromosomes drawn one from each differ —
which is the denominator $\hat{D}_{\mathrm{H}}$ below. So
$H_B/H_W = 1/(1-F_{\mathrm{ST}})$, and at an F_ST of 0.11 — roughly the divergence between
European and East Asian ancestries — that ratio is 1.123596: **a between-population draw finds
a difference 12.4% more often than a within-population one.**

<Callout variant="warning" title="Two conventions, and they differ by a factor of two here">

Wright's and Nei's version normalises by the *total* heterozygosity of the pooled sample,
$F_{\mathrm{ST}} = (H_T - H_S)/H_T$ with $H_T = 2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})$. That is a different
denominator: a pooled draw can take both chromosomes from the same population, so $H_T < H_B$
whenever the populations differ. On the two-variant example below, Hudson gives 0.166667 where
the pooled-total form gives 0.090909 — a factor of 1.83 for the same data.

Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. But **the ratio $1/(1-F_{\mathrm{ST}})$ is
$H_B/H_W$ under Hudson and something else under Nei**, so a heterozygosity ratio quoted without
its convention is not interpretable. This lesson uses Hudson's throughout.

</Callout>

It does **not** mean that 11% of variants differ between continents, and it does not mean two
people from different continents are 11% different. It is a statement about variance, and
variance is not the same currency as difference.

</Callout>

### Estimating it, and the sample size that is not the one you think

With real data you have counts, not frequencies, and the sample frequencies carry their own
sampling variance which inflates $(p_1 - p_2)^2$ even when the populations are identical.
Hudson's estimator <Citation id="hudson1992gene" /> subtracts it:

$$
\hat{N}_{\mathrm{H}} = (p_1-p_2)^2 - \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1-1} - \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2-1},
\qquad
\hat{D}_{\mathrm{H}} = p_1(1-p_2) + p_2(1-p_1)
$$

with $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = \hat{N}_{\mathrm{H}} / \hat{D}_{\mathrm{H}}$. The alternative in wide
use is the ANOVA estimator of <Citation id="weir1984fst" />, which conditions on observed
heterozygosity and is more sensitive to unequal sample sizes.

<Callout variant="warning" title="n is chromosomes, not individuals">

The correction terms divide by $n-1$ where $n$ is the number of **sampled chromosomes**. Pass
diploid individual counts instead and the correction is twice as large as it should be, which
at a genuinely small F_ST drives the estimate negative. A negative F_ST is not by itself a
bug — an estimator that is unbiased at zero has to be able to return one, and both estimators
here go slightly below zero at zero divergence, by an amount that shrinks as $1/n$. But a
*systematically* negative genome-wide estimate is almost always this.

</Callout>

### Pool the numerators, not the ratios

The choice that matters more than the estimator is what you do across variants.
<Citation id="bhatia2013fst" /> is the reference: pool the numerators, pool the denominators,
and divide **once**. Averaging per-variant ratios estimates a different quantity, because it
weights every variant equally regardless of how much information it carries, and low-frequency
variants have tiny denominators and enormous relative noise.

<WorkedExample
  title="Two variants, two answers, one of them wrong"
  given={[
    { label: 'Variant A', value: 'p₁ = 0.60, p₂ = 0.30' },
    { label: 'Variant B', value: 'p₁ = 0.05, p₂ = 0.10' },
    { label: 'Sample', value: '200 chromosomes per population' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"

**Variant A.** The raw gap squared is $0.30^2 = 0.09$. The corrections are
$0.6 \times 0.4 / 199$ and $0.3 \times 0.7 / 199$, together $0.002261$, so
$\hat{N}_{\mathrm{H}} = 0.087739$. The denominator is
$0.6 \times 0.7 + 0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.54$. That gives $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.162479$ —
against $0.09/0.54 = 0.166667$ if the correction is skipped.

**Variant B.** The gap squared is $0.05^2 = 0.0025$, the corrections total $0.000691$, so
$\hat{N}_{\mathrm{H}} = 0.001809$, and the denominator is
$0.05 \times 0.90 + 0.10 \times 0.95 = 0.140$. That gives $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.012922$.

**Pooled, the right way.** $(0.087739 + 0.001809)/(0.54 + 0.14) = 0.131688$.

**Averaged, the wrong way.** $(0.162479 + 0.012922)/2 = 0.087700$.

The wrong answer is low by 33.4%, on two variants. Across a genome the gap does not cancel —
it grows with the spread in per-variant divergence, because the mis-weighting is worst exactly
where the frequencies are most different. Extend the set to six variants spanning common and
rare, strongly and negligibly differentiated — frequency pairs 0.60/0.30, 0.50/0.46, 0.20/0.35,
0.05/0.10, 0.80/0.78 and 0.12/0.30, at 1,000 chromosomes per population — and the pooled
estimate is 0.064880 against an average of ratios of 0.055207, low by 14.9%.

</WorkedExample>

## Why a principal component finds ancestry at all

Standardise the genotype matrix the way <Citation id="patterson2006eigen" /> does: for marker
$j$ with pooled frequency $\bar{p}_j$, replace the genotype $g_{ij} \in \{0,1,2\}$ by
$x_{ij} = (g_{ij} - 2\bar{p}_j)/\sqrt{2\bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)}$, so every column has unit
variance and no marker dominates through frequency alone. Then form the $N \times N$ Gram
matrix $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}/M$ and take its eigenvectors — this is exactly what
EIGENSTRAT <Citation id="price2006eigenstrat" /> does.

Here is why structure shows up in it. For an individual in population 1 the expected
standardised genotype at marker $j$ is $d_j/\sqrt{2\bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)}$ with
$d_j = p_{1j} - p_{2j}$, and for population 2 it is the negative of that. So the
between-population mean vector contributes, per marker,

$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}[d_j^2]}{2\bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)}
= \frac{2 F_{\mathrm{ST}}\,\bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)}{2\bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)}
= F_{\mathrm{ST}}
$$

independent of the marker's frequency — which is the payoff from F_ST being a ratio. Summing
over $M$ markers and dividing by $M$, the Gram matrix picks up the rank-one term
$F_{\mathrm{ST}}\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{\top}$, where $\mathbf{s}$ is the ±1 population
indicator. Since $\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2 = N$, the eigenvalue of that term is

$$
\lambda = N F_{\mathrm{ST}}
$$

<Callout variant="key" title="The structure spike is linear in the sample size">

Every extra person adds to the spike. This is the formal version of the observation that
<Citation id="novembre2008genes" /> made visible — that a large enough European sample
resolves geography at the scale of a few hundred kilometres, at F_ST values around 0.001 that
no smaller study could see at all. Nothing about the populations changed; N did.

</Callout>

## The threshold below which there is nothing to find

The spike sits on top of noise, and the noise is not zero. For a matrix of independent
standardised entries the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix spread over the Marchenko–Pastur
interval, whose upper edge is $(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^2$ with $\gamma = N/M$ — the same edge that
decides how many components a single-cell experiment has, and the distribution whose
fluctuations <Citation id="johnstone2001largest" /> characterised.

A spike is visible only if it clears that edge. The Baik–Ben Arous–Péché transition
<Citation id="baik2005phase" /> makes this exact: the leading sample eigenvalue separates from
the bulk if and only if $\lambda > \sqrt{\gamma}$. Substituting $\lambda = N F_{\mathrm{ST}}$:

$$
N F_{\mathrm{ST}} > \sqrt{N/M}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
F_{\mathrm{ST}} > \frac{1}{\sqrt{NM}}
$$

The threshold is symmetric in $N$ and $M$: ten times the markers buys exactly what ten times
the people does.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The cliff is in the eigenvector, and a scree plot cannot see it">

Below the transition the squared overlap between the estimated leading eigenvector and the
true structure axis is **exactly zero** in the limit. Not small — zero. The leading principal
component is a random direction, and plotting PC1 against PC2 shows a featureless blob that
is not a faint version of the truth but an unrelated one.

Above the transition the overlap is $(1 - \gamma/\lambda^2)/(1 + \gamma/\lambda)$, and it
climbs steeply. The eigenvalue, meanwhile, leaves the bulk edge with zero derivative: 1% past
the transition it is still within 0.1% of the edge, while the overlap is already 0.2418 at 20%
past it. So the quantity a scree plot displays is precisely the one that says nothing at the
point where it matters.

</Callout>

The aspect ratio is $\gamma = 5000/500000 = 0.01$, so $\sqrt{\gamma} = 0.1$ and the
Marchenko–Pastur upper edge is $(1+0.1)^2 = 1.21$.

**The threshold.** $F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*} = 1/\sqrt{NM} = 1/\sqrt{2.5\times10^{9}} = 2\times10^{-5}$.
Equivalently, the spike at that F_ST is $\lambda = 5000 \times 2\times10^{-5} = 0.1$, which is
$\sqrt{\gamma}$ exactly — the two statements of the threshold are the same statement.

**Below it.** At $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 10^{-5}$ the spike is $\lambda = 0.05$. The leading
eigenvalue is pinned at 1.21, the overlap with the truth is 0, and no amount of care in the
plotting recovers the split.

**A realistic within-Europe value.** At $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.001$ the spike is $\lambda = 5$,
fifty times the threshold. The leading eigenvalue is $(1+5)(1+0.01/5) = 6.012$ and the overlap
is 0.997605. The component is essentially the population label.

**What it would take to go lower.** To reach $F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*} = 10^{-5}$ at the same
$N$ you need $M = 1/(N F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}) = 2{,}000{,}000$ markers — four times as many for
half the F_ST, because the threshold falls only as $1/\sqrt{M}$.

</WorkedExample>

## What structure does to an association test

Now give the two populations different trait means: $\delta$ phenotype standard deviations
apart, for reasons that have nothing to do with any genotype. Test a variant with **no
effect whatsoever**.

The covariance between genotype and trait is $d_j \sigma \delta / 2$ over the pooled
sample. The genotype variance is the within-population part plus the between part, which is
$\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})$ terms plus $d_j^2$ — and when the expectation is taken over markers
those denominators cancel against the numerator's $\mathbb{E}[d_j^2] = 2F_{\mathrm{ST}}\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})$
exactly. What survives is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\chi^2] = 1 + \frac{N F_{\mathrm{ST}}\,\delta^2}{4}
$$

to first order in F_ST. That cancellation is why one number summarises the whole genome
instead of each variant needing its own frequency gap.

Put the within-Europe divergence of 0.001 and a trait gap of a fifth of a standard deviation
into it. A study of 10,000 is inflated by a tenth — invisible on a quantile–quantile plot, and
harmless. The same populations at 100,000 give 2.00. At 1,000,000 the expected chi-square at a
variant with no effect whatsoever is 11.00: an eleven-fold inflation, at every null variant in
the genome, from a divergence small enough that the two groups would not be described as
different populations by anyone looking at them.

<Callout variant="key" title="The confounding grows with the cure">

Both the spike and the inflation are linear in $N$. A study large enough to resolve its own
structure by PCA is, by exactly the same token, a study large enough for that structure to
have inflated every null test in the genome. This is why the fix cannot be a constant divisor:
genomic control rescales all statistics by one factor, but the thing being rescaled grew with
the sample and is not the same shape as real polygenic signal. Distinguishing the two is what
LD score regression is for, and correcting it properly is what principal-component covariates
and mixed models are for.

</Callout>

The practical consequences — how many components to fit, how to diagnose residual inflation,
and when a correction over-corrects — belong to the workflow track and are covered in the
GWAS series' population-structure lesson. The derivation of what a mixed model does with the
same information is in
<Citation id="astle2009structure" />
and in this track's association lesson, which quotes the linear-in-N result proved here.

<Exercise n={1} title="Reading a differentiation" difficulty="warm-up">

A variant has $p_1 = 0.40$ and $p_2 = 0.44$, measured on 500 chromosomes in each population.
(a) Compute Hudson's estimate. (b) The same frequencies measured on 50 chromosomes per
population give what? (c) What does the comparison say about single-variant F_ST?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The gap squared is $0.04^2 = 0.0016$. The corrections are $0.40 \times 0.60/499 = 0.000481$
and $0.44 \times 0.56 / 499 = 0.000494$, together $0.000975$, so the numerator is $0.000625$.
The denominator is $0.40\times0.56 + 0.44\times0.60 = 0.224 + 0.264 = 0.488$. The estimate is
$0.001281$.

**(b)** With 50 chromosomes the corrections are $0.24/49 = 0.004898$ and $0.2464/49 = 0.005029$,
together $0.009927$ — 6.20 times the squared gap of 0.0016. The numerator is $-0.008327$ and the
estimate is $-0.017063$.

**(c)** A single variant carries almost no information about F_ST at realistic sample sizes.
The correction is $O(1/n)$ while the signal is $O(F_{\mathrm{ST}})$, so any variant with
$F_{\mathrm{ST}} \ll 1/n$ is dominated by its own sampling noise and individually returns
something negative about as often as not. F_ST is a genome-wide quantity by necessity, not by
convention — which is also why it must be pooled as a ratio of averages.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Will this study see its own structure?" difficulty="core">

A cohort of 800 individuals is genotyped at 45,000 markers. The two subpopulations it draws
from have $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.0002$. (a) Will PCA resolve them? (b) How many markers would
be needed? (c) How many individuals, at the original marker count?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*} = 1/\sqrt{800 \times 45000} = 1/\sqrt{3.6\times10^{7}} = 1.6667\times10^{-4}$.
The actual F_ST of $2\times10^{-4}$ is above it, so — just barely — yes. The spike is
$\lambda = 800 \times 0.0002 = 0.16$ against $\sqrt{\gamma} = \sqrt{800/45000} = 0.13333$,
a ratio of 1.20. The overlap is
$(1 - \gamma/\lambda^2)/(1 + \gamma/\lambda) = 0.305556/1.111111 = 0.275$: the component is
real but is more noise than signal, and would be a poor covariate.

Note what you cannot do here — read that number off Figure 1. That curve is drawn at
$\gamma = 0.1$ and gives 0.2418 at the same ratio of 1.20. The overlap is not a function of
$\lambda/\sqrt{\gamma}$ alone: writing $u = \lambda/\sqrt{\gamma}$ it is
$(1 - 1/u^2)/(1 + \sqrt{\gamma}/u)$, and only the first factor is universal. The threshold is
universal; the climb away from it is not.

**(b)** To put the threshold at, say, a quarter of the true F_ST — comfortably resolved —
you need $F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*} = 5\times10^{-5}$, so $NM = 4\times10^{8}$ and
$M = 500{,}000$ markers.

**(c)** Alternatively $N = 4\times10^{8}/45000 = 8{,}889$ individuals. Both routes work
because the threshold depends only on the product — which is worth noticing, because markers
are usually much cheaper than people.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The inflation, and what it is not" difficulty="core">

A study of 250,000 people is drawn from two groups with $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.0008$, whose
trait means differ by 0.15 standard deviations. (a) What is the expected chi-square at a null
variant? (b) A colleague proposes dividing every chi-square by that number. What does that
cost, and what does it fail to fix?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $1 + 250000 \times 0.0008 \times 0.15^2/4 = 1 + 200 \times 0.0225/4 = 1 + 1.125 = 2.125$.

**(b)** Dividing by 2.125 restores the mean of the null distribution, and for that reason it
will make a quantile–quantile plot look acceptable. It costs power everywhere: a real
association is divided by the same factor, so its chi-square falls by more than half. And it
fails to fix the thing that matters, because stratification is not a uniform lift. Variants
whose frequencies differ most between the groups are inflated most, and those are not
distributed at random across the genome — they cluster wherever the two ancestries have the
most distinct history. A constant divisor leaves the ranking of variants unchanged, which
means the most structure-driven variants stay at the top of the list.

Note also that the quantity computed here is the *mean* chi-square. λ_GC is built from the
median and rescaled by the median of the null χ²₁ distribution; for a non-central chi-square
the two are not equal, so they should not be quoted for one another.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="Why the threshold is symmetric" difficulty="stretch">

The detection threshold $F_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*} = 1/\sqrt{NM}$ treats individuals and markers
identically, which is surprising: one is a sample from a population and the other is a sample
from a genome. Explain where the symmetry comes from, and give a reason it must eventually
break in real data.

<div slot="solution">

**Where it comes from.** The spike grows as $\lambda = N F_{\mathrm{ST}}$ — linear in
individuals only. The noise, however, grows as $\sqrt{\gamma} = \sqrt{N/M}$, which rises with
$N$ too. Detection needs $N F_{\mathrm{ST}} > \sqrt{N/M}$, and the $N$ on both sides partly
cancels: $\sqrt{N} F_{\mathrm{ST}} > 1/\sqrt{M}$. So the *product* is what matters. Adding
people helps the signal linearly but also raises the noise floor as $\sqrt{N}$, leaving a net
$\sqrt{N}$; adding markers lowers the noise floor as $1/\sqrt{M}$ and does nothing to the
signal. The two happen to enter with the same exponent.

**Why it breaks.** The Marchenko–Pastur law assumes independent columns, and markers are not
independent — they are in linkage disequilibrium. The effective number of independent markers
saturates as $M$ grows past the number of independent LD blocks, so doubling the array beyond
that point buys nothing while doubling the cohort still buys $\sqrt{2}$. The symmetry is a
property of the idealised matrix, and the idealisation fails on exactly the axis where markers
are cheap.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} title="Two estimators, one dataset" difficulty="stretch">

You compute F_ST across 100,000 variants two ways and get 0.0421 by pooling and 0.0361 by
averaging per-variant ratios. (a) Which is the estimate to report? (b) What does the size of
the gap tell you about the variant set? (c) Would restricting to common variants shrink it?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** The pooled value, 0.0421. Averaging ratios weights each variant equally regardless of
how much information it carries.

**(b)** The gap is 14.3% of the pooled value. A gap that size says the variant set is
dominated in *count* by variants that contribute little to either sum — low-frequency
variants, whose denominators $p_1(1-p_2)+p_2(1-p_1)$ are small and whose individual ratios are
therefore both noisy and, on average, biased low.

**(c)** Yes. Restricting to common variants removes exactly the variants whose per-variant
ratios are least informative, so the two estimators converge. This is the central practical
point of <Citation id="bhatia2013fst" />: the discrepancy between published F_ST values for
the same pair of populations is often not a disagreement about the populations but about which
variants went in and how they were pooled.

</div>

</Exercise>

## Summary

- **F_ST is a variance ratio**, the between-population share of the allele-frequency variance,
  normalised by $\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})$ so that it is comparable across variants of any frequency.
- **Correct for sampling, and count chromosomes.** Hudson's correction divides by $n-1$ in
  sampled gametes; passing diploid counts doubles it and can drive a small F_ST negative.
- **Pool as a ratio of averages.** On a two-variant example the average of ratios reads
  0.087700 against a pooled 0.131688, low by a third; on a six-variant set it reads 0.055207
  against a pooled 0.064880, low by 14.9%.
- **The structure spike is $\lambda = N F_{\mathrm{ST}}$**, exactly, for two equal populations
  under Patterson normalisation — which puts the visibility of ancestry inside random matrix
  theory rather than inside intuition.
- **PCA resolves structure if and only if $F_{\mathrm{ST}} > 1/\sqrt{NM}$** — $2\times10^{-5}$
  for 5,000 people at 500,000 markers. Below it the leading component is a random direction
  with overlap exactly zero, not a weak signal.
- **The cliff is in the eigenvector.** The eigenvalue leaves the bulk edge with zero
  derivative, so the scree plot is uninformative precisely where the decision is being made.
- **Inflation is $1 + N F_{\mathrm{ST}}\delta^2/4$**, linear in $N$. More data makes
  stratification worse, which is why the correction has to be structural rather than a
  constant divisor.
