## Behavioral answers are evidence samples

Structured interviews improve comparability by using common questions and anchored evaluation
<Citation id="campion1997structured" />; broad selection research likewise finds structured
evidence more predictive than intuition-led conversation <Citation id="schmidthunter1998selection" />.
Prepare stories as inspectable evidence, not scripts engineered to sound flawless.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="STAR/L is compression, not theater">
Situation and task establish why the decision mattered. Action reveals *your* judgment and
behavior. Result supplies evidence and uncertainty. Learning shows the update that persisted.
If the action is vague or the result has no denominator, eloquence cannot rescue the signal.
</Callout>

## Build the evidence inventory

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-introduction">
Use a 90–120 second arc: **now**—the professional identity relevant to this role; **evidence**—
two or three capabilities illustrated by real projects; **pattern**—the problems/impact you
repeatedly create; **next**—why this scope follows. Avoid a chronological CV reading.

For this portfolio, possible verified anchors include splice-junction modeling (Splam and
OpenSpliceAI), probabilistic sequence generation (Shorkie), and genome-liftover tooling (LiftOn).
Choose only projects you can defend deeply and describe public facts; do not invent adoption,
speedups or business outcomes. Distinguish team output from personal ownership and end with a
bridge the interviewer can probe: “The common thread is…”
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-ambiguity">
Choose ambiguity with consequences: unclear objective, missing labels, changing biology/product
constraint or uncertain architecture. **S/T:** state decision deadline, stakeholders and what
you owned. **A:** build an uncertainty map, identify irreversible versus reversible choices,
establish a baseline and cheapest discriminating experiments, and create a decision cadence.
Explain how you made assumptions visible and aligned people.

**R/L:** quantify the decision or learning rate, not necessarily a launch. Say what remained
unknown and what you would instrument earlier. Senior signal is creating a process that converts
uncertainty into evidence; claiming you supplied certainty is a warning sign.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-disagreement">
Represent the other position well enough its advocate would agree. Name shared objective and the
specific disputed assumption—quality, latency, interpretation, schedule or risk. Explain your
decision rights and the evidence mechanism: design review, prototype, ablation, load test or
predeclared tie-breaker. Show listening and a real concession.

Report decision, relationship and later evidence. If you were wrong, say how you updated. If
risk exceeded your authority, explain proportionate escalation. Do not make the story depend on
an irrational colleague or “I convinced everyone”; durable alignment matters more than winning.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-failure">
Pick a decision you influenced with real consequence. State the leading indicators available at
the time and why your judgment missed them. Separate containment from prevention: stop/rollback
and communicate first, then trace system/process causes, add the earliest detection, assign
ownership and test recovery. Google's SRE practice treats blameless postmortems as learning from
system conditions while preserving accountability <Citation id="google2016postmortem" />.

Quantify recovery or changed recurrence where evidence exists; otherwise state the observed
process change. The learning must alter later behavior. A disguised strength, failure entirely
caused by others, or claim of “never again” without a control is not credible.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Influence and team capability

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-influence">
Map who decides, who supplies evidence, who operates the result and what each risks. Seek context
before proposing. Translate the technical issue into shared outcomes, offer options rather than
one demand, and reduce adoption cost with a small pilot, migration path or reviewable document.
Invite dissent and credit contributors.

Escalate based on consequence and accountability—not frustration—and tell counterparts before
doing so. Result includes whether the decision process and future collaboration improved, not
only whether your proposal shipped. Influence without authority is making the good decision
easier to see and execute.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-mentoring">
Diagnose a concrete capability gap with the learner, define success, then scaffold ownership:
worked example, paired attempt, independent decision, review and teach-back. Give timely feedback
on observable behavior and adapt to response. Feedback research shows effects vary and can worsen
performance when attention shifts away from the task <Citation id="klugerdenisi1996feedback" />.

Measure independent capability—designs reviewed without rescue, incidents handled, explanations
taught onward—not hours you spent. Psychological safety supports speaking up and learning in
teams <Citation id="edmondson1999psychsafety" />, but safety is compatible with explicit high
standards. Protect credit and do not turn someone's growth into your hero story.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-tech-debt">
Convert debt into consequences: experiment invalidity, incident probability, on-call burden,
lead time, duplicated work or compliance exposure. Separate reversible shortcuts with expiry
from compounding hazards such as unlabeled lineage and train–serve skew—classic ML debt
mechanisms. Offer options: stop and repair, scoped launch plus guardrail, or scheduled migration;
assign owner and trigger.

Use expected impact and opportunity cost, not “quality versus speed.” Reserve capacity and track
whether intervention changes incidents or delivery. A senior answer may accept debt deliberately;
the signal is that the risk is bounded, visible and repayable rather than denied.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-stakeholder">
Begin with decision and consequence. Translate a metric into frequencies: “among 1,000 flagged
cases at this threshold, about 120 may be false alerts,” with interval and population/time scope.
Contrast scenarios and recommend an action, fallback and review trigger. Ask the listener to
restate the decision, not the mathematics.

Remove implementation detail while preserving caveats. Calibration is not certainty; an average
can hide subgroups; lack of external validation is decision-relevant. Goal-setting research
emphasizes specific goals and feedback <Citation id="lockelatham2002goals" />—give the stakeholder
a measurable choice, not a cloud of caveats or one unsupported score.
</InterviewQuestion>

The per-run reduction is 7 minutes. At the observed denominator,
$7\times700=4{,}900$ minutes, or $81.7$ hours/month. A calibrated statement is: “In a
four-week replay over 700 comparable runs, median runtime fell from 18 to 11 minutes; this
corresponds to about 82 compute-hours at that workload.”

This is a hypothetical teaching example—not a claim about any portfolio project. A real answer
must name measurement window, comparable workload, uncertainty, compute versus human time and
any quality/cost regression.
</WorkedExample>

## Risk, collaboration and strategy

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-stop-launch">
Choose a concrete risk and evidence threshold. Explain your responsibility, affected users and
what made the decision urgent. Present alternatives—narrow scope, more data, human review,
canary, rollback—rather than only objection. State how you communicated proportional severity,
who held decision authority and what happened.

The ACM ethics code prioritizes avoiding harm and honest disclosure of limitations
<Citation id="acm2018ethics" />; NIST's AI RMF supplies an official risk-governance frame
<Citation id="nist2023airmf" />. Do not claim personal moral certainty: discuss residual risk,
process and later evidence. These governance expectations were reviewed 2026-08-22.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-cross-functional">
Diagnose whether failure is goal, language, incentives, ownership, interface or trust. Bring
observable examples to a private conversation, ask for their constraints, and reset an operating
contract: decision owner, input deadline, artifact/interface, escalation and definition of done.
Deliver a small joint win and inspect recurrence.

If risk persists, escalate transparently with evidence and options. Avoid motive attribution and
public surprise. Result should cover both delivery and the relationship/process; learning should
name what signal you now surface earlier. “I communicated more” is weak without a changed
interface or behavior.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-strategy">
Strategy begins with decisions/users and constraints, diagnoses current capabilities/bottlenecks,
chooses a few reinforcing bets and explicit non-goals, then sequences learning. Define leading
signals and outcome measures, ownership, dependencies and triggers that cause review. Specific,
challenging goals work when paired with feedback and commitment—not when they are a disconnected
project list <Citation id="lockelatham2002goals" />.

Explain what you will not build and which assumption each first milestone tests. Balance platform
leverage, near-term product/science evidence and risk reduction. A roadmap becomes strategy when
choices share a causal theory of impact and can change as evidence changes.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-lead-why-role">
Use three verified links: a specific target problem from the job/team's public material; a
capability demonstrated by your actual work; and a growth edge the scope enables. Explain why now
as a coherent next responsibility and what you could learn/contribute in the first phase.

Avoid generic mission praise, invented insider priorities and claiming every requirement. Prepare
one version per role, updated from current sources; this question is fast-moving and was reviewed
2026-08-22. The strongest answer includes a falsifiable fit statement and a thoughtful question
about the missing information needed to confirm it.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="Remove the invented metric" difficulty="warm-up">
Your draft says, “My tool dramatically improved adoption,” but the only public evidence is a paper,
repository and documentation. Rewrite the result.
<div slot="solution">State verified output and owned contribution: for example, “The team released
the method, repository and documentation; I owned X as documented in Y.” Then say adoption was not
measured or provide an authorized source. Never substitute stars, citations or intuition for a
defined adoption outcome.</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Make action the center" difficulty="core">
A two-minute conflict story spends 80 seconds describing the colleague and 15 seconds on your
action. How should it be rebalanced?
<div slot="solution">Compress context to shared goal, stakes and disputed assumption. Spend most
time on how you elicited the other view, evidence/experiment, tradeoff, communication, concession
and decision. Close with outcome, relationship and learning. Remove motive speculation.</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Calibrate a leadership claim" difficulty="stretch">
You introduced reviews and no incident occurred in the next quarter. Can you claim the reviews
prevented incidents?
<div slot="solution">No causal claim follows from one before/after period. Report what is known:
review coverage, defects caught before merge, response time or recurrence across comparable work.
Describe prevention as a hypothesis unless stronger comparison exists, and name other changes and
the evidence you would collect.</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- STAR/L compresses context so decisions, evidence, result and learning remain inspectable.
- Calibrate every claim to ownership, denominator, time window and uncertainty.
- Strong conflict stories represent the other view fairly and improve the decision process.
- Mentoring is transfer of independent capability under explicit standards and psychological safety.
- Leadership turns ambiguity and debt into options, owners, tests and reversible decisions.
- Portfolio claims may use verified artifacts; outcomes and metrics must never be invented.
