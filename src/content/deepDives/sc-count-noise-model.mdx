## The question this lesson answers

Between 80% and 95% of a single-cell count matrix is zeros. What are they?

For most of the field's first decade the answer was "technical failures" — molecules that were
present but not captured, a phenomenon named **dropout**, modelled with an extra parameter and
frequently imputed away before analysis <Citation id="kharchenko2014scde" />. The name stuck,
the software spread, and a large literature grew on top of a premise that had not been checked
against the simplest alternative.

The alternative is that a zero is what you get when you count a small number of things. This
lesson works out what that alternative predicts, in cells, so that the two can be told apart.

## Capture is a thinning, and a thinning is Poisson

A cell contains $M$ molecules of some gene. Each is captured, reverse-transcribed and
sequenced independently with probability $p$ — the capture efficiency, which for droplet
protocols is on the order of 0.1. The observed count is therefore binomial:

$$
c \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(M, p).
$$

$M$ is large and $p$ is small, so this is the classical Poisson regime. Writing $\mu = Mp$,

$$
\Pr(c = k) = \frac{\mu^k e^{-\mu}}{k!}, \qquad \Pr(c = 0) = e^{-\mu}.
$$

That last expression is the whole argument in one line. A gene with 1,000 molecules in a cell
and 10% capture has $\mu = 100$ and is never zero. A gene with **one** molecule in a cell and
10% capture has $\mu = 0.1$ and is zero in

$$
e^{-0.1} = 0.904837,
$$

**90.48%** of cells. No molecule failed to be captured in any interesting sense; the count is
small, and small counts are mostly zero. Most genes in most cells are in the second regime,
which is why most of the matrix is zeros.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Zero is a number, not a missing value">

The instinct that a zero is *missing data* comes from technologies where it was. A microarray
spot with no signal really did fail; a bulk RNA-seq gene with zero reads at 30 million depth
really is off.

A single-cell zero is neither. It is an observation, and it carries information: a gene
observed zero in a cell is evidence that gene is lowly expressed there, and the strength of
that evidence is exactly $e^{-\mu}$. Treating it as missing and filling it in replaces a
correct observation with a guess derived from other cells — which is to say, with the very
similarity the analysis is about to go looking for.

</Callout>

## Cells differ, so the rate is not fixed

Poisson would be the whole story if every cell of a type had the same $\mu$. They do not.
Cells differ in size, in total RNA content, in cell-cycle phase, in how much ambient fluid
came with them and in how deeply they happened to be sequenced. So $\mu$ is itself a random
variable across cells.

Let it vary as a Gamma with mean $\mu$ and shape $\theta$. Mixing a Poisson over a Gamma rate
gives, exactly, a **negative binomial**:

$$
\Pr(c = k) = \frac{\Gamma(k + \theta)}{k!\,\Gamma(\theta)}
\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu + \theta}\right)^{k}
\left(\frac{\theta}{\mu + \theta}\right)^{\theta},
$$

with

$$
\mathbb{E}[c] = \mu, \qquad
\mathrm{Var}(c) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{\theta}, \qquad
\Pr(c = 0) = \left(\frac{\theta}{\theta + \mu}\right)^{\theta}.
$$

Three things to read off this. The variance exceeds the mean by $\mu^2/\theta$, which is
**quadratic**, so overdispersion is invisible at low mean and dominant at high. As
$\theta \to \infty$ the Gamma collapses to a point and the negative binomial returns to
Poisson. And the zero probability is *larger* than Poisson's for every finite $\theta$ —
mixing rates always adds zeros, because the cells that drew a low rate contribute more zeros
than the cells that drew a high rate remove.

That last point matters: **overdispersion alone manufactures excess zeros over Poisson.** Any
comparison that finds "more zeros than Poisson predicts" and concludes dropout has skipped
the model that was going to explain them.

## Testing a gene's zeros against its own mean and variance

The negative binomial has two parameters and there are two moments to fit them with. Solving
$\sigma^2 = \mu + \mu^2/\theta$ for $\theta$ gives the method-of-moments estimator

$$
\hat{\theta} = \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2 - \mu},
$$

which needs no zeros at all — only the mean and variance. That makes it an honest test: fit
$\theta$ without looking at the zero count, then predict the zero count and compare.

<WorkedExample
  title="Does this gene need a dropout parameter?"
  given={[
    '5,000 cells',
    'Observed mean count 0.5, observed variance 0.625',
    'Observed zeros: 3,200 cells',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — is it overdispersed at all?** The variance exceeds the mean by
$0.625 - 0.5 = 0.125$, so yes, and Poisson is already ruled out.

**Step 2 — fit θ from the moments, ignoring the zeros.**

$$
\hat{\theta} = \frac{0.5^2}{0.625 - 0.5} = \frac{0.25}{0.125} = 2.
$$

**Step 3 — predict the zeros.**

$$
\Pr(c = 0) = \left(\frac{2}{2 + 0.5}\right)^{2} = 0.8^2 = 0.64,
$$

so **64.00%**, or $0.64 \times 5{,}000 = 3{,}200$ cells.

**Step 4 — compare.** 3,200 predicted, 3,200 observed. A zero-inflation parameter fitted to
this gene comes back at $\pi_0 = 0$, because there is nothing left for it to explain.

**What the naive comparison would have concluded.** Poisson at the same mean predicts
$e^{-0.5} = 0.606531$, or 3,033 cells. Against *that* baseline the gene has 167 excess zeros,
and a paper reporting "5.5% dropout" would be reporting the difference between a negative
binomial and a Poisson — that is, reporting overdispersion under another name.

</WorkedExample>

Run over whole datasets, this is what the analysis found: droplet counts are consistent with a
negative binomial, and the zero-inflation parameter is estimated at essentially zero
genome-wide <Citation id="svensson2020zeroinflation" />. The same conclusion had been reached
earlier with spike-in controls, where the true input is known
<Citation id="grun2014noise" />, and it underpins the modern normalisation and feature-selection
methods, which model counts directly rather than imputing them
<Citation id="hafemeister2019sctransform" /><Citation id="townes2019glmpca" />.

The nuance worth keeping: **full-length protocols are a different case.** Plate-based methods
without UMIs have genuine technical zeros from failed reverse transcription, and zero-inflated
models were developed on exactly that kind of data. The claim is about droplet UMI counts
<Citation id="zheng2017chromium" /><Citation id="islam2013umi" />, which is what nearly
everyone now generates.

## Why this decides what you may do to the matrix

If the zeros are sampling, imputation is not recovering a hidden value — it is smoothing. And
smoothing a matrix before testing it for differences is the classic way to manufacture
significance: imputed values are borrowed from similar cells, which makes similar cells more
similar, which makes any grouping you then test look cleaner than it is
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

The count model also decides what a normalisation is allowed to do, which is the subject of
lesson 5, and what "highly variable" means, which is lesson 6. Both are questions about the
mean–variance trend in Figure 2, and both go wrong when the trend is assumed rather than fitted.

## Summary

- Capture is a binomial thinning of a molecule count, so in the small-$p$ regime the observed
  count is **Poisson**: $\Pr(c = 0) = e^{-\mu}$, which is **90.48%** at a mean of 0.1.
- Cells differ, so $\mu$ varies. Mixing Poisson over a Gamma gives the **negative binomial**,
  with $\mathrm{Var}(c) = \mu + \mu^2/\theta$ and
  $\Pr(c = 0) = (\theta/(\theta+\mu))^{\theta}$.
- The excess variance is **quadratic in the mean**, so overdispersion is negligible at low mean
  and dominant at high — and it always adds zeros over Poisson, so "more zeros than Poisson"
  is not evidence of dropout.
- Fit $\hat\theta = \mu^2/(\sigma^2 - \mu)$ from the moments and predict the zeros. For a gene
  with mean 0.5 and variance 0.625 this gives $\theta = 2$, predicts 64.00% zeros, and matches
  3,200 of 5,000 observed exactly.
- Genome-wide, droplet UMI counts need **no zero-inflation parameter**. Full-length protocols
  without UMIs are a genuinely different case.
- Zeros are observations. Imputing them before a test smooths the matrix towards whatever
  grouping is about to be tested.

<Exercise n={2} title="Auditing a dropout claim" difficulty="core">

A paper reports that a gene shows substantial dropout. Across 5,000 cells its mean count is
2.0, its variance is 8.0, and 1,950 cells are zero.

(a) Fit $\theta$ from the moments.

(b) How many zeros does the negative binomial predict?

(c) How many does Poisson predict, and what would the "dropout rate" be if Poisson were used
as the baseline?

(d) What should the paper have concluded?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\hat\theta = 2.0^2/(8.0 - 2.0) = 4/6 = 0.6667$.

(b) $\Pr(c=0) = (0.6667/2.6667)^{0.6667} = 0.25^{0.6667} = 0.396850$, so **1,984 cells**.

(c) Poisson gives $e^{-2} = 0.135335$, or **677 cells**. Against that baseline the gene has
$1{,}950 - 677 = 1{,}273$ "extra" zeros, which is 25.5% of all cells — a dramatic-sounding
dropout rate.

(d) That the gene is overdispersed and its zeros are fully accounted for. Observed 1,950
against 1,984 predicted is a shortfall of 34 cells; the standard deviation of a binomial count
with $n = 5{,}000$ and $p = 0.3969$ is $\sqrt{5{,}000(0.3969)(0.6031)} = 34.6$, so the
discrepancy is under one standard deviation and in the *wrong direction* for dropout. The
entire "25.5% dropout" is the gap between a Poisson and the negative binomial that fits.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="When zero-inflation is not identifiable" difficulty="stretch">

Exercise 1 found a 43-cell difference between two count models at a mean of 0.1 in 10,000
cells.

(a) Compute the standard deviation of the number of zeros observed in 10,000 cells under the
Poisson model.

(b) How many standard deviations is the 43-cell difference?

(c) A collaborator proposes settling the zero-inflation question using the lowly expressed
genes, since they have the most zeros. Explain why that is exactly backwards, and say which
genes carry the information.

<div slot="solution">

(a) The zero count is binomial with $n = 10{,}000$ and $p = 0.904837$:

$$
\sqrt{10{,}000 \times 0.904837 \times 0.095163} = \sqrt{861.06} = 29.34.
$$

(b) $43/29.34 = 1.47$ standard deviations. Two models as different as Poisson and
$\theta = 1$ are separated by less than one and a half standard errors on this gene, so no
amount of care distinguishes them here — and a zero-inflation parameter, which sits between
them, is even less identifiable.

(c) Lowly expressed genes have the most zeros and the least *information about where the zeros
came from*, because at low mean every candidate model predicts nearly the same number. The
discriminating power is in the mean–variance behaviour and the zero counts of **well-expressed
genes**, where Figure 1's curves are far apart: at a mean of 5, Poisson predicts 0.67% zeros
and a negative binomial with $\theta = 2$ predicts 8.16%, a twelvefold difference that
thousands of cells resolve easily. The uncomfortable consequence is that the question is
settled on the minority of genes that have almost no zeros.

</div>

</Exercise>
