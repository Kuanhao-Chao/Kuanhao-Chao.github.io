## The question this lesson answers

Linkage and linkage disequilibrium have the same physical cause: recombination does not
separate loci that sit close together on a chromosome. They are not the same thing, and
treating them as interchangeable is the most common error in this corner of the field.

| | **Linkage** | **Linkage disequilibrium** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observed in | One family | A population |
| Counts | Meioses you can see, tens to hundreds | Meioses since a shared ancestor, thousands |
| Detects | Co-segregation of alleles within pedigrees | Correlation of alleles across unrelated people |
| Resolves to | Tens of megabases | Tens of kilobases |
| Finds | Rare variants of large effect | Common variants of small effect |

The two rows that matter are the last two, and they are consequences of the second. A study
sees only as many recombination events as it has meioses, and a pedigree has very few. That
single fact explains both why linkage analysis identified the genes for cystic fibrosis and
Huntington's disease, and why two decades of linkage studies of schizophrenia and diabetes
produced almost nothing.

## Kinship: counting shared ancestry

Two individuals are related to the extent that they can draw the same ancestral allele.
<Citation id="wright1922coefficients" /> put a number on this; Malécot's formulation is the
one that computes. Define $f(x,y)$ as the probability that an allele drawn at random from
$x$ and one drawn at random from $y$ are **identical by descent** — copies of one ancestral
allele.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Kinship is the answer to a two-draw experiment">

Before any recursion, fix what the number *is*. Reach into individual $x$ and take one of
its two alleles at a locus, at random. Reach into $y$ and take one of its two. Kinship is
the probability that those two are the same physical copy, inherited from one ancestor.

Three things follow without algebra. Two unrelated people have kinship zero, because no
ancestor is shared. An individual's kinship *with itself* is $\tfrac{1}{2}$, not 1 — you may
draw the same allele twice, which happens half the time, or the two different ones, which
are not copies of each other unless the individual is inbred. And every coefficient is a sum
over the paths by which an allele could descend to both parties, which is exactly what the
recursion below evaluates without your having to enumerate them.

Holding onto the experiment is what keeps the sign and the factor of two straight later:
$\mathbf{A} = 2f$ has full sibs at $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and an individual at 1, because the
relationship matrix is a *covariance* rather than a probability.

</Callout>

The whole calculation is one recursion. An allele drawn from $x$ came from $x$'s sire or
$x$'s dam with equal probability, so

$$
f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[f(\text{sire}_x, y) + f(\text{dam}_x, y)\big]
$$

provided $x$ is not an ancestor of $y$ — which is why the pedigree must be processed with
parents before offspring. Two special cases complete it: an individual with unknown parents
is unrelated to everyone and has $f(x,x) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and in general

$$
f(x, x) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + F_x), \qquad F_x = f(\text{sire}_x, \text{dam}_x)
$$

The inbreeding coefficient of an individual is precisely the kinship of its parents. That is
not a coincidence or a convention — it is the same probability, read from a different end.

**Step 1 — the founders.** G1, G2, S1 and S2 have unknown parents, so they are mutually
unrelated and each has $f(x,x) = 0.5$ and $F = 0$.

**Step 2 — the full sibs.** Apply the recursion to P1 and P2, both children of G1 × G2:

$$
f(\text{P1}, \text{P2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[f(\text{G1}, \text{P2}) + f(\text{G2}, \text{P2})\big]
= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4}\big] = \tfrac{1}{4}
$$

**Step 3 — the first cousins.** C1 has parents P1 × S1 and C2 has parents P2 × S2, with S1
and S2 unrelated to everything:

$$
f(\text{C1}, \text{C2}) = \tfrac{1}{4}\, f(\text{P1}, \text{P2}) = \tfrac{1}{4}\times\tfrac{1}{4}
= \tfrac{1}{16} = 0.0625
$$

**Step 4 — the inbred child.** X's inbreeding coefficient *is* its parents' kinship:

$$
F_X = f(\text{C1}, \text{C2}) = 0.0625,
\qquad
f(X, X) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + 0.0625) = 0.53125
$$

**Step 5 — the relationship matrix.** Doubling gives $\mathbf{A}$:

$$
A_{\text{C1},\text{C2}} = 2 f(\text{C1},\text{C2}) = 0.125,
\qquad
A_{XX} = 2 f(X,X) = 1 + F_X = 1.0625
$$

The diagonal exceeding 1 is the whole content of inbreeding in this framework: an inbred
individual carries **more** additive genetic variance than an outbred one, because its two
alleles at a locus are more often the same.

</WorkedExample>

The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ with $A_{ij} = 2f(i,j)$ is the object the rest of the curriculum
uses. It is the covariance of breeding values in units of $V_A$ — full sibs at $\tfrac{1}{2}$,
parent and offspring at $\tfrac{1}{2}$, half sibs at $\tfrac{1}{4}$ — and it is what a mixed
model puts in its variance structure. <Citation id="henderson1976inverse" /> gave the result
that made this practical at scale: $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$, which is what the equations actually
need, can be written down directly from the pedigree without ever forming or inverting
$\mathbf{A}$, and it is extremely sparse.

<Callout variant="key" title="The same matrix, twice, from different data">

Everything above is computed from a **recorded pedigree**: it gives the *expected* fraction
of alleles shared. Two full sibs have $A_{ij} = 0.5$ by definition of being full sibs.

The genomic relatedness matrix in Module 3 estimates the same quantity from **genotypes**,
and gets a different answer: two full sibs actually share somewhere near 0.5, but with a
standard deviation of about 0.04, because Mendelian segregation is itself random. That
difference is not error — it is real information, and it is what lets a within-family
design estimate heritability free of shared environment.

</Callout>

## Linkage: co-segregation within a family

Now the mapping problem. A disease allele segregates through a pedigree; so does a marker
allele. If the two loci are far apart, recombination separates them half the time and they
segregate independently. If they are close, they travel together.

Let $\theta$ be the recombination fraction. Observing $r$ recombinants in $n$ informative
meioses, the likelihood is binomial, and the natural comparison is against **free
recombination**, $\theta = 0.5$:

$$
Z(\theta) = \log_{10}\frac{L(\theta)}{L(0.5)}
= \log_{10}\frac{\theta^{r}(1-\theta)^{n-r}}{(1/2)^{n}}
$$

<Citation id="morton1955lod" /> introduced this as a sequential test, and the base-10
logarithm is deliberate: it makes the score readable as an order of magnitude of odds. The
maximum-likelihood estimate of $\theta$ falls out immediately as $\hat\theta = r/n$, and
$Z(0.5) = 0$ by construction.

  given={[

**Step 1 — the estimate.** $\hat\theta = r/n = 3/25 = 0.12$.

**Step 2 — the LOD at that estimate.**

$$
Z(0.12) = 3\log_{10}(0.12) + 22\log_{10}(0.88) + 25\log_{10} 2
$$

$$
= -2.762456 - 1.221381 + 7.525750 = 3.5419
$$

**Step 3 — convert to the scale Module 1 used.** The LOD is a base-10 log likelihood ratio,
so twice the natural log of the same ratio is

$$
\chi^2 = 2\ln(10)\,Z = 4.60517 \times 3.5419 = 16.3111
$$

on 1 df, a point-wise $p$ of $5.375\times10^{-5}$.

**Step 4 — read the interval, not just the peak.** The conventional support interval drops
one LOD unit from the maximum, here down to 2.5419, which the curve reaches at
$\theta = 0.0266$ and $\theta = 0.3008$. In a genome where 1 cM is about a megabase, that is an
interval tens of megabases wide containing hundreds of genes.

**This is the real limitation of linkage**, and it is not statistical power — the peak is
perfectly significant. It is that twenty-five meioses contain only twenty-five opportunities
for recombination to tell you anything about position.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout variant="warning" title="LOD 3 is not p = 0.001">

The threshold is routinely explained as "a thousand to one", and then silently treated as a
$p$-value of $10^{-3}$. It is neither of those things.

As a **frequentist statistic**, $\mathrm{LOD} = 3$ is $\chi^2 = 2\ln(10)\times 3 = 13.8155$
on 1 df, which is a point-wise $p$ of $2.0\times10^{-4}$ — five times smaller than $0.001$.

The thousand-to-one is a **prior odds** argument, and <Citation id="morton1955lod" /> was
explicit about it: any two loci picked at random are unlikely to be linked, roughly 50:1
against for the human genome, so a likelihood ratio of 1000:1 is needed before the posterior
odds favour linkage. It is a Bayesian threshold that happens to be quoted in log units.

For a genome scan neither number is enough. <Citation id="lander1995thresholds" /> derived
the threshold that accounts for searching the whole genome, and got **LOD 3.3** for
sib-pair linkage — and by the same logic, a genome-wide association threshold of
$5\times10^{-8}$ corresponds to $\chi^2 = 29.7168$, or a LOD of 6.4529. The gap between
3.3 and 6.45 is the difference between scanning a few hundred markers and scanning a million.

</Callout>

## Why linkage worked for Mendelian disease and stalled on complex traits

Both failures and successes follow from the meiosis count.

**Resolution.** A study observes $n$ meioses and therefore at most $n$ informative
recombination events. Distances shorter than about $1/n$ in recombination units cannot be
resolved, and $\theta$ is converted to map distance by a mapping function — Haldane's
$d = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1-2\theta)$ from <Citation id="haldane1919" /> — which only makes
things worse at short distances by correcting for double crossovers. A few hundred meioses
buys a few centimorgans. Population-scale LD, by contrast, reflects every meiosis since the
variant arose, which is thousands of generations, and resolves accordingly.

**Power.** Linkage detects a locus through its effect on *sharing within families*, which
requires the locus to substantially determine who in the family is affected. A variant with
a genotype relative risk of 1.2 does not do that in any pedigree of realistic size.
<Citation id="risch1996future" /> made this argument precisely and concluded that
association testing would be far more powerful than linkage for such variants — a
prediction the genome-wide association era then confirmed comprehensively.

The likelihood computation itself is not the obstacle. <Citation id="elston1971peeling" />
gave the peeling algorithm, which evaluates a pedigree likelihood in time linear in the
number of individuals rather than exponential, and it is still the basis of exact pedigree
likelihood software.

## QTL mapping: linkage for a quantitative trait

For a continuous trait in an experimental cross, the same logic applies with the disease
status replaced by a phenotype. The naive approach tests each marker separately, which
confounds the QTL's effect size with its distance from the marker: a large effect far away
and a small effect nearby look identical.

<Citation id="lander1989interval" /> solved this with **interval mapping**. At each position
between two flanking markers, the genotype at that position is unobserved, so its
probability is computed from the flanking markers and the trait is modelled as a mixture over
the possible genotypes. Maximising the likelihood at every position gives a LOD profile
along the chromosome, whose peak estimates position and whose height estimates significance —
and, crucially, effect size and position are no longer confounded.

The threshold for that profile cannot come from a $\chi^2$ table. The statistic is maximised
over a continuum of positions, the tests are correlated by linkage, and the null distribution
depends on the marker map and the missing-data pattern. The standard solution is
**permutation**: shuffle the phenotypes against the genotypes, recompute the whole genome
scan, record the maximum LOD, and repeat. The 95th percentile of those maxima is the
threshold. It is expensive and it is correct, and it automatically accounts for the density
of the marker map — which no table can.

## The TDT: association immune to population structure

Case-control association has a well-known failure mode. If cases and controls are drawn
from populations with different ancestry, any variant whose frequency differs between those
populations will appear associated, whether or not it has anything to do with the trait.

<Citation id="spielman1993tdt" /> proposed a design that cannot fail this way. Take
**trios**: an affected child and both parents. Consider only parents heterozygous at the
variant — each transmits one of two alleles to the child, and under the null of no
association transmits either with probability one half. Count $b$ transmissions of the
candidate allele and $c$ non-transmissions:

$$
\chi^2_{\mathrm{TDT}} = \frac{(b-c)^2}{b+c}
$$

on 1 df — McNemar's test, applied to transmission.

<WorkedExample
  title="A transmission disequilibrium test"
  given={[
    { label: 'Heterozygous parents', value: '55' },
    { label: 'Transmitted the allele', value: 'b = 38' },
    { label: 'Did not transmit', value: 'c = 17' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the statistic.**

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(38-17)^2}{38+17} = \frac{441}{55} = 8.018182
$$

**Step 2 — the $p$-value.** On 1 df, $p = 4.631\times10^{-3}$.

**Step 3 — why this cannot be confounded.** The test conditions on the parents. Population
structure changes **which** parents are heterozygous — an ancestry group with a higher allele
frequency contributes more heterozygous parents — but it does not change what a heterozygous
parent transmits, which is one allele or the other with probability one half regardless of
ancestry. There is no mechanism by which stratification can bias $b$ relative to $c$.

That immunity is bought with data. Recruiting 55 informative trios means genotyping at least
165 people and finding families with both parents available, which for a late-onset disease
is often impossible. Modern studies mostly pay for robustness a different way, by modelling
ancestry directly in unrelated samples.

</WorkedExample>

<Exercise n={1} title="Kinship for three relationships" difficulty="warm-up">

Using the recursion, compute the kinship coefficient for (a) half sibs — two individuals
sharing one parent — and (b) an uncle and his niece. Convert both to entries of
$\mathbf{A}$. What do you notice?

<div slot="solution">

**(a) Half sibs.** Let HS1 and HS2 share sire A but have unrelated dams B and C:

$$
f(\text{HS1}, \text{HS2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[f(A, \text{HS2}) + f(B, \text{HS2})\big]
= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\tfrac{1}{4} + 0\big] = \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.125
$$

**(b) Uncle and niece.** Let N be the child of HS1 and an unrelated female. HS2 is then N's
uncle:

$$
f(\text{HS2}, N) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[f(\text{HS2}, \text{HS1}) + 0\big]
= \tfrac{1}{2}\times\tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{1}{16} = 0.0625
$$

In $\mathbf{A}$ these are 0.25 and 0.125 respectively.

**What to notice:** the uncle–niece kinship of 1/16 is identical to the first-cousin kinship
in the worked example, and half sibs at 1/8 match a grandparent–grandchild pair. Kinship
counts paths, not relationship names, so genealogically distinct relationships routinely
share a coefficient — and a pedigree-based model therefore cannot tell them apart. Only
genotype data can, because the *realised* sharing differs even when the expected sharing
does not.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="The smallest study that reaches LOD 3" difficulty="core">

A single large pedigree yields informative meioses with **no** recombinants observed between
a marker and a disease locus. (a) Write $Z(\theta)$ for $r = 0$ and find where it is
maximised. (b) How many meioses are needed to reach LOD 3? (c) Contrast the interval this
gives with the one in the worked example.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** With $r = 0$ the likelihood is $(1-\theta)^n$, maximised at $\hat\theta = 0$, and

$$
Z(0) = \log_{10}\frac{1}{(1/2)^n} = n\log_{10} 2
$$

**(b)** Setting $n \log_{10} 2 \ge 3$ gives $n \ge 3/0.301030 = 9.97$, so **ten meioses**.
With exactly ten, $Z = 10\log_{10}2 = 3.010300$ — the classical threshold is cleared, only
just, by a single well-chosen family.

**(c)** The two studies reach comparable LODs from very different evidence. Ten meioses with
no recombinants put $\hat\theta$ at 0, and the support interval runs from 0 to $\theta = 0.2057$ — one-sided, because $\theta$ cannot be negative. Twenty-five meioses with three
recombinants put $\hat\theta$ at 0.12 with a two-sided interval.

The instructive point is that the zero-recombinant study, despite its tiny sample, localises
*better*: every meiosis it observed was consistent with complete linkage. This is why
linkage studies of Mendelian disease sought large consanguineous families rather than many
small ones. It is also why $\hat\theta = 0$ should be reported with its interval and never
as a point estimate — "no recombinants in ten meioses" is entirely compatible with
$\theta = 0.10$.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="When the case-control test and the TDT disagree" difficulty="stretch">

A variant is tested two ways in the same population. A case-control analysis gives a highly
significant association; a TDT on trios from the same families gives $b = 25$, $c = 25$.
(a) Compute the TDT statistic. (b) What is the most likely explanation of the discrepancy?
(c) What would you check, and what would reverse your conclusion?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\chi^2 = (25-25)^2/50 = 0$, $p = 1$. There is no evidence of transmission
distortion at all.

**(b)** The most likely explanation is **population stratification** in the case-control
analysis. The variant's frequency differs between ancestry groups, and those groups are
represented unequally among cases and controls, which produces association at a variant with
no causal role. The TDT conditions on the parents and is therefore blind to that mechanism,
so it correctly reports nothing.

**(c)** Check whether the case-control result survives explicit adjustment for ancestry, and
check the genomic inflation factor across all variants — stratification that is strong enough
to manufacture one association will inflate the whole distribution of test statistics, not
just this one. If it does inflate, the TDT is right.

**What would reverse the conclusion:** the TDT is a test of linkage *and* association
jointly, and it has far less power than a case-control analysis of the same number of
individuals — 55 trios cost 165 genotypes. A null TDT on 50 informative transmissions is
consistent with a genuine odds ratio around 1.3, so absence of evidence here is weak
evidence of absence. If the case-control association survived ancestry adjustment and the
inflation factor were near 1, the honest reading would be that the TDT was simply
underpowered.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **Kinship is one recursion**, $f(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}[f(\text{sire}_x, y) + f(\text{dam}_x, y)]$,
  and an inbreeding coefficient is just the kinship of the parents. First cousins are at
  $1/16$; their child carries $F = 0.0625$.
- **$\mathbf{A} = 2f$ is the covariance of breeding values**, with $A_{ii} = 1 + F_i$.
  Henderson's rules give $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ directly from the pedigree, which is what makes
  mixed models on large pedigrees tractable. The genomic relatedness matrix estimates the
  same quantity and disagrees with it, and that disagreement is information.
- **The LOD score is a base-10 log likelihood ratio against free recombination**, maximised
  at $\hat\theta = r/n$. Three recombinants in twenty-five meioses give LOD 3.5419 at
  $\theta = 0.12$.
- **LOD 3 is $\chi^2 = 13.8155$ and a point-wise $p$ of $2.0\times10^{-4}$**, not 0.001.
  The thousand-to-one is a prior-odds argument. A genome scan needs LOD 3.3 by
  Lander–Kruglyak, and the association-era $5\times10^{-8}$ corresponds to LOD 6.4529.
- **Linkage resolves badly because a pedigree contains few meioses.** That, not statistical
  power, is why linkage intervals span megabases; and its inability to detect small effects
  is why it stalled on complex traits while association did not.
- **Interval mapping** removes the confounding of QTL effect and position by modelling the
  unobserved genotype between flanking markers; its threshold must come from permutation,
  because the statistic is maximised over a correlated continuum.
- **The TDT cannot be confounded by ancestry** because it conditions on the parents:
  structure changes which parents are heterozygous, not what they transmit. It pays for that
  in sample size, needing complete trios.
