## The question this lesson answers

A genotype has a **value**. An allele has an **effect**. These sound like the same statement
in two vocabularies, and almost every confusion in this subject comes from treating them as
if they were.

The reason they cannot be the same is mechanical: **a parent transmits alleles, not
genotypes**. Whatever part of an individual's superiority comes from the particular
*combination* of alleles it happens to carry is destroyed at meiosis and reassembled at
random in its offspring. Only the part that travels with single alleles is transmitted.

Everything below is the machinery for separating those two parts. It was built by
<Citation id="fisher1918infinitesimal" />, in the same paper that reconciled Mendelian
inheritance with continuous traits, and the separation it produces is what makes
heritability, breeding programmes, and polygenic scores possible.

## One locus, three values

Set the origin midway between the two homozygotes and measure everything from there. Then
the three genotypes have values

$$
G_{AA} = +a, \qquad G_{Aa} = d, \qquad G_{aa} = -a
$$

The parameter $d$ carries all the dominance: $d = 0$ is purely additive, $d = a$ is complete
dominance of $A$, and $d > a$ is overdominance. With Hardy–Weinberg genotype frequencies the
population mean is

$$
M = p^2(a) + 2pq(d) + q^2(-a) = a(p - q) + 2pq\,d
$$

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Why the effect of an allele is a regression slope">

Ask what an allele is *worth*. It has no value of its own — its contribution depends on
which allele it lands beside. An $A$ allele that meets another $A$ produces a genotype worth
$+a$; the same $A$ meeting an $a$ produces one worth $d$.

So the honest question is not "what is this allele worth" but "**by how much does the
expected value change per extra copy of it**", averaged over the partners it will actually
meet — which, under random mating, means averaged with weights $p$ and $q$.

That is precisely a regression slope: regress genotypic value on the number of copies of
$A$, weighting each genotype by how often it occurs. The slope is the average effect
$\alpha$, and it depends on the allele frequencies because the partners do.

Once you see it as a regression, the rest of the decomposition is automatic. The fitted
values are what the allele count predicts, which is exactly the part that gets transmitted —
the **breeding value**. The residuals are what the allele count cannot predict, which is
exactly the part destroyed at meiosis — the **dominance deviation**.

</Callout>

Carrying out that regression gives

$$
\alpha = a + d(q - p)
$$

and the three breeding values, measured as deviations from $M$:

$$
A_{AA} = 2q\alpha, \qquad A_{Aa} = (q-p)\alpha, \qquad A_{aa} = -2p\alpha
$$

<WorkedExample
  title="Decomposing one locus by hand"
  given={[
    { label: 'Homozygote effect a', value: '10' },
    { label: 'Dominance d', value: '4' },
    { label: 'Frequency p(A)', value: '0.6' },

**Step 1 — the population mean.** With $q = 0.4$:

$$
M = 10(0.6 - 0.4) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(4) = 2 + 1.92 = 3.92
$$

**Step 2 — the average effect.**

$$
\alpha = a + d(q-p) = 10 + 4(0.4 - 0.6) = 10 - 0.8 = 9.20
$$

Note that $\alpha \ne a$. The substitution effect is smaller than the homozygote effect here
because $A$ is the commoner allele, so an extra copy of it more often lands beside another
$A$ — where the dominance bonus has already been collected.

**Step 3 — breeding values.**

$$
A_{AA} = 2(0.4)(9.2) = 7.36, \quad
A_{Aa} = (0.4-0.6)(9.2) = -1.84, \quad
A_{aa} = -2(0.6)(9.2) = -11.04
$$

These average to zero over the population, as they must:
$0.36(7.36) + 0.48(-1.84) + 0.16(-11.04) = 0$.

**Step 4 — dominance deviations.** Each is the genotypic value minus what the breeding value
predicts, $G_i - (M + A_i)$:

$$
D_{AA} = 10 - 11.28 = -1.28, \quad
D_{Aa} = 4 - 2.08 = +1.92, \quad
D_{aa} = -10 - (-7.12) = -2.88
$$

**Step 5 — the two variances.**

$$
V_A = 2pq\,\alpha^2 = 2(0.6)(0.4)(9.2)^2 = 0.48 \times 84.64 = 40.6272
$$

$$
V_D = (2pq\,d)^2 = (0.48 \times 4)^2 = 1.92^2 = 3.6864
$$

As a check, computing $V_D$ the long way as the frequency-weighted mean squared deviation
gives $0.36(1.28)^2 + 0.48(1.92)^2 + 0.16(2.88)^2 = 3.6864$. The decomposition is exact, and
the two components are uncorrelated by construction — that is what makes them *components*.

</WorkedExample>

## The variances belong to the population, not to the locus

Nothing about the locus changes as allele frequencies drift, yet the variance it contributes
changes enormously. $V_A = 2pq\alpha^2$ is zero when either allele is fixed, for the obvious
reason that a monomorphic locus produces no variation at all.

This has a consequence that reaches all the way to genome-wide association studies. A
variant contributes to $V_A$ in proportion to $2pq\alpha^2$, so a rare variant with a large
effect and a common variant with a small one can contribute equally — and the additive
variance a study can *detect* depends on the allele frequencies in the population it
sampled, not on biology alone.

<Callout variant="key" title="Why additivity dominates in practice, even when biology does not">

Real gene action is full of dominance and epistasis, and yet empirical variance
decompositions consistently find that most genetic variance is additive.
<Citation id="hill2008additive" /> explains why, and it is not that biology is additive.

Take the overdominant locus in Exercise 3, or any strongly non-additive one. Its
*contribution to $V_A$* is $2pq\alpha^2$, and $\alpha$ is a frequency-weighted regression
slope. At most allele frequencies, a non-additive locus still has a non-zero slope — the
regression picks up whatever linear trend exists, and there almost always is one. Only at
isolated frequencies does $\alpha$ vanish.

So the statement "most genetic variance is additive" is a claim about **variance**, not
about **mechanism**. A locus can act by pure overdominance and still deliver most of its
variance to $V_A$. Reading additive variance as evidence of additive biology is the standard
misreading, and it is why <Citation id="hill2010quantitative" /> insists on keeping the two
questions separate.

</Callout>

## Heritability and the breeder's equation

Narrow-sense heritability is the fraction of phenotypic variance that is additive:

$$
h^2 = \frac{V_A}{V_P}
$$

and it is the regression of offspring on mid-parent — which is the same statement, because
what parents transmit is breeding value. That gives the **breeder's equation**:

$$
R = h^2 S
$$

Select parents whose mean exceeds the population mean by $S$; their offspring exceed it by
$h^2 S$. The shortfall $\left(1 - h^2\right)S$ is everything about the selected parents that
was not transmissible — environment, dominance, and the particular allele combinations they
happened to carry.

It is often more useful to express the selection differential in standard deviations. If the
top fraction $\pi$ is selected and the trait is normal, the **selection intensity** is

$$
i = \frac{\varphi(z_\pi)}{\pi}
$$

where $z_\pi$ is the truncation point. Then $S = i\,\sigma_P$ and

$$
R = i\,h^2 \sigma_P = i\,h\,\sigma_A
$$

The second form is worth remembering: response is intensity times the *square root* of
heritability times the additive standard deviation.

<WorkedExample
  title="One generation of truncation selection"
  given={[
    { label: 'Phenotypic SD σ_P', value: '10' },
    { label: 'Heritability h²', value: '0.40' },
    { label: 'Selected fraction', value: 'top 10%' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"

**Step 1 — selection intensity.** For the top 10% of a normal distribution,

$$
i = 1.754983
$$

**Step 2 — the selection differential.**

$$
S = i\,\sigma_P = 1.754983 \times 10 = 17.549833
$$

The selected parents average 17.55 units above the population — more than 1.75 standard
deviations, which is what truncating at the top decile buys.

**Step 3 — the response.**

$$
R = h^2 S = 0.40 \times 17.549833 = 7.019933
$$

**Step 4 — the same answer the other way.** With $h = \sqrt{0.40} = 0.632456$ and
$\sigma_A = h\,\sigma_P = 6.324555$:

$$
R = i\,h\,\sigma_A = 1.754983 \times 0.632456 \times 6.324555 = 7.019933
$$

**Step 5 — read the intensity table.** The returns to selecting harder diminish quickly:

| selected | $i$ | $R$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| top 50% | 0.797885 | 3.1915 |
| top 20% | 1.399810 | 5.5992 |
| top 10% | 1.754983 | 7.0199 |
| top 5% | 2.062713 | 8.2509 |
| top 1% | 2.665214 | 10.6609 |

Going from the top half to the top tenth roughly doubles the response; going from the top
tenth to the top hundredth adds only half as much again, while cutting the number of parents
tenfold — which in a real programme means a collapse in effective population size and the
inbreeding that follows.

</WorkedExample>

## Why the equation over-promises

Multiply $R$ by the number of generations and you get a prediction that no breeding
programme has ever met. Three mechanisms account for the shortfall, and they act on
different timescales.

**The Bulmer effect, immediately.** Truncation selection does not only shift the mean; it
truncates the distribution, which reduces its variance. <Citation id="bulmer1971selection" />
showed that selection generates negative linkage disequilibrium between loci with
same-direction effects, reducing $V_A$ below the value the allele frequencies imply. The
reduction appears within one or two generations and then stabilises, so the *second*
generation's response is already smaller than the first's, with no allele frequency change
at all. Relaxing selection restores it, which is how you can tell it apart from the next
mechanism.

**Allele frequency change, over tens of generations.** Selection moves favourable alleles
toward fixation, and $V_A = 2pq\alpha^2$ falls as $p$ approaches 1. This is permanent.

**The selection limit, eventually.** <Citation id="robertson1960limits" /> gave the theory:
the total response achievable is bounded, and the bound depends on $N_e$ through the
competition between selection pushing an allele up and drift losing it. The striking
prediction is that a *smaller* selected fraction gives faster early response but a *lower*
final limit, because it reduces $N_e$ and so loses more favourable alleles to drift. Long-term
selection experiments confirm it.

None of this makes $h^2$ wrong. It makes it a **local** quantity — a statement about one
population, one environment, and one generation, which <Citation id="visscher2008" />
emphasises precisely because the term is so often read as a permanent property of a trait.
For several traits at once the equation generalises to $\Delta\bar{z} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}^{-1}s$
from <Citation id="lande1983selection" />, which is taken up in the multivariate lesson.

## Exercises

<Exercise n={1} title="A purely additive locus" difficulty="warm-up">

A locus has $a = 5$, $d = 0$ and $p = 0.3$. Compute $M$, $\alpha$, the three breeding
values, $V_A$ and $V_D$. What is special about the relationship between $\alpha$ and $a$?

<div slot="solution">

With $d = 0$ and $q = 0.7$:

$$
M = 5(0.3 - 0.7) + 0 = -2, \qquad \alpha = a + 0 = 5
$$

$$
A_{AA} = 2(0.7)(5) = 7, \quad A_{Aa} = (0.7-0.3)(5) = 2, \quad A_{aa} = -2(0.3)(5) = -3
$$

$$
V_A = 2(0.3)(0.7)(25) = 10.5, \qquad V_D = 0
$$

**What is special:** with no dominance, $\alpha = a$ exactly, and it no longer depends on
allele frequency. That is the only case in which "the effect of the allele" is a
well-defined property of the locus alone. Note also that the breeding values are now exactly
$2q a$, $(q-p)a$, $-2p a$ — evenly spaced, one $\alpha$ apart — so the three genotypic values
lie exactly on the regression line and every residual is zero. Additivity means the fit is
perfect, not that dominance happens to be small.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Three generations of selection" difficulty="core">

Using $\sigma_P = 10$ and $h^2 = 0.40$, a breeder selects the top 10% each generation.
(a) What does the breeder's equation predict after three generations? (b) Give two reasons
the realised gain will be smaller, and say which appears first. (c) The breeder proposes
tightening to the top 1% to go faster. Evaluate.

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** Each generation gives $R = 7.019933$, so three generations predict

$$
3 \times 7.019933 = 21.059800
$$

**(b)** First, the **Bulmer effect**: truncation induces negative disequilibrium among
same-direction loci, cutting $V_A$ and therefore $h^2$ within one or two generations. It
appears immediately and then plateaus. Second, **allele frequency change**: as favourable
alleles rise toward fixation, $2pq$ falls and with it $V_A$. This is slower and permanent.
The Bulmer effect appears first, which is why the second generation already underperforms.

**(c)** Tightening to the top 1% raises $i$ from 1.754983 to 2.665214, so the per-generation
prediction rises from 7.0199 to 10.6609 — about 52% faster. But it also cuts the number of
parents tenfold, which reduces $N_e$ and, by Robertson's argument, *lowers the eventual
limit*: more favourable alleles are lost to drift before selection can fix them. Faster
early, less in total.

The right answer depends on the horizon. For a two-generation project, tighten. For a
long-term programme, the top 10% with attention to effective population size will end up
further ahead — and this is exactly the trade-off that made effective population size a
managed quantity in animal breeding rather than an incidental one.

</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="A large-effect locus with no additive variance" difficulty="stretch">

Consider an overdominant locus with $a = 4$ and $d = 10$: the heterozygote exceeds both
homozygotes. (a) Find the allele frequency at which $\alpha = 0$. (b) Compute $V_A$ and
$V_D$ there. (c) What would a standard additive association test see at this locus, and what
does that imply?

<div slot="solution">

**(a)** $\alpha = a + d(q - p) = 4 + 10(1 - 2p)$, which is zero at

$$
p = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{a}{d}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + 0.4) = 0.70
$$

**(b)** At $p = 0.7$, $q = 0.3$:

$$
V_A = 2pq\,\alpha^2 = 0, \qquad V_D = (2pq\,d)^2 = (0.42 \times 10)^2 = 4.2^2 = 17.64
$$

The locus contributes **no additive variance at all**, while contributing 17.64 units of
dominance variance. Its genotypic values span 14 units, from $-4$ at $aa$ to $+10$ at the heterozygote — this
is not a small-effect locus.

**(c)** An additive test regresses phenotype on allele dosage. Its slope *is* $\alpha$, which
is exactly zero here, so the test sees nothing — no association, no matter how large the
sample. The variant would be absent from every GWAS catalogue, absent from every polygenic
score, and would contribute nothing to SNP heritability.

Two implications follow. First, "no additive variance" is not "no effect", and the two are
routinely conflated. Second, the situation is not a curiosity: overdominance is exactly the
gene action that maintains polymorphism, and $p = 0.7$ here is the equilibrium frequency
balancing selection would produce — so a locus under this kind of selection sits precisely
where the additive test is blind. Detecting it requires a genotypic test with two degrees of
freedom, which most association pipelines do not run because it costs power everywhere else.

</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- **A parent transmits alleles, not genotypes**, so only the part of a genotypic value that
  travels with single alleles is inherited. Separating that part is what quantitative
  genetics is for.
- **The average effect is a regression slope**: regress genotypic value on allele count,
  weighted by genotype frequency, and $\alpha = a + d(q-p)$ falls out. Fitted values are
  breeding values; residuals are dominance deviations; both average to zero.
- **$V_A = 2pq\alpha^2$ and $V_D = (2pq d)^2$ are properties of a population**, not of a
  locus. The same locus contributes 40.6272 of additive variance at $p = 0.6$ and none at
  all at fixation.
- **Most genetic variance is additive even when the biology is not.** A regression through
  non-additive gene action still has a slope at almost every allele frequency, so $V_A$
  captures most of the variance. This is a fact about variance, not about mechanism.
- **The breeder's equation is $R = h^2 S = i\,h\,\sigma_A$.** Selecting the top 10% of a
  trait with $\sigma_P = 10$ and $h^2 = 0.4$ returns 7.0199 units per generation, and
  selecting harder yields diminishing returns.
- **Realised response always falls short.** The Bulmer effect cuts $V_A$ within a generation
  or two and reverses if selection is relaxed; allele frequency change cuts it permanently;
  and Robertson's limit means a tighter selected fraction buys speed at the cost of the
  eventual total.
