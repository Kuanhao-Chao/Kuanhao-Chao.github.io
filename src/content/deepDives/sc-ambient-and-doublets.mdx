## The question this lesson answers

Noise is not the dangerous thing in a single-cell dataset. Noise scatters, and scattered points
do not cluster.

The dangerous artefacts are the ones that are *coherent* — that push many cells in the same
direction, survive every quality filter, form a tight group in the embedding and annotate as
something a reviewer will believe. There are two of them, and between them they account for a
large share of the cell types in the literature that no one has been able to find again.

## The soup, and what it is made of

Dissociation kills cells. Every one that lyses releases its transcripts into the buffer, and
the suspension that goes into the instrument is therefore a mixture of intact cells and free
mRNA. Each droplet captures its cell *and* a sample of the surrounding fluid, so every barcode
in the run carries some ambient counts <Citation id="young2020soupx" />. Nothing about the
chemistry prevents this — the partition is a boundary around a volume of buffer, not around a
cell <Citation id="zheng2017chromium" />.

The soup is not arbitrary. It is the tissue's average, weighted by how much each type
contributed to it:

$$
s_g = \sum_t f_t \, x_{gt},
$$

the share of the soup that is gene *g*. A type that is abundant, or fragile and therefore
over-represented among the lysed, dominates the soup — and its markers are exactly the genes
that will show up everywhere else.

If a fraction $\alpha$ of barcode *j*'s counts come from the soup, the expected ambient count
of gene *g* in that barcode is

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{ambient}} = n_j \, \alpha \, s_g,
$$

and by the Poisson model of the last lesson it is **detected at all** — one count or more —
with probability

$$
\Pr(\text{detected}) = 1 - e^{-n_j \alpha s_g}.
$$

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are counter-intuitive enough to have caused real
errors.

**Contamination scales with depth.** $\lambda_{\mathrm{ambient}}$ is proportional to $n_j$. A
deeply sequenced cell picks up proportionally more soup, so it crosses the detection threshold
for more foreign markers. Deep cells look more "multi-lineage" than shallow ones, for a purely
technical reason, and any annotation done on presence/absence of markers will systematically
mis-assign them.

**Detection saturates.** Because detection is $1 - e^{-\lambda}$, it climbs steeply and then
flattens. Halving the ambient fraction does not halve the number of cells showing a marker; in
the saturated regime it barely moves it. Contamination must be removed from the counts, not
diluted.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="The empty droplets are the control">

The reason ambient contamination is correctable at all is that the experiment contains tens of
thousands of measurements of the soup on its own: the empty droplets.

A run producing 5,000 cells uses hundreds of thousands of partitions, and the great majority
catch no cell. What they catch is soup — an unbiased sample of $s_g$, at high total depth
because there are so many of them. That is what makes the soup profile estimable rather than
assumed, and it is why cell calling and ambient correction are the same problem seen from two
sides: deciding which barcodes are cells *is* deciding which barcodes are pure soup
<Citation id="lun2019emptydrops" />.

Correction methods differ in what they do with that estimate — SoupX fits a single
contamination fraction against marker genes known to be off in some cells
<Citation id="young2020soupx" />, while CellBender models the counts of every barcode as a
mixture of cell and soup and removes the soup component
<Citation id="fleming2023cellbender" /> — but both rest on the same fact: the control is
already in the data.

</Callout>

## Doublets do not look like noise, they look like a cell type

Lesson 1 priced the multiplet rate: at a 62,500-partition device recovering 10,000 cells it is
8.46%, and hashing four samples brings the residual to 2.26%. This lesson is about what the
survivors do.

A barcode holding one T cell and one monocyte produces a count vector that is the sum of the
two. Normalised to its own depth, it is roughly the *average* of the two profiles — it
expresses T-cell markers at about half their usual level and monocyte markers at about half of
theirs. In an embedding it lands between the two parent clusters, and because doublets of the
same two types all land in the same place, they form a **cluster**.

That cluster is tight, reproducible across replicates, and expresses a coherent combination of
markers. Every property that normally indicates a real population is present. Published
"transitional states", "hybrid lineages" and "dual-positive" populations have repeatedly turned
out to be exactly this <Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />.

### Depth alone cannot find them

The obvious diagnostic is that two cells give twice the RNA. It is true on average and nearly
useless in practice, and the reason is worth deriving because the arithmetic is exact.

Take singlet depth to be Gamma-distributed with shape $k$ and scale $s$ — a reasonable and
convenient family, with mean $ks$. A doublet is the sum of two independent cells, and the sum
of two independent Gammas with the same scale is *exactly* Gamma with the shapes added:

$$
D_{\mathrm{singlet}} \sim \Gamma(k, s) \implies D_{\mathrm{doublet}} \sim \Gamma(2k, s).
$$

So the doublet mean is $2ks$ — the modes are a factor of two apart, as expected. But the
standard deviation is $\sqrt{2k}\,s$, only $\sqrt{2}$ times the singlet's, so in units of its
own spread the doublet distribution is *narrower* while sitting on top of the singlet tail.
The overlap is severe, and the price of a threshold is paid in both currencies at once.

<WorkedExample
  title="A marker that means nothing"
  given={[
    'Gene G is 2% of cell type A’s transcripts, and off in type B',
    'Type A is 20% of the sample',
    'Ambient fraction 10%; type B cells sequenced to 5,000 UMIs',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — G's share of the soup.** The soup is the composition-weighted average, so the only
contributor is type A:

$$
s_G = f_A \, x_{GA} = 0.20 \times 0.02 = 0.004,
$$

**0.40%** of the soup.

**Step 2 — how many ambient counts a type-B cell gets.** Of its 5,000 counts, 10% are soup, so
500 counts are drawn from the soup profile, of which

$$
\lambda = 5{,}000 \times 0.10 \times 0.004 = 2.0
$$

are gene G.

**Step 3 — how often that is visible.**

$$
\Pr(\text{at least one}) = 1 - e^{-2.0} = 0.864665,
$$

so **86.5%** of type-B cells carry at least one count of a marker they do not express. On a
presence/absence criterion, type B is positive for A's marker.

**Step 4 — the depth trap.** The same calculation at 20,000 UMIs gives $\lambda = 8.0$ and a
detection rate of **99.97%**; at 1,000 UMIs it gives $\lambda = 0.4$ and **33.0%**. The
apparent marker expression of a cell type is largely a statement about how deeply it was
sequenced — and cell types differ systematically in depth, because they differ in RNA content.

**What this rules out.** Any annotation rule of the form "cells expressing G are type A". At
these numbers, most type-B cells express G. The usable statement is about *level* relative to
the ambient expectation, not about presence, which is why marker-based annotation has to be
quantitative and is the subject of lesson 11.

</WorkedExample>

## Telling the two apart, and what actually works

The signatures are different, which is fortunate because the remedies are.

**Ambient contamination** is proportional to the barcode's own depth and has the *same profile*
in every barcode — the soup profile. It therefore affects all cells, correlates with depth, and
shows up as low-level expression of the most abundant types' markers everywhere. It is
corrected by estimating $s_g$ from empty droplets and subtracting.

**Doublets** are a property of individual barcodes, not a gradient. They carry roughly twice
the depth, express two markers at roughly full complementary strength rather than at a trace
level, and — the useful part — their profiles are *reconstructible*. That is what the
computational detectors exploit: simulate doublets by adding pairs of observed cells together,
embed them alongside the real data, and score each real barcode by how many simulated doublets
are among its neighbours <Citation id="wolock2019scrublet" />
<Citation id="mcginnis2019doubletfinder" />.

That approach has a hard limit, and it is the same one lesson 1 derived. A doublet of two cells
*of the same type* has the profile of a single cell of that type at twice the depth. There is
no expression signature to find, because there is no mixture. Simulation-based detectors
recover heterotypic doublets and are blind to homotypic ones by construction — and homotypic
doublets are the majority whenever one cell type dominates the sample.

Which is why the real answer is experimental. Hashing samples with barcoded antibodies
<Citation id="stoeckius2018hashing" />, or demultiplexing pooled donors by their genotypes,
makes every doublet spanning two samples detectable regardless of cell type — the
$(S-1)/S$ fraction from lesson 1. Those methods do not compete with computational detection;
they catch a different, larger set, and the two compose.

## Summary

- The ambient soup is the composition-weighted average of the sample,
  $s_g = \sum_t f_t x_{gt}$, sampled by every barcode in proportion to its depth.
- A marker at 2% of a type that is 20% of the sample is **0.40% of the soup**, which at a 10%
  ambient fraction and 5,000 UMIs appears in **86.5%** of cells of every other type.
- Contamination **scales with depth** and **saturates**, so deep cells look more multi-lineage
  and halving the ambient fraction does not halve the artefact.
- The empty droplets are a large, unbiased measurement of the soup, which is why ambient
  correction and cell calling are the same problem.
- A doublet's depth is **exactly** $\Gamma(2k, s)$ when singlets are $\Gamma(k, s)$. Keeping
  95% of singlets catches **48.8%** of doublets; catching 90% of doublets costs **31.7%** of
  singlets. Depth is a weak diagnostic.
- Simulation-based detectors find **heterotypic** doublets only. Homotypic doublets have no
  expression signature, and hashing or genetic demultiplexing is the only thing that catches
  them.

<Exercise n={1} title="A fragile cell type poisons the well" difficulty="warm-up">

Hepatocytes make up 60% of a liver sample and are fragile, so they are over-represented in the
soup; albumin is 5% of a hepatocyte's transcripts.

(a) What share of the soup is albumin, assuming the soup matches the tissue composition?

(b) A Kupffer cell sequenced to 8,000 UMIs sits in a run with a 5% ambient fraction. How many
albumin counts does it expect, and how often does it show at least one?

(c) A reviewer asks whether these Kupffer cells are "albumin-positive". Answer.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $s = 0.60 \times 0.05 = 0.03$, so **3% of the soup** is albumin.

(b) $\lambda = 8{,}000 \times 0.05 \times 0.03 = 12$ counts. $1 - e^{-12} = 0.99999386$, so
**essentially every** Kupffer cell shows albumin — and not one count, but around twelve.

(c) No. Twelve counts is exactly what the ambient model predicts for a cell expressing none,
so the observation carries no evidence either way. The question is only answerable by comparing
the observed level against the ambient expectation — a Kupffer cell would need substantially
*more* than twelve counts to be called positive. This is also the case where the soup is worst:
the dominant type's markers contaminate at a level that looks like real expression.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Pricing a depth filter honestly" difficulty="core">

Singlet depth is $\Gamma(4, 1250)$ and the run has a 10% doublet rate among 10,000 barcodes.

(a) How many doublets are present?

(b) A threshold at the singlet 95th percentile, 9,692 UMIs, catches 48.8% of doublets. How many
doublets are removed, how many good cells are lost, and what is the doublet rate afterwards?

(c) Was the filter worth applying?

<div slot="solution">

(a) 10% of 10,000 is **1,000 doublets**, leaving 9,000 singlets.

(b) It removes $0.488 \times 1{,}000 = 488$ doublets and $0.05 \times 9{,}000 = 450$ singlets.
What survives is $8{,}550$ singlets and $512$ doublets, or

$$
\frac{512}{9{,}062} = 5.65\%,
$$

down from 10%.

(c) Marginally. It nearly halved the contamination, but it cost 450 real cells and it removed
them non-randomly — the discarded singlets are the deepest ones, which are disproportionately
the largest, most transcriptionally active cell types. A filter that preferentially deletes one
cell type while removing half of an artefact is not obviously an improvement, and a
simulation-based detector applied to the same data would remove the heterotypic doublets
without a depth bias. Depth filtering is best used as a diagnostic that doublets exist, not as
the remedy.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The homotypic blind spot" difficulty="stretch">

A sample is 70% T cells, 20% monocytes and 10% B cells, and has an 8% doublet rate.

(a) Assuming cells pair independently of type, what fraction of doublets are homotypic?

(b) A simulation-based detector removes every heterotypic doublet and no homotypic one. What is
the residual doublet rate?

(c) The same sample is run as a pool of eight hashed donors. What is the residual then, and why
does the answer not depend on the cell-type composition?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Two cells drawn independently match when both are T, both mono or both B:

$$
0.70^2 + 0.20^2 + 0.10^2 = 0.49 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.54,
$$

so **54%** of doublets are homotypic — a majority, because one type dominates.

(b) 46% of the doublets are removed. Starting from 8% in 10,000 barcodes: 800 doublets, 368
removed, 432 remaining among 9,632 barcodes, or

$$
\frac{432}{9{,}632} = 4.49\%.
$$

Nearly half the problem is untouched, and what remains is invisible to the method by
construction.

(c) Hashing detects any doublet whose two cells came from different donors, which is
$(S-1)/S = 7/8$ of them regardless of what types they are — the detection depends on the donor
labels, not on expression. That leaves $800/8 = 100$ doublets among $10{,}000 - 700 = 9{,}300$
barcodes, or **1.08%**. The two methods are complementary: hashing is blind to same-donor
doublets and simulation is blind to same-type ones, and running both leaves only doublets that
are same-donor *and* same-type.

</div>

</Exercise>
