## The question this lesson answers

An experiment treats four mice and leaves four untreated, and twenty thousand cells come back
from each condition. A gene is tested for a difference between conditions. How many
observations does that test have?

The default answer, and the one both major toolkits make easy, is twenty thousand per group
<Citation id="wolf2018scanpy" />
<Citation id="hao2021seurat" />. The right answer is four. Everything else in this lesson
follows from the gap between those two numbers.

## Where the design effect comes from

Write a cell's measurement as a sample effect plus a cell effect:

$$
y_{ij} = \mu + a_i + e_{ij},
\qquad
a_i \sim (0, \sigma_b^2),
\qquad
e_{ij} \sim (0, \sigma_w^2),
$$

with $a_i$ shared by every cell of sample $i$. Two cells from the same sample have covariance
$\sigma_b^2$; two from different samples have none. That shared term is everything the sample
has in common — its genotype, its age, its treatment history, its dissociation batch, its
sequencing run.

The mean of one sample's $m$ cells has variance $\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2/m$, so the mean of $n$
such samples has

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{y}) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\sigma_b^2 + \frac{\sigma_w^2}{m}\right).
$$

A per-cell analysis instead treats all $nm$ cells as independent draws of total variance
$\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2$, giving $(\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2)/(nm)$. The ratio of the truth to
that belief is

$$
\mathrm{DE}
= \frac{\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2/m}{(\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2)/m}
= 1 + (m-1)\rho,
\qquad
\rho = \frac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2}.
$$

**Linear in $m$, and unbounded.** Every standard error computed as though cells were
independent is too small by $\sqrt{\mathrm{DE}}$, so a two-sided test at nominal $\alpha$ has
false-positive rate

$$
\mathrm{FPR} = 2\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{-z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{1 + (m-1)\rho}}\right),
$$

which increases monotonically in $m$ with limit 1. **More cells make the test worse.** That is
not a figure of speech; it is what the formula says, and it is why lists of thousands of
differentially expressed genes with vanishing p-values are the field's most reproducible
artefact <Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />.

## The simulation

The derivation assumes a variance structure. It is worth checking that data generated with
that structure behaves as predicted, because if it does then nothing about the argument depends
on the reader accepting an algebraic step.

There is nothing in these datasets to find. Every gene is null by construction; the only
structure is that cells from a donor share that donor's offset. The per-cell test finds
differences anyway, at exactly the rate the design effect predicts.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Ask how many times the experiment was done">

The cure is a question rather than a method: **how many times was this experiment performed?**

Four mice treated and four untreated is eight times. Sequencing twenty thousand cells from each
does not make it eighty thousand times; it makes it eight very precise measurements. The number
of cells controls how well each of the eight was measured, and the number of mice controls how
many measurements there are — and it is the second that a test between conditions runs on.

Everything in the rest of this lesson is that sentence made arithmetic.

</Callout>

## Aggregating costs nothing

The reflex objection to pseudobulk is that it throws away 99.99% of the data. It does not throw
away any of it, and this is provable rather than a matter of taste.

For a balanced design — the same $m$ for every sample — the mean of the sample means is
identically the mean of all cells:

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_j y_{ij}\right) = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} y_{ij}.
$$

The two estimators are the *same number*. Across eight hundred simulated datasets the two
never differed by more than $9.4 \times 10^{-16}$, which is floating-point noise and not a
statistical difference. Under the mixed model this aggregate is also the best linear unbiased
estimator of the group mean, so nothing is lost in the technical sense either
<Citation id="crowell2020muscat" />.

What changes is only the standard error — and the standard error was the thing that was wrong.

<WorkedExample
  title="What the two analyses charge"
  given={[
    'Four donors per group, 200 cells from each',
    'Intra-donor correlation ρ = 0.05',
    'A two-sided test at nominal 5%',
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/deepDiveExamples.test.ts"
>

**Step 1 — the design effect.**

$$
\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (200 - 1)(0.05) = 10.95.
$$

**Step 2 — what the per-cell analysis gets wrong.** Its standard error is too small by
$\sqrt{10.95} = 3.309078$ — that is, **230.9%** too small — and its false-positive rate is

$$
2\Phi\!\left(\frac{-1.959964}{3.309078}\right) = 0.5537,
$$

so **55.4%** of null genes are reported as significant. The simulation of Figure 1 returns
56.6% at this setting.

**Step 3 — what the correct analysis charges.** The point estimate is unchanged, so the only
change is the reference distribution: with four donors per group there are $2(4) - 2 = 6$
degrees of freedom instead of $2(4)(200) - 2 = 1{,}598$. The critical value moves from
$z = 1.959964$ to $t_{0.975,\,6} = 2.446912$, widening the interval by

$$
\frac{2.446912}{1.959964} - 1 = 0.2484,
$$

**24.84%**.

**Step 4 — the comparison.** Doing it correctly costs a quarter more width. Doing it
incorrectly reports a standard error three and a third times too small, and a false-positive
rate eleven times the nominal one. Those are the two options, and they are not close.

**Step 5 — what the cells were for.** The 200 cells per donor are not wasted. They make each
donor's value precise: that donor's mean has variance $\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2/200$, and the
second term is nearly gone. What they cannot do is create donors. Cells buy precision within a
sample; samples buy evidence about the population.

</WorkedExample>

## Estimating ρ instead of assuming it

Everything above depends on $\rho$, and $\rho$ is measurable from the same data. A one-way
analysis of variance of cells within samples gives

$$
\hat{\rho} = \frac{\mathrm{MSB} - \mathrm{MSW}}{\mathrm{MSB} + (m-1)\mathrm{MSW}},
$$

which needs nothing the per-cell analysis has not already computed. There is therefore no
excuse for not knowing it, and reporting it per gene — or its distribution across genes —
would let a reader judge a differential expression result in one line.

The practical range in real data is 0.01 to 0.20 depending on the gene and the tissue, so
almost nothing sits at the $\rho \approx 0$ where a per-cell test would be safe
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />. Reporting it is the single cheapest thing a
paper can do to make its differential expression checkable
<Citation id="luecken2019bestpractices" />. Note also what $\rho$ is *not*: it is not a
property of the technology, and it does not shrink with better normalisation
<Citation id="hafemeister2019sctransform" />
<Citation id="lause2021pearson" /> or with a better count model
<Citation id="svensson2020zeroinflation" />. It is the fraction of a gene's variance that lives
between animals, and that is biology.

## Summary

- Cells within a sample share the sample's effect, so the variance of a group mean is inflated
  by $\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (m-1)\rho$ — **linear in cells per sample and unbounded**.
- A per-cell test therefore has false-positive rate $2\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt{\mathrm{DE}})$,
  which **rises with the number of cells**. Simulation confirms it: 29.5% at fifty cells per
  sample, 56.6% at two hundred, against a theory of 29.1% and 55.4%.
- **Aggregating to one value per sample loses no information about the effect.** For a balanced
  design the mean of sample means is identically the grand mean — across 800 simulations the
  two never differed by more than $9.4 \times 10^{-16}$ — and it is the BLUE under the model.
- The entire price is degrees of freedom: 6 instead of 1,598, so the interval widens
  **24.84%**. The alternative leaves the standard error **230.9%** too small.
- $\rho$ is estimable by one-way ANOVA from data the analysis already has. Real values run 0.01
  to 0.20, and no normalisation or count model reduces it, because it is biology.

<Exercise n={1} title="Two designs, same cell count" difficulty="warm-up">

Two studies each sequence 4,000 cells per group at $\rho = 0.05$. Study A uses 2 donors of
2,000 cells; study B uses 20 donors of 200 cells.

(a) Compute the design effect for each.

(b) Compute the false-positive rate of a per-cell test in each.

(c) Which study is better powered, and by roughly how much?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Study A: $1 + 1999(0.05) = 100.95$. Study B: $1 + 199(0.05) = 10.95$.

(b) Study A: $2\Phi(-1.959964/\sqrt{100.95}) = 0.8453$, so **84.5%**. Study B: 0.5537, so
**55.4%**. Both are catastrophic, which is the point — the per-cell test is not rescued by a
better design, only made less absurd.

(c) Compare the analyses that are actually valid. Study A has 2 degrees of freedom and study B
has 38, and effective sample size is $nm/\mathrm{DE}$: **39.62** for A against **365.30** for
B, a factor of **9.22**. Same cells, same reagent cost, nine times the evidence — because the
currency is donors.

<Exercise n={2} title="Reading a reported result" difficulty="core">

A paper reports 4,200 differentially expressed genes at FDR < 0.05, from 3 patients per group
with about 1,500 cells each, analysed per cell.

(a) Estimate the design effect at $\rho = 0.05$ and the per-cell false-positive rate.

(b) The authors argue FDR control handles this. Respond.

(c) Roughly how many of the 4,200 would you expect to survive a correct analysis, and what
would you need to know to say?

<div slot="solution">

(a) $\mathrm{DE} = 1 + 1499(0.05) = 75.95$ and
$\mathrm{FPR} = 2\Phi(-1.959964/\sqrt{75.95}) = 0.8221$, so **82.2%** of null genes are
called significant before any correction.

(b) FDR procedures control the false discovery rate *given valid p-values*. Here every p-value
is computed from a standard error $\sqrt{75.95} = 8.715$ times too small, so they are not
p-values in the required sense — they are not uniform under the null. Benjamini–Hochberg
applied to them controls nothing, because its guarantee is conditional on exactly the property
that has failed. The same objection defeats Bonferroni, permutation over cells, and any other
multiplicity method: they fix the number of tests, not the calibration of each.

(c) Not answerable from what is reported, and that is the honest answer. With 3 patients per
group there are 4 degrees of freedom, so only very large effects will clear the correct
threshold; published re-analyses of this design typically retain a small fraction
<Citation id="squair2021falsediscoveries" />. To say more you would need $\rho$ per gene —
estimable by one-way ANOVA from their own data — and the effect sizes at the patient level.

</div>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Where the next money goes" difficulty="stretch">

A pilot has 4 donors per group and 500 cells each, at $\rho = 0.05$. There is budget for either
4 more donors per group or a doubling of cells per donor.

(a) Compute the effective sample size now, and under each option.

(b) Which wins, and by how much?

(c) Give a circumstance in which more cells would nevertheless be the right purchase.

<div slot="solution">

(a) $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = nm/(1 + (m-1)\rho)$. Now: $4 \times 500/25.95 = 77.07$. Doubling the
donors to 8: $8 \times 500/25.95 = 154.14$. Doubling the cells to 1,000:
$4 \times 1000/50.95 = 78.51$.

(b) Donors, overwhelmingly. Four more donors buy **77.07** effective cells; twice as many cells
buy **1.44**. That is a factor of **53.6** for the same budget line, and it gets worse as cells
accumulate, because the ceiling is $n/\rho = 80$ and 500 cells per donor has already reached
96.3% of it.

(c) When the question is not about the population. If you need to characterise a rare
subpopulation — describe its states, find its markers, resolve its substructure — that is a
within-sample question and cells are exactly what buys it. Lessons 7 to 9 showed a rare
population needs cells to survive dimensionality reduction, graph construction and clustering
at all. The rule is that cells buy resolution and samples buy power, and a study usually needs
both, but only one of them is on the critical path for a test between conditions.

</div>

</Exercise>
