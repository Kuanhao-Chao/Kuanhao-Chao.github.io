## The map: choose the operations before the family

“Generate realistic samples” is not one objective. An application may need normalized likelihood,
fast sampling, a meaningful encoder, exact reconstruction, conditional control, uncertainty or a
feature representation. Each family makes a different subset convenient and pays elsewhere.

<Callout variant="intuition" title="Every generator moves probability mass">
An autoregressive model moves through coordinates in an order. A VAE moves through a stochastic
latent bottleneck. A GAN learns a sampler by a game. A flow transports a base density through an
invertible map. Diffusion deliberately destroys structure and learns to reverse that destruction.

Interview strength comes from connecting that path to what can be computed, optimized and tested.
</Callout>

## What is the model required to compute?

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-density-implicit-representation">
An **explicit-density** model defines $p_\theta(x)$ directly or a tractable lower bound. It can
compare log scores, but a high likelihood need not mean perceptually good samples. An **implicit**
model defines a sampling procedure without a tractable normalized density; the original GAN is the
canonical example. A **self-supervised representation** objective predicts held-out structure or
matches views and need not estimate all of $p(x)$.

The categories overlap: a VAE has a density bound and encoder; an autoregressive model can supply
features; diffusion admits likelihood-related formulations; a representation can condition a
generator. Begin an interview design by listing required operations: `score(x)`, `sample()`,
`encode(x)`, reconstruct, condition, edit and serve. The operation table is more informative than
calling one family “generative.”
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-autoregressive-factorization">
The probability chain rule gives, for any chosen order,

$$
p(x_1,\ldots,x_D)=\prod_{i=1}^{D}p(x_i\mid x_{<i}).
$$

Maximum likelihood is a sum of conditional negative log-likelihoods. With a causal mask, every
training position can be scored in parallel because true earlier values are known. Generation is
sequential: sample $x_1$, append it, then sample $x_2$, and so on. The factorization is exact, but
the parameterized conditionals and optimization are approximate.

Ordering and tokenization are inductive biases. Raster order makes image pixels far apart across a
row boundary; subword choices alter sequence length and likelihood units. Report bits per dimension
or per token with preprocessing specified, and do not compare likelihoods under different
dequantization or tokenization contracts as if they were identical.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Variational autoencoders

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-vae-elbo">
Start from $p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x,z)dz$ and introduce $q_\phi(z\mid x)$:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]
+D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x)).
$$

The nonnegative second term leaves the evidence lower bound

$$
\mathcal L_{ELBO}=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)).
$$

The first term is a log likelihood, not universally pixel MSE; Gaussian, Bernoulli or categorical
choices encode different observation assumptions. The KL makes approximate posteriors compatible
with the prior. Kingma and Welling paired this bound with amortized neural inference
<Citation id="kingma2014vae" />. A loose bound can reflect an inference-family limitation rather
than only a poor generator.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-reparameterization">
A Monte Carlo sample $z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x)$ appears to interrupt differentiation through $\phi$.
For a diagonal Gaussian, move randomness into a fixed distribution:

$$
\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad
z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon.
$$

The sample is now a differentiable function of $\mu,\sigma$ and external noise, giving a low-variance
pathwise gradient. Parameterize `log_variance`, recover $\sigma=\exp(0.5\log\sigma^2)$, and avoid
an unconstrained negative standard deviation. Discrete latents require score-function estimators,
continuous relaxations or specialized reparameterizations; the Gaussian trick is not universal.
</InterviewQuestion>

<WorkedExample
  title="One-dimensional VAE KL and ELBO"
  given={[
    { label: 'Approximate posterior', value: 'q(z|x) = Normal(μ=1, σ=0.5)' },
    { label: 'Prior', value: 'p(z) = Normal(0, 1)' },
    { label: 'Expected reconstruction log likelihood', value: 'E_q log p(x|z) = -1.2 nats' },
  ]}
  verifiedBy="src/lib/mlInterviewExamples.test.ts"
>
For diagonal Gaussians,

$$
D_{KL}(q\Vert p)=\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2-\log\sigma^2-1).
$$

Substitution gives
$\tfrac12(1+0.25-\log0.25-1)=0.8181$ nats. Therefore
$\mathcal L_{ELBO}=-1.2-0.8181=-2.0181$ nats and the negative ELBO loss is $2.0181$.
Changing log base would change the unit; dropping the factor one-half would be a derivation bug.
</WorkedExample>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-posterior-collapse">
Posterior collapse means $q_\phi(z\mid x)\approx p(z)$ and the decoder output barely depends on
$z$. It is common when an expressive autoregressive decoder can model $x$ without paying KL for
information. A small average KL is a clue; inspect per-dimension KL, active units, latent
interventions, reconstructions and an estimate of information between $x$ and $z$.

KL warmup delays regularization; free bits tolerate a minimum information budget; weakening the
decoder increases pressure to use $z$; richer priors reduce aggregate mismatch. Each changes the
objective or capacity and can trade likelihood against representation. Also distinguish collapse
from the opposite failure: a highly informative $q(z\mid x)$ whose aggregate distribution does not
match the prior, causing poor prior samples.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Adversarial games and invertible transport

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-gan-objective">
The original minimax objective is

$$
\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{data}}\log D(x)
+\mathbb E_{z\sim p(z)}\log(1-D(G(z))).
$$

For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator is
$D^*(x)=p_{data}(x)/(p_{data}(x)+p_g(x))$. Substitution yields a constant plus twice the
Jensen-Shannon divergence, with global optimum $p_g=p_{data}$ and $D=1/2$. This is the idealized
result in the founding GAN paper <Citation id="goodfellow2014gan" />.

Finite alternating updates do not keep $D$ optimal, neural families are restricted and supports may
barely overlap. The commonly used non-saturating generator maximizes $\log D(G(z))$ for stronger
early gradients; it has the same ideal fixed point but different optimization dynamics.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-gan-collapse-stability">
GAN training follows a vector field for two objectives, so updates can rotate, oscillate or let one
player overpower the other. Mode collapse occurs when many $z$ values map to a few outputs that
currently fool $D$. Diagnose with nearest-neighbor checks, latent interpolation, class or feature
coverage, discriminator margins, gradient norms, multiple seeds and held-out data—not one curated
sample grid.

Balanced update rates, gradient penalties, spectral constraints, alternative divergences and replay
can improve a particular failure, but none proves distribution coverage. If discriminator accuracy
is perfect, it may exploit a trivial artifact; if it is one-half, the generator may have succeeded
or the discriminator may be broken. Inspect losses together with samples and controlled tests.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-normalizing-flows">
Let $x=f(z)$ be differentiable and invertible. Change of variables gives

$$
\log p_X(x)=\log p_Z(f^{-1}(x))
+\log\left|\det J_{f^{-1}}(x)\right|.
$$

For a composition, log determinants add. Real NVP uses coupling transformations whose triangular
Jacobians make determinants cheap while alternating partitions mixes all coordinates
<Citation id="dinh2017realnvp" />. Depending on direction, autoregressive flows make either
sampling or density evaluation parallel and the other sequential.

Exact likelihood and reconstruction require matching dimensions and invertibility. This can force
architecture constraints and awkward treatment of discrete data or lower-dimensional manifolds.
“Exact” describes computation under the model, not that its density matches reality.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Denoising diffusion

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-diffusion-forward-reverse">
A DDPM fixes a forward Markov chain that adds Gaussian noise. With
$\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$,

$$
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

A network predicts noise, score, velocity or clean data from $(x_t,t)$; the learned reverse
transition denoises toward data. The widely used noise MSE is a weighted form connected to the
variational bound. Ho, Jain and Abbeel established the modern DDPM formulation and its denoising
parameterization <Citation id="ho2020ddpm" />.

Training samples a random $t$ directly; it need not simulate every earlier step. Generation starts
from noise and integrates many reverse steps, so sampler discretization, schedule and parameterization
affect speed and error even with fixed network weights.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-diffusion-guidance">
Conditional diffusion supplies class, text or another observation to the denoiser. Classifier-free
guidance combines conditional and unconditional predictions, schematically
$\hat\epsilon=(1+w)\epsilon_{cond}-w\epsilon_{uncond}$. Increasing $w$ often strengthens condition
adherence and apparent sharpness but narrows sample diversity and can introduce saturation or
artifacts.

Separate four choices: conditioning representation, model objective, reverse sampler and guidance
rule. Evaluate control accuracy, quality, coverage and latency across guidance scales at fixed random
seeds and step budgets. Guidance is a sampling-time tilt, not evidence that the base conditional
density is calibrated.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Self-supervised representations

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-infonce">
For anchor $z_i$, positive $z_i^+$, candidate set $\mathcal K$ and similarity $s$,

$$
\mathcal L_i=-\log\frac{e^{s(z_i,z_i^+)/\tau}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal K}e^{s(z_i,z_k)/\tau}}.
$$

Lower temperature concentrates probability and gradients on hard competitors. A large, representative
negative dictionary gives a stronger discrimination task, while false negatives can repel examples
that share semantics. Normalize embeddings if cosine similarity is intended; otherwise norm becomes
another route to alter logits. SimCLR showed the impact of view composition, projection heads and
large contrastive batches <Citation id="chen2020simclr" />.

Positive views declare what should be invariant. For pathology, cropping away a lesion is not a
positive view. For identity, background may be a shortcut. Validate that learned invariances match
the downstream task.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-self-supervised-families">
Contrastive methods match positives against alternatives. Masked prediction reconstructs hidden
tokens or patches from context. Self-distillation aligns student predictions with a teacher view and
needs centering, sharpening, architectural asymmetry or stop-gradient to avoid constant outputs.
Predictive methods can target future or transformed representations instead of raw input.

MoCo uses a momentum encoder and queue to build a large, consistent negative dictionary
<Citation id="he2020moco" />. That differs operationally from in-batch SimCLR but shares a
contrastive principle. Compare methods using frozen probes, fine-tuning at several label budgets,
shift and robustness tests, and equal pretraining compute. Pretext losses across different objectives
are not commensurate.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Evaluation and family choice

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-evaluation">
Generative evaluation has at least fidelity, coverage, memorization, conditional correctness and
utility axes. Log likelihood is a proper density score under a common data transform, yet can
disagree with perception. FID fits Gaussians to features of real and generated samples and compares
their means and covariances; it depends on feature extractor, preprocessing and sample count. It was
introduced with the GAN two-timescale analysis <Citation id="heusel2017fid" />.

Use precision-recall-style coverage measures, nearest training neighbors, duplicate rates, subgroup
metrics and blinded human preference with inter-rater reliability. For conditional generation,
measure condition adherence and counterfactual sensitivity. Report confidence intervals and exact
sample counts. A scalar ranking without these qualifiers invites metric gaming.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion id="q-gen-family-selection">
Build a requirements matrix:

- Autoregressive: tractable likelihood, flexible data; sequential sampling.
- VAE: amortized encoder and smooth latent operations; bound and observation-model tradeoffs.
- GAN: fast parallel sampling and sharp outputs; no density and unstable coverage.
- Flow: exact density, sampling and inversion; restrictive invertible transforms.
- Diffusion: flexible denoising and control; iterative sampling cost.

Then test the smallest viable family under equal data, compute and evaluation. If the product needs
an embedding more than samples, a self-supervised encoder may be the actual deliverable. Include
latency, memory, privacy and harmful-content filtering in the design. The right answer is an
accuracy-quality-cost frontier tied to operations, not a universal winner.
</InterviewQuestion>

## Practice

<Exercise n={1} title="A sign in the ELBO" difficulty="warm-up">
A VAE has expected reconstruction log likelihood $-40$ and KL $6$ nats. What are the ELBO and the
usual minimized negative-ELBO loss?

<div slot="solution">
The ELBO is reconstruction log likelihood minus KL: $-40-6=-46$. The minimized negative ELBO is
$46$. Adding KL to the log likelihood would violate the lower-bound identity; adding KL to the
negative reconstruction loss is the equivalent minimized form.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="A flow rescales density" difficulty="core">
Let $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $x=2z+3$. Express $\log p_X(x)$ in terms of the standard-normal
log density.

<div slot="solution">
The inverse is $z=(x-3)/2$ with derivative $dz/dx=1/2$. Therefore
$\log p_X(x)=\log p_Z((x-3)/2)+\log(1/2)$. The negative log-Jacobian term reflects spreading the
same probability mass over twice the length.
</div>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="The attractive but collapsed generator" difficulty="stretch">
Two image generators have similar FID. One produces diverse images with minor artifacts; the other
produces ten nearly perfect templates. What evaluation would reveal the difference?

<div slot="solution">
Add an explicit coverage or recall measure in the same feature space, class- and subgroup-frequency
checks, duplicate and nearest-neighbor analysis, and latent-seed diversity. Inspect precision and
recall separately rather than one combined distance. Use enough held-out real and generated samples
with uncertainty. Similar FID does not establish equal support coverage.
</div>
</Exercise>

## Summary

- Generative families differ in which operations—scoring, sampling, encoding and control—they make tractable.
- The VAE ELBO balances observation fit with compatibility between approximate posterior and prior.
- GANs optimize a game; sample fidelity and distribution coverage must be measured separately.
- Flows trade architectural freedom for invertibility and exact change-of-variable likelihood.
- Diffusion learns a reverse denoising process and pays iterative sampling cost.
- Self-supervised objectives define invariances through views, masks or teacher-student targets.
- No single likelihood, FID or sample grid is a complete evaluation.
